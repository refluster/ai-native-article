How a book
gets made

This is the process chart for dividing the work of one book among about 40 AI personas. We read the procedures publishers have built up from the planning meeting through to sign-off, and recast the ones that still hold in an AI organization as ten stages and ten gates: the page plan (台割), the separation of structural editing from copy-editing, fact review (校閲) independent of the writer, checking that corrections (赤字) were made, and a signed sign-off. The first title is the web book The Art of War (Sunzi).

How to read this. This document explains a process for dividing a book among about 40 AI personas, designed with reference to how publishers actually work. Take in the whole with the single process map (01) first, then read the two publishing traditions (02), what the stages and gates contain (03), who does what (04), the rules for publishing a classic (05), and the first run on The Art of War (06). The sources it rests on are in 07. The execution procedure itself is written in the session skill book-production; this document is the manual people use to check the process. How personas receive work is covered in the whitepaper.

01Process map

Process map

Read the process in two rows. The upper row decides what to write, in what order and at what length, and sets the skeleton. The lower row puts the prose, the facts and the rights in order before publication. Between one stage and the next there is always a gate, and nothing moves on until it passes.

A gate is passed or failed in one of two ways. One is the mechanical check: owen writes checks for what can be settled by counting — schema, length, quotation matches, sentence length, links, color tokens, whether corrections were applied — and farah runs them in CI. The other is judgment review: personas who hold the relevant role judge the value of the proposal, the choice of base text, the structure, the voice, whether a beginner can follow it, whether the claims survive refutation, the boundaries of rights, whether the figures are correct, and, last, the sign-off.

What a machine can decide is not left to human eyes, and what needs human judgment is not settled by a machine pass. That distinction is the frame of this process. A new check enters as a warning first, and is promoted to a gate when the same kind of finding comes up twice within 90 days (the same procedure as the organization-wide memory → lint ratchet).

02Two traditions

How publishers make books

We did not invent the process. We read how Japanese shinsho and bunko paperbacks are made side by side with how English-language trade and scholarly books are made, and combined them, taking in each case the side that shores up the places where an AI organization tends to break down.

A Japanese book starts with a planning meeting. The editor puts a working title, the readership, a draft table of contents, how it differs from comparable titles, and the trim size, page count and list price into a proposal; if it passes the meeting, the publisher signs the author. Next, the page plan fixes how pages are allocated and how the book unfolds. One editing guide goes as far as to say that “magazine editing could be said to mean making this page plan.” After reporting and writing, copy-editing, and book design and typesetting, the galleys come out: fact review examines whether facts and expressions hold up, and proofreading checks the galleys against the manuscript through first, second and third proofs. Proofreading ends with either sign-off (校了) or responsibility sign-off (責了), and the book moves on to cover design and color proofs, printing and binding (about one month from sign-off in Bungeishunjū’s example), and distribution.

In the English-speaking world, each stage of editing has its own name: acquisition, which chooses the project (for scholarly books, after two or more peer reviews and approval by a faculty Board); the developmental edit, which fixes structure and argument; the line edit, which improves the quality of the prose; the copyedit, which corrects grammar, style and cross-references; and fact-checking, typical of magazines. Chicago treats the copyedit as “the last opportunity for substantive changes,” and the developmental edit is always done first, because polishing the prose of a chapter that will later disappear is wasted effort. In proofreading after typesetting, author’s alterations (AA) are distinguished from editor’s alterations (EA) to separate cost and responsibility. Four to six months before publication, ARCs (advance reader copies) go out to the trade press.

Japan · shinsho and bunko

Independent fact review and a clear end point

  • The planning meeting is the entry point. The proposal sets out readers, a draft table of contents, comparable titles and specifications
  • The page plan fixes page allocation first. A powerful design tool
  • Fact review is a specialist profession with its own department, independent of the writer
  • Proofreading runs first → second → third proofs, checking that the previous round’s corrections went in
  • Sign-off / responsibility sign-off: a defined end point, with a clear owner of responsibility
  • Weakness: structural and stylistic fixes tend to get mixed together inside copy-editing
English-speaking · trade / academic

Editing stages have names and a fixed order

  • Acquisition, plus peer review and Board approval for scholarly books
  • In the order developmental → line → copyedit. Structure is fixed first
  • The copyedit is the last chance for substantive changes (Chicago)
  • Fact-checking is a separate department at magazines (New Yorker). In books it is often folded into the copyedit
  • Proofreading separates AA and EA, distinguishing cost from responsibility
  • ARCs go out 4–6 months ahead to gather early readers’ responses

Six things carried into the design

Independent review

Quality assurance belongs to someone other than the writer

We take the Japanese model. Fact review (S6), which checks facts, quotations and rights, is separated from the writers, and no chapter’s fact reviewer is the same persona as that chapter’s writer.

Structure → prose

Structural editing and copy-editing are separate gates

We take the English-language order. Structural editing (S4, G4) locks the skeleton before copy-editing (S5, G5) polishes the prose. Nothing enters S5 or later until the structure is locked.

Planning meeting

The entry point is an in-house planning meeting

G0 follows the same planning-meeting model as a shinsho. For Part I’s translation notes only, however, an independent reading equivalent to peer review (the source-text check in S6) is mandatory.

Sign-off

Put the end point and who is responsible in writing

The sign-off sheet names its signatories, and the scope of what may be fixed under responsibility sign-off (on whose responsibility, and what may be fixed) is written down. The cost and responsibility that the English-speaking world separates through the AA/EA distinction are carried here by rules on signatures and scope.

Early readers

The ARC becomes “early-release chapters plus readers’ responses”

For a web book, releasing a few chapters to a limited audience and gathering readers’ responses stands in for ARCs and review copies. The responses gathered are kept as an S7 deliverable.

Page plan

Divide by characters and reading time, not pages

The page plan is a powerful tool peculiar to Japanese publishing. The web has no pages, so we use it as a per-chapter budget of characters and of reading time (estimated at 600 characters per minute, as the Reader does).

Restructure loop R. It starts at G4 if any one of the following applies. ① A beginner’s read-through marks places where “it is unclear why I should go on to the next chapter” in two or more chapters. ② Explanations of the same source paragraph are duplicated across three or more chapters. ③ A Part II chapter is no more than a rewording of Part I (nadia judges that it has “no so what”). ④ Three or more chapters deviate from the page plan by ±25% or more in actual length. ⑤ A central claim is found REFUTED in refutation review. The procedure: ingrid writes down which chapters to keep, which to merge and which to send back, with reasons; camille raises the page plan to v(n+1) and redraws the schedule; the G2 mechanical checks are passed again; and only the affected chapters go back to S3. The limit is two rounds. If a third is needed, the problem is treated as one of planning (G0) rather than structure, and escalated to maya and the operator.
Sign-off and responsibility sign-offThe sign-off sheet is signed by four people — ingrid (the whole book), astrid (sources and disclosure), idris (rights) and owen (every mechanical gate passed) — and G9 is reached once all four signatures are in. A single-character typo found after sign-off that does not change the meaning may be fixed and published on ingrid’s responsibility (responsibility sign-off). It is recorded in the errata, and kai checks it afterwards. A fix that changes the meaning is announced in the errata, the version is raised, and fact review (S6) is partly redone. Writing down the name of the person who checks prevents the typical publisher’s failure in which nobody verifies what was fixed under responsibility sign-off.

05Classics

Rules for publishing a classic

“Faithful to the original” cannot be checked unless it is pinned down in advance. Taking as our model the structure of the Iwanami Bunko edition Shintei Sonshi (translated and annotated by Kanaya Osamu) — source text, kundoku transcription, modern translation, notes and index — we defined operationally what has to be kept for a text to count as “faithful.”

Base text

Name a single base text

The Sunzi comes down in several lineages: the Wei Wu (Cao Cao) annotated text, the Seven Military Classics text, the Eleven Commentators text and the Yinqueshan bamboo-slip text (excavated in 1972). The recommendation is to use the received text of the Eleven Commentators lineage as the main text and to show the main variants from the bamboo-slip text in the notes. The final decision is made at G1. A widely known variant — that the bamboo-slip text reverses the word order of the attack-and-defense lines in the Xing (Dispositions) chapter — has not been confirmed in a primary source, so sora will verify it while compiling the variant table.

Source text

Transcribe the source text exactly as the base text has it, without alteration

How older character forms and variant characters are handled is written into the style sheet first. A source-text block in the body that does not match the segmented source text exactly after normalization fails the quote lint at G3. The commentary and Part II refer to source paragraph IDs, and never present as the source’s claim something the source does not say.

Kundoku

We make our own kundoku

Following the conventional readings (the customary return marks and okurigana) is fine. Where we adopt a reading of our own, a note gives the grounds. Someone else’s kundoku transcription may be protected if it contains original choices, so we stay on the safe side and do not copy it.

Modern translation

Translate in a clean room

While drafting, the translator does not look at anyone else’s modern translation. Only the source text, dictionaries and the glosses in commentaries may be consulted, and what was consulted is recorded in the work log. idris audits the logs by sampling. Words supplied by the translator are shown in 〔 〕, and the latitude for free translation is set by the rules.

Maxims

A maxim box is source text + kundoku + citation

A maxim box always sets out the source text, the kundoku, the translation, the chapter name and the paragraph ID. Where the form in circulation differs from the source, it is recorded separately as “the commonly quoted form” and marked as an error. For example, the Mougong (Attack by Stratagem) chapter’s “you will not be imperiled in a hundred battles” is often reworded as “a hundred victories in a hundred battles.”

Rights

The source text is in the public domain; other translations are derivative works

The source text was written more than two thousand years ago, far beyond the term of protection (in principle, 70 years after the author’s death). Published modern translations, on the other hand, are protected as translations — derivative works — separately from the original, so both borrowing from them and working from them as a base are prohibited. Only when discussing a comparison with other translations do we quote the minimum that meets the four requirements for quotation (already published, consistent with fair practice, within a justified scope, source indicated).

06First run

The first run: The Art of War

On the calendar, the process is planned at about nine weeks. For the first title, it was compressed into a single operator session, with a sub-agent started for each work package (WP) and run in parallel. Only waiting time is compressed: no gate is skipped and nothing is reordered.

On how it was run. The first title is role-play chaired by the operator’s session. Each WP’s sub-agent writes, reads and judges from the standpoint of the responsible persona, but the personas did not start on their own from their bindings. The signatures on the sign-off sheet and the engagement records state this explicitly as “operator-orchestrated.” The mechanical gates (check-book and the prebuild hook, and generation of the book of maxims) enter the repo in the same PR as the Reader; until that lands on main, what this document describes is a design.

The book has two parts. Part I gives each Sunzi chapter a chapter of its own, in this order: summary; source text, kundoku, translation and word notes for each paragraph; commentary; a maxim box; and one figure. Part II is made up of chapters that read the ideas of the Sunzi chapters against modern projects and organizations, from measuring the odds of victory before starting (Ji, “Laying Plans”) to the ethics of deception (Ji, and Huogong, “Attack by Fire”).

  1. Research pack (WP-10–16) — fixing the base text, segmenting the source text with paragraph IDs, the variant table, kundoku v1, candidate maxims, the list of earlier books, the glossary, the rights memo and the disclosure statement. Everything else depends on it, so it is finished first. → G1
  2. Page plan and chapter design briefs (WP-20) — per-chapter character budgets, corresponding source IDs, figure slots, owners. → G2
  3. Writing in parallel lanes (WP-30a/b/c, WP-31) — Part I in three lanes, Ji–Xing / Shi–Jiubian / Xingjun–Yongjian, in chapter order within each lane. Each Part II chapter starts as soon as the first drafts of the chapters it draws on are done. → G3
  4. Figures (WP-70) — an SVG for each chapter and one for the whole, plus the cover. Drawn in parallel with the writing, from the figure slots in the chapter design briefs.
  5. Review and lint (WP-40, 50, 60; the checks brought forward and implemented in WP-51) — structural editing → copy-editing → fact review. Every manuscript is read through at least three lenses (structure, readability, facts and refutation). → G4–G6
  6. Reader build and proofreading (WP-71, 80) — build into a reading screen with switching between three layers, a resume position and the time left in the chapter, and run the first, second and third proofs through the corrections ledger. → G7, G8
  7. PR — the Reader, the mechanical gates and the text go into one PR (the process page and the skill in a separate PR), and once review turns green by consensus, the workforce’s pr-autopilot merges it.
  8. Deploy and sign-off (WP-90) — publication, live smoke, signatures on the sign-off sheet. From then on, the errata are maintained. → G9
