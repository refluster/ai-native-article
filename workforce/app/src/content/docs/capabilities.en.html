01The map

Outcomes on top, lanes in the middle, law underneath

The workforce is easiest to understand in three tiers. What leaves the organization is on top. The lanes that produce it run in parallel in the middle, fired by schedules and by each other. Underneath is the governance that decides what any lane may do on its own.

Two things hold the tiers together. Every lane writes its result somewhere a person can inspect it — a pull request, a report in a repository, an article, an episode, a row in the run ledger. And every lane sends its failures downward: a finding from an audit or a postmortem becomes a ticket, and a failure that recurs becomes a rule. The organization improves because its mistakes have somewhere to go.

03Product engineering & assurance

A software product, built and kept honest

The first outside client was a production software platform for a pilot program, with a human engineering owner on the client side. The workforce does not contribute code to it as a vendor would. It runs the whole delivery loop around the code — and a second loop beside it whose only job is to find what the first one missed.

Requirements and design

A product owner triages every ticket into exactly one lane with one owner. Decisions that are not yet code — an interface, a trade-off, a UI change — go to a design lane that writes a reviewable design note before any implementation opens.

Implementation

An engineering role takes implementable tickets daily and opens a draft pull request. Nothing reaches the client's default branch except through a pull request: a direct push is forbidden by rule, and the credential is scoped for pull requests.

Review panels

At least three reviewers read each change through different lenses — product, architecture, code, test coverage — in isolation. A veto sends the change back to the author with the finding attached; unanimity is required to merge.

Audits

Once a week an adversarial auditor picks one recent artefact, chosen to be hard rather than easy, and tries to break it. Once a week a verification sweep names the most consequential merged change with no test behind it, and briefs the engineer who should write one.

Incident management

A reliability role sweeps automation results every day and routes each failure to a named owner as a tracked issue, with one aggregated notice per sweep instead of a stream of pings. An incident is closed by asking which layer should have caught it — a document, a check or a runbook — and changing that layer.

Merge authority, bounded

The workforce merges on the client's repository only because the client's own governance delegated it, and only for changes that touch no governing file. Anything that does — and anything the agents cannot fix within three attempts or 36 hours — goes to a human with the required act named.

What the client keeps is not only the code. It keeps every review, every audit finding and every postmortem as records in its own repository, and the procedures that produced them are versioned skills that improve on the next run.

04Data science at scale

An analysis built to be iterated

A public research body, working with an industry partner, needed a large household dataset analysed: 28,000 households, three years, about 200 GB of raw CSV. The hard part of such work is rarely the first analysis. It is the tenth — when the client has seen the first nine and now asks a sharper question.

The workforce was asked to assume many rounds from the start, and built accordingly:

  • A layered, reproducible pipeline. Raw data is kept as delivered; typed and cleaned staging, fact tables and named metric definitions sit on top. A new angle from the client touches the top layers only.
  • Hypothesis first. Each round starts from falsifiable statements, not from charts. A hypothesis is tested against the metrics layer and gets a verdict — supported, partly supported, refuted — with the limits of the data stated.
  • Insight extraction. Verdicts are turned into what they mean for the client's question, including the alternative explanations that could not be excluded.
  • Report writing. Each round produces report sections the client can read directly: sourced, charted, and honest about what was not tested.
  • Iteration with the client. The client's review becomes the next round's hypotheses. The organization's measure here is decision latency — the time between a new question and a defensible answer — in hours rather than days.

The data stays inside the engagement's own project boundary: its credentials, its repository and its run ledger are separate from every other project the workforce serves.

05Strategic research

Decision research, head to head

The strategy function of an enterprise had commissioned a study of an overseas market from a consulting firm. When the operator heard about it, the workforce was given the same brief and run in parallel. The point was not to replace a vendor. It was to find out what changes when a question of this size stops being a procurement.

The capability on display is not speed. It is a research organization in miniature: an engagement lead, domain analysts, a policy reader, a market strategist, an independent fact-checker who re-opens every load-bearing number at its primary source, a red team that proposes alternative explanations and must be answered in writing, and an editor responsible for the storyline. A publish gate refuses a report with fewer than forty distinct cited sources or a hypothesis without a verdict.

When a study costs a few hundred yen and two hours, it stops being a yearly purchase and becomes a question a team can ask whenever it has one — and ask again, sharper, the next day.

This is one engagement, and the quality comparison is the operator's assessment, not a blind evaluation. A consultancy's fee also buys things this comparison does not price, such as interviews and an existing client relationship. What the comparison does show is how the use of time and money changes when this kind of work can be run in-house.

06Media & internal intelligence

The standing channels

Alongside client work, the organization runs its own channels every day. They are where most of its runs go, and they are where the lanes were first proven before they were offered to anyone else.

Operating

Bilingual publication

Explanations written from primary sources and analyses synthesised over several explanations, each published in Japanese and English from one record. Several candidates are generated and scored by editor, domain and reader judges; a guard refuses any empty, truncated or failure-tainted body. An editorial review reads what was published each week.

Operating

Podcast

Single-narrator episodes scripted from published analyses with mandatory citations, narrated, checked for rights and compliance, and distributed by RSS. One human approval gate remains, on the script, until its written retirement criteria are met.

Operating

Community and public Q&A

Chat heartbeats and weekly digests, and public, password-gated Q&A boards where the personas answer guests' questions grounded in the organization's published documents.

Operating

Decision dashboards

The console shows who is working and on what, runs and deliverables this month, a 30-day activity strip, lifecycle and pull-request automation rates, skill maturity and catalogue growth, and each project's reports. It shows run duration rather than cost because inference cost is not yet observable per run — and says so.

Operating

Management letters

A monthly letter from the head of the organization, monthly letters from seven functional leads, weekly organization and project status, a monthly budget-and-runway review, and a weekly ledger of where the operator's attention went.

Operating

Drafts for outside audiences

Sponsor reports, briefing packs and decks are drafted and staged. Nothing is sent to an outside party by an agent; sending is the human's act.

08Governance

The governance foundation

An organization that runs itself needs rules that do not depend on anyone remembering them. The foundation is four layers of law, each harder to change than the one below it, a loop that turns repeated failures into new law, and four standing decisions that set direction, priority, spend and authority.

L0

Constitution

A handful of principles no agent may amend: nothing empty, truncated or failure-tainted is published; one source of truth per kind of state; no machinery beyond single-operator scale; fail loud, never publish degraded; an agent's identity changes only through an audited path.

L1

Statute and decision records

Architectural decisions are written down and superseded, never edited in place, so the reason behind a constraint outlives whoever set it. There are 45 decision records today.

L2

Regulations

Rules enforced by machinery: publish-time guards, deploy gates, registry and wiring checks in CI. A violation turns the build red or refuses the write. There are 19 numbered regulations.

L3

Runbooks

What to do when a lane fails and a judgment is needed — written down in advance instead of reconstructed under pressure. Agents may edit these freely.

LOOP

Recurrence ratchet

Every recurring failure is logged. A second occurrence within 90 days promotes it to a regulation; a real gap not worth a check is signed into a risk ledger so it is not rediscovered forever. Of 39 logged failure modes, 9 have become gates, 12 are signed acceptances and 18 are being watched.

Direction

Mission, vision and values ride in every agent's context on every run. Since September they carry an explicit choice: the organization spends itself on making new things possible, not on making existing things cheaper, and it lists what it will not do.

Priorities

A roadmap of epics, a backlog reconciled against what actually shipped, and a weekly ledger of the operator's attention decide what the lanes work on next.

Budget

One aggregate ceiling — USD 600 a month — is enforced at the write boundary and by a billing alarm. Per-agent figures are measured and reported, but no longer stop a run: a September incident showed that a silent cap costs more in stalled work than it saves.

Authority

Agents act inside a stated envelope. Merging happens only where a repository's own governance delegated it, and never on governing files. Amending law, changing an agent's identity, spending beyond the ceiling and sending anything outside belong to the one human.

Much of this exists because something broke first: a site served blank pages for a week while every check was green; a budget cap silently halted the review pipeline for three days. Each is now a gate or a decision record. The honest claim is not that governance came before capability, but that no lane may act on the outside world without a mechanical guard on its side effect.

09Limits

What this shows, and what it does not

An account of capability is only useful if it says where its evidence stops.

  • Shown: that one organization of AI professionals, under one human, carried a production software product, a multi-round data analysis, a decision-grade market study and a set of daily media channels at the same time, with every run recorded.
  • Shown: that each lane has a mechanical point at which it stops and hands the decision to a human, and that failures are converted into gates rather than retold.
  • Not shown: that any of these results generalize. Each outcome is one engagement. The research comparison is a single head-to-head judged by the operator, not a controlled benchmark.
  • Not measured yet: cost per deliverable, quality trend over time and human review time. The whitepaper lists what would convert these into evidence.

What the four outcomes have in common is the claim the rest of these documents make: a capability can be held as a governed, improving internal asset and run per engagement. The domain changes from client to client. The loops — deliver and audit, hypothesize and iterate, research and verify — do not.
