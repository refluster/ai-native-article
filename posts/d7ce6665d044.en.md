## Executive Summary

In an April 15, 2020 essay, Andreessen Horowitz's Seema Amble and Angela Strange argue that the CFO, the person most critical to helping the CEO make high-stakes calls in a crisis, has more data than ever but tools that have not kept pace. The ERP only offers a backward-facing view, typically two or more weeks after month-end; products stand alone; and manual entry and reconciliation never end. They frame this as a moment to build, and sort the opportunity into four areas.

## What the crisis exposed: a role outgrowing its tools

**Evidence (from the source)**

- In the current economic crisis, businesses have been forced to make difficult decisions about resource allocation and cash flow planning.
- The CFO is the person most critical in helping the CEO make those calls, and the role has become highly strategic over the years.
- Yet despite an influx of data to base decisions on, the tools at the CFO's disposal have not kept pace.
- Today's CFO is expected to be "part data analyst and part systems architect," stitching together piecemeal data from rudimentary software products.
The authors pose four concrete questions that new tools should answer:

- Headcount planning: headcount was planned to grow 20 to 50 percent across several divisions in January; revenue will now miss targets, so a reduction must be considered. How many people were hired under the original plan, at what cost, and where should staff be reduced under various revenue scenarios?
- Cash flow: when will the company run out of cash in scenarios A, B, and C, and when does it next need to raise debt or equity?
- Resource allocation: what is the fully loaded cost of product A versus product B, and how would shifting resources toward one affect revenue?
- Sales: if the pipeline for the next three months is light but the financial model does not reflect Salesforce changes in real time, how does finance know to adjust the forecast?
**Conclusion (the implication we draw)**

Every one of these questions demands multiple scenarios, run on current data, answered immediately. Tools built for bookkeeping and reporting cannot answer them by design. The case the authors make for building now rests less on a surge in demand than on a mismatch between what the tools were designed for and what the job actually requires.

## Three problems with today's finance software

Finance teams have traditionally spent the bulk of their time on the base of the pyramid, the transactional and reporting tasks. As a result, most CFO tools built in recent years focused on transacting and record-keeping. The authors group the problems into three.

**1. The software stands alone**

- None of these products integrate with each other, and no data flows from one to another.
- They also don't connect to key sales, HR, and marketing systems such as Salesforce, Workday, or Marketo.
- To update the forecast for a newly hired employee, for example, someone on the finance team typically needs to send a series of emails across finance and HR.
- Each product is built solely for the finance team, usually for a single person to own and operate. Although finance works closely with other departments to develop plans, the tools do not let multiple users across teams contribute and collaborate.
**2. Data is stale, limited, and hard to access**

- The ERP, the central repository for financial data, is designed around accounting, so it gives only a backward-facing view, typically two or more weeks after month-end.
- With that lag, the CFO often has difficulty assessing cash burn, revenue, or expenses in real time. In the authors' words, "particularly now, most companies can't afford to wait six weeks to figure out when they are going to run out of cash."
- These building-block tools offer little predictive forecasting or benchmarking against competing companies.
- Retrieving, cleaning, and formatting the data often requires SQL experience, which the finance team may or may not have.
**3. Manual entry and reconciliation never end**

- The CFO role is plagued by manual, repetitive tasks, from initiating bank transfers to recording checks into the ledger.
- Ahead of month-end close, cash movement (wires, credit cards, bank account balances, etc.) must be matched against total product sold and invoices paid, and then painstakingly pulled into reports and investor presentations.
- Closing software such as FloQast exists to create checklists and flag irregularities, but data entry and categorizing is largely still done by hand.
- None of these products has a particularly intuitive interface, and training users can take months. That is why so many finance teams stick with a familiar workhorse: Excel.
**Conclusion**

The three problems are not independent. Isolation (1) degrades data freshness (2), and people patch stale data by hand, so reconciliation never shrinks (3). Retreating to Excel is a rational adaptation by finance teams to all three at once.

## The four areas of opportunity

The authors say we have rightly come to expect software that enables predictive analytics, integrated communication, cross-company collaboration, and automation, and they list four areas.

**1. Intelligent Building Blocks**

- Rebuild point solutions such as expense management so they also offer predictive analytics and forecasting.
- Example: expense software that tells you who usually paid on time and who didn't, sends reminders, and sets payment terms based on past performance.
- Brex, Divvy, Airbase, Ramp, and others have already replaced clunky legacy systems with products that are intuitive, easily integrated, and give greater control. Many issue physical and virtual cards that provide an accurate record of all transactions, saving the back-office hours otherwise spent correcting human error.
- A bigger challenge is to design a new general ledger: one that pulls in operational data and tracks customer lifetime value in real time, rather than just accounting statements.
**2. Integrated Data Layer**

- Connective tissue: software that sits on top of existing tools, extracts data, and provides intelligence for dynamic planning.
- It could also benchmark a company's metrics, from compensation to cash burn to days payable, against peers.
- Example: a workforce planning tool that assesses payroll, cap table management, and budget to enable better headcount decisions, automatically mapping headcount to revenue growth and product-launch planning.
- Another example: a collaborative cash flow management tool that sits on top of procurement and expense management and tracks spending.
**3. Banking Operations**

- Software that connects to bank accounts would let companies understand their cash position, debt, and money movements in real time.
- It could send and receive payments automatically, which beats manually filling out a form to initiate a wire transfer.
- It could assist with treasury management and reconcile payments.
**4. Automating Data Entry and Reporting**

- Receipts and checks will not disappear overnight, but optical character recognition and machine learning can eliminate manual entry of invoice information, receipt collection, and matching against checks received on the bank statement.
- Reports and board updates can be pre-populated by connecting to the right data sources.
## Why now: fintech changed the revenue model

The authors stress that companies in this space can now monetize through fintech, via transaction revenue, lending, or even insurance, in addition to SaaS contracts. They cite several drivers:

- Companies are eager to transact online; it is viewed as more efficient and trustworthy than cutting paper checks to pay invoices or payroll.
- A fintech infrastructure now exists that lets companies take payments and connect with banking partners. Expense management companies, for example, can easily spin up virtual cards and collect an interchange fee on all expenses filed.
- Accounts payable (AP) and accounts receivable (AR) software companies can collect transaction fees when customers use their platforms to facilitate payments.
- Software companies are providing better data to underwrite risk for financial services. Cash flow lending and invoice factoring, for example, are possible based on AP and AR data. Because these tools are tied to volume, the market grows, and the product becomes stickier, as customers process more business.
**Conclusion**

On SaaS subscriptions alone, a finance tool stays a cost-saving tool for one department. Add transaction revenue, lending, and insurance, and it becomes a business that grows with its customers' volume. That shift in revenue structure is the core support for the authors' claim that now is the time to build.

## Takeaways

- The essay is dated April 15, 2020 and is written against that economic crisis. Its four areas (building blocks, an integrated data layer, banking operations, and automated entry and reporting) read as a proposal to move finance from a place of record-keeping to a place of decision-making.
- Sales, marketing, and engineering have all substantially upgraded their tools over the last 5 to 10 years, the authors say, and it is time to shift the focus to finance.
- The essay names companies and products but gives no market-size or impact figures. The opportunities it describes should therefore be read as an investor's hypothesis, not a verified result.
