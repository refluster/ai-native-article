## Executive Summary

On August 27, 2026, Anthropic opened a research preview of the Model Hardware Standard (MHS) — a shared specification that lets AI agents safely operate physical lab and manufacturing equipment such as microscopes, liquid handlers, and robotic arms — to a first group of scientific research labs and manufacturers. At QuEra Computing, a quantum-computing company, laser relock recovery improved from a human baseline of 150 seconds and 58% success to 6–14 seconds and 99.3% success after Claude autonomously rewrote the recovery script overnight. At Carnegie Mellon University, hardware integration time dropped from weeks to eight hours, and at Genentech, Claude autonomously optimized liquid-handling parameters. MHS's core idea is a standardized "driver" that lets an AI agent discover, understand, and operate hardware regardless of its native, incompatible interface.

## What MHS Is: One Specification Over a Menagerie of Incompatible Machines

Integrating lab or manufacturing hardware normally takes "weeks, if not months," Anthropic says, because most devices have their own bespoke programming interface with no standard way to connect. MHS addresses this with a standardized driver built on simple primitives — commands like "read" (e.g., "get temperature") and "write" (e.g., "set temperature") — that any hardware device can understand. The driver is populated from natural-language tags (written by a user directly, or by an agent that interviews the user about the setup), from which it auto-generates a reference file describing what a device can measure, what can be adjusted, and its safety limits. An agent then controls the hardware through one of three mechanisms — MCP, a command-line interface, or code files (APIs) — letting it orchestrate multiple instruments from a single line of code. In testing, Anthropic observed Claude adjust a laser, watch the result through a camera, and repeat the process, then package what it learned into a deterministic script it could run as a single command.

## QuEra Computing: Laser Recovery Sped Up Nearly a Hundredfold

The most quantitatively documented case is QuEra's. QuEra's neutral-atom quantum computer requires its lasers to hold frequency to roughly one part in a trillion; when vibration or pressure shifts knock a laser out of "lock," a human operator previously needed 5 to 10 minutes to recover it. QuEra had already spent several months building a bespoke recovery script with a team of a laser-systems engineer, a software engineer, an algorithms specialist, and a tester — but that script only succeeded 58% of the time, in about 150 seconds per attempt.

QuEra then handed the same problem to Claude via MHS. Four separate Claude instances ran as distinct roles — one proposing a hypothesis for improving recovery, one writing that change into the script, one running the updated script against the live laser and logging every step, and one reading the logbook to decide what to try next — cycling hundreds of times, unattended, overnight. By morning, recovery took about 6 seconds with a 96% success rate; in a later 700-trial blind test with no agent involved, the finished script succeeded 695 times (99.3%). The hardest disturbances, where frequency had drifted far from target, took 10–14 seconds to recover; simpler ones took 0.9–5.4 seconds. Rather than the original linear, one-path-for-every-disturbance script, Claude converged on a decision tree that reads each instrument and builds if-then conditions from what it sees.

QuEra also pointed Claude at the laser's 12 interdependent PID tuning parameters. The specialist's standing tune measured 15.7 mV of residual error; over 363 experiments and 16 unattended hours, Claude brought it down to 1.55 mV. When the specialist independently retuned the same laser from scratch and both parameter sets were checked on a phase noise analyzer, the two matched across most of the band — except around a roughly 220 kHz resonance, where the manual tune left about a thousand times more noise than Claude's. Over a 19-hour run, Claude's PIDs never lost lock, while the expert-tuned PIDs unlocked about 1.6 times per hour.

## Genentech and Carnegie Mellon: Lab Integration From Weeks to Hours

Genentech piloted MHS on the BCA protein assay, which requires coordinating a liquid handler, robotic arm, and microplate reader. Claude autonomously converged on a flow rate of about 140 µL/s for water (RMSE 0.016) and 10 µL/s for the more viscous BSA protein solution (RMSE 0.181) — parameters automation experts confirmed were reasonable. But when bubbles formed during liquid handling, Claude's initial instinct was to simply retry the same operation with different parameters, which only agitated the fluid further; it took explicit guidance about the physical cause of the failure before it adopted an effective fix — a clear instance of the limits of Claude's physical-world reasoning.

At Carnegie Mellon, researchers used MHS to unify a liquid handler, plate reader, robotic arm, and monitoring cameras spread across three computers with fundamentally incompatible interfaces, letting a Claude Opus 4.8 agent run serial-dilution dose-response experiments autonomously. Building the drivers and orchestration layer took about eight hours, versus the multiple weeks a vendor-built setup typically requires. In a safety test, six artificially induced fault conditions (missing plate, rotated plate, busy reader, disconnected camera, unreachable device, active emergency stop) were all correctly blocked before any device moved. In the actual experiment, the first run (maximum concentration 200 µg/mL) was rejected by the model itself for a poor fit (R² < 0.9, caused by saturation); it independently reran with a compressed 100 µg/mL range and reached a strong fit (R² > 0.98) with no human input.

## Adoption and the Limits Anthropic Itself Names

Hardware and software vendors adding MHS support include AWS (via its Strands Robots library), Automata, Danaher, Doosan Robotics, MBF Bioscience, QIAGEN, Tecan, Universal Robots, Hugging Face (LeRobot), and Raspberry Pi. At HHMI Janelia, a researcher who previously had to launch seven separate vendor programs in a fixed order just to start a zebrafish-imaging experiment reported that MHS reduced that startup process to a single click on a shared dashboard.

Anthropic is explicit about the limits. Because Claude learns about the physical world through text and images, its spatial and physical reasoning remains limited and still requires expert oversight — the Genentech bubble-troubleshooting episode above is one instance of this. In QuEra's pilot, Claude sometimes could not diagnose a physical hardware fault, and it paused experiments overnight to wait for human approval before taking any action it judged even slightly risky. MHS also does not yet support hardware lacking a programmable interface, which Anthropic says it is working with manufacturers to address. The company plans to build additional safety evaluations during the research preview before open-sourcing the standard.
