## Executive Summary

AI is supposed to make work lighter, so why does a day spent working alongside it so often end in exhaustion? Product-development specialist Shin (@shin_sasaki19, posting on X on August 27, 2026) argues the answer is structural: AI has automated "tasks," not "judgment." What he calls "AI fatigue" is five distinct burdens — supervision, decision-making, verification, incompletion, and updating — that concentrate on the human side of the exchange. His proposed fix is not less AI, but a deliberate redesign of how many judgment calls a person has to make.

## The core claim: AI made everyone a manager

Shin opens from a before-and-after contrast. "It used to take two hours to draft a single proposal. Now AI produces three drafts in a few minutes" — genuinely faster. But the speed shifts the burden onto the human: "which one to adopt, whether the facts are right, whether the wording feels off, whether to have it redone again" — a cluster of judgments returned all at once.

He compares this to the fatigue of a manager: "A manager isn't tired because they aren't doing the hands-on work themselves. They're tired because they have to understand the context of multiple jobs, judge quality, and keep deciding what comes next." AI never tires and returns with the next deliverable within thirty seconds — "only the human never gets to step down from the supervisor's seat." This is the first of his five fatigue types: supervision fatigue.

## Five sources of fatigue

Shin breaks the fatigue down into five components, each grounded in his own description:

- **Supervision fatigue** — everyone using AI is placed in the position of continually evaluating its output.
- **Decision fatigue** — "Which model to use. Whether to let it think deeply or answer fast. Whether to allow it to search. Whether to accept the output. Whether to ask for another revision" — with more options, "the judgment doesn't end even after you've decided."
- **Verification fatigue** — "The frustrating case is the answer that's ninety percent right and ten percent suspicious." He likens reading AI-written text to "less like proofreading and more like auditing logic someone else wrote."
- **Incompletion fatigue** — because AI lowers the barrier to starting anything, "the screen fills with half-generated tabs, text awaiting review, and research still to be integrated," and because "AI's output has no natural stopping point," work stays open unless the person sets their own end condition.
- **Update fatigue** — as new models and features keep arriving, a low-level anxiety persists even off the clock: "should I try that tool," "am I the only one falling behind."
Shin sums up: "AI fatigue isn't simply information overload. It's the fatigue that arises when five burdens — supervision, decision-making, verification, incompletion, and updating — concentrate on the human side." He adds a warning that better models won't necessarily fix this: the fatigue "may not resolve naturally as AI performance improves, and could in fact intensify as processing speed increases."

## The fix isn't stepping away from AI — it's designing fewer decisions

The second half of the post lays out five concrete countermeasures.

1. **Fix a "standard" AI** — stop hunting for the optimal model every time; pick one default tool and reserve switching for tasks where the difference is clear. Compare new models only "once a week or once a month," not every time news breaks. 2. **Vary how deeply you check work by its risk** — "for internal brainstorming, just watch for major errors. For customer-facing text, check names and numbers. For decisions tied to management or contracts, trace it back to primary sources yourself." 3. **Decide "done" before you write the prompt** — settle in one line what counts as finished before asking AI for anything — e.g., "it's just a draft for internal discussion, so seventy percent is fine." As he puts it: "raising a ten-point piece of work to twelve points is not the same value as raising a seventy-point piece to eighty." 4. **Don't run too many jobs in parallel** — "keep the combined total of tasks out for AI and tasks awaiting your review to around two at a time." As he notes, "the bottleneck just moved from generation to judgment." 5. **Form your own hypothesis before opening AI** — write down your own take first, and "AI stops being a stand-in for your thinking and becomes something that hunts for counterarguments and gaps" instead.

## The takeaway: measure judgment calls, not hours saved

The core of Shin's argument is a challenge to how AI's benefit gets measured in the first place. "We tend to measure AI's effect by how many hours it saved. But what actually matters is how many times that work demanded a judgment call." A single metric of time saved doesn't capture the cognitive load of supervising, deciding, and verifying.

He closes: "Mastering AI means deciding where not to use it. Fix a standard, vary how carefully you check, and close things out once they're done. The people who can draw that line are the ones who can keep using AI for the long run." The post was published at 2:42 PM on August 27, 2026, and had logged 1.6K views at the time of writing.
