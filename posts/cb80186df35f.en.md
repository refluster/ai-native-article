Anthropic's own economic scenario model estimates that the faster AI adoption accelerates, the more the resulting growth flows to capital rather than to workers' wages. A separate piece from the same company, on how it builds commerce agents, argues that safety should be delegated not to careful prompt wording but to verification built into the harness surrounding the model. And Meta's secret internal initiative, "Project OT," collapsed under a surge of trust and reliability incidents. These three facts look unrelated, but they resolve into a single principle: where the function of verification is placed determines whether AI's economic surplus is captured by capital or pushed back by the human organization it was meant to replace.

## The Tilt Toward Capital — What Anthropic's Scenario Numbers Actually Say

"Anthropic's Economic Scenario Model and the 2030 Capital Share of an AI-Run Half of Knowledge Work" modeled US GDP in 2030 ranging from +1.6% to +32.4%, depending on how capable and how widely adopted AI becomes. The overlooked detail isn't the width of that range — it's the direction of the tilt within it: the faster the adoption scenario, the worse it is for labor. In the most aggressive scenario, knowledge-worker wages fall by more than 10%, and unemployment exceeds levels typically seen in a recession. In other words, AI's economic effect is not primarily a question of how big the pie gets; it is a question of who governs how the pie is cut. The paper itself does not explain *why* faster adoption tilts toward capital. That causal gap is filled by a second piece from the same source.

## The "Single Agent Loop" — Safety Engineered Into the Harness, Not the Prompt

"The Anatomy of Effective Commerce Agent Design: Three Lessons Anthropic Drew From Building Them" distills a year of building commerce agents for retail, travel, and telecom with Claude. Its headline lesson is architectural: an effective commerce agent is not a swarm of domain-specific subagents, nor an intent router, but a single agent loop equipped with skills and tools. More consequential still is a second finding: latency and cost are solved through prompt caching (a 90-99% hit rate), while safety is guaranteed not by clever prompting but by harness-side mechanisms — server-issued ID verification and staged approval flows. Put plainly, Anthropic's own implementation knowledge converges on one point: trust is not something you hope a model's judgment will produce; it is something you engineer as permanent infrastructure sitting outside the model. That is a design-level answer to what the economic model could only gesture at numerically — capital captures the gains of speed only where it can build and own the verification infrastructure that makes speed survivable.

## Project OT's Collapse — The Cost of Substituting Labor Without Verification

That answer is proven from the opposite direction by the collapse described in "Why Meta's 'Project OT' Fell Apart — Inside an Ambitious Plan for Mass AI Substitution of Staff." A secret initiative led by CEO Mark Zuckerberg, Project OT aimed to have AI take over the work of thousands of employees, shrinking many internal teams by as much as 60%. But a leak triggered internal backlash, AI productivity fell short of expectations, and — critically — "security and reliability incidents surged." The plan effectively collapsed. Employee satisfaction dropped from 74% to 55%, and Zuckerberg himself admitted at a July internal meeting that the reform "wasn't working." What matters is the anatomy of the failure: it was not simply that "AI wasn't as capable as hoped." A surge in trust and reliability incidents was named explicitly as a breaking factor. That is exactly the consequence the commerce-agent lesson warned about — scaling substitution without engineering safety into the harness. Meta, despite having abundant capital and technical talent, tried to raise the speed of substitution without first investing in verification as infrastructure, and that speed broke the organization itself.

## The Unifying Principle: Where Verification Sits Decides Who Captures the Surplus

Laid side by side, these three facts reveal a causal chain none of them shows alone. The economic model shows a correlation — faster adoption tilts toward capital — without explaining its mechanism. The commerce-agent design knowledge supplies a proposition Anthropic itself arrived at through implementation: trust and safety are not products of a model's judgment but infrastructure you build into the harness. And Meta's collapse functions as a falsification experiment for organizations that ignore that proposition: raising the speed of substitution without investing in verification produced a surge of trust incidents, drove employees away, and forced the plan itself into retreat.

Taken together, these facts suggest that the distribution point for AI's economic surplus is decided not by model capability itself, but by *where, and by whom, the function of verification is owned*. An organization that leaves verification sitting in the unstable location of prompt wording or model judgment pays for it, the moment it scales, in the form of incidents — and is ultimately forced to retreat from the substitution bet altogether. An organization that can own verification as permanent infrastructure outside the model — server-issued ID checks, staged approval flows — can run at speed without breaking, and keep capturing the surplus that speed produces, as capital. The tilt Anthropic's economic model shows numerically — faster scenarios being worse for labor — is, in this light, a projection of a more fundamental governance structure: which side, the side that owns verification infrastructure or the side that doesn't, retains the surplus AI produces. Speed itself does not favor capital. The asymmetry that only capital can afford to build the verification infrastructure that makes speed survivable — that is what favors capital.

## What Comes Next, and What It Means for You

If this principle holds, large-scale AI substitution attempts over the next twelve to eighteen months should bifurcate. On one side, organizations that invest ahead of time in a "harness layer" — server-side permission verification, staged approval flows — before pushing substitution forward, and that lock in productivity gains stably as capital income. On the other, organizations that treat a model's rising capability, or the impact of an announcement, as sufficient justification for substitution, the way Meta's Project OT initially did — these are likely to hit the same wall: a surge of trust incidents, employee flight, and disappointing productivity, forcing a retreat. The confidence behind this prediction rests on a double confirmation: the field's leading lab has itself codified the same lesson as implementation knowledge, and a large-scale real-world case that ignored that lesson has already been observed collapsing for exactly the reasons it predicts.

The practical implication for a reader is straightforward. When evaluating an organization's AI-substitution announcement, the question to ask is not "how capable is the model," but "where does this system draw its trust boundary." If that boundary sits inside the prompt or the model's own judgment, the substitution plan is likely to become fragile the moment it scales. If the boundary sits in a permanent structure outside the model — server-side verification, staged approval — the plan is more likely to keep capturing its gains sustainably. The principle is also falsifiable: if a large-scale AI substitution effort at Meta's scale succeeds durably without any upfront investment in verification infrastructure, this unifying principle breaks down. Conversely, the next time some company's substitution plan collapses, checking whether "a surge of trust and reliability incidents" appears among the stated causes is a concrete way to keep testing it.
