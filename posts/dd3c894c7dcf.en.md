## AI-Driven Disruption Is Reshaping SIer Business Models and Slimming Internal IT Departments

## Executive Summary

The central argument of this article is that advances in AI are exerting direct restructuring pressure not only on SaaS pricing models, but also on the "man-month" model used by SIers, where revenue is based on "work hours × unit price," as well as on staffing structures inside corporate IT departments. The source argues that while automation is advancing in downstream development processes, the importance of upstream strategy and planning functions, industry-specific knowledge, and personnel who can guarantee the final deliverable is actually increasing.

## How AI Is Disrupting SaaS Pricing Models and the SIer Man-Month Revenue Structure

The spread of AI agents is portrayed as forcing a rethink of both account-based pricing in SaaS and man-month billing in the SIer business. For SIers in particular, lower development effort could directly compress revenue, while also creating room for prime contractors to improve profitability.

- **Evidence**
- The source states that people are proclaiming "the death of SaaS (Software as a Service)."
- Traditional SaaS companies have "assigned permissions on an account basis for each user and charged on an account basis."
- With AI agents, the model is expected to shift to one in which "AI sits between people and SaaS and uses SaaS," meaning that "the number of user accounts previously required will decline."
- The direct revenue model for SIers is described as a "man-month model based on work hours × unit price," and the source states that "AI-powered automatic programming will become possible, leading to lower revenue as work hours decline."
- In Japan, "IT engineering resources are in short supply even compared with overseas," and SIers have therefore "focused on securing IT engineers."
- **Conclusion**
- Because AI can erode both SaaS pricing design and the revenue base of SIers, business operations that depend on simply expanding license counts or accumulating billable work hours will face changing preconditions.
- In Japan’s SIer market, the traditional model of "securing people and meeting demand" will no longer be sufficient on its own; revenue models will need to be redesigned on the assumption that AI is built in.
## Compression of the Multi-Tier Subcontracting Structure and the Return of In-House Development at Major SIers

The source positions development work such as programming as the area most heavily affected by AI. As a result, demand for labor at second- and third-tier subcontractors may shrink, while major SIers gain the option of cutting outsourcing costs and bringing more development work back inside.

- **Evidence**
- The SIer industry is described as having a "multi-tier subcontracting structure," in which "large system integrators act as prime contractors and handle project management, while subcontracted system development companies take on programming and other system development work."
- With AI, "the area especially affected will be development processes such as programming," and "reductions in development work hours and greater development efficiency" are expected.
- While "there is a possibility that the work hours of companies responsible for second- and third-tier development subcontracting will decline significantly," for "major SIers that outsource development, there is a possibility this will lead to lower outsourcing costs."
- The source says that although "total revenue, including outsourced portions, will decline," there is "a possibility of improving profitability," and adds that "for major SIers, it will become important not to carry large development headcounts, but to bring development processes into the company."
- At the same time, AI "does no more than produce the most statistically plausible answer," so there will not be "complete replacement" of core systems or accounting systems, and checking functions are considered "indispensable."
- The source states that "it is still the company that delivers the final deliverable to the client, and the need for human assurance of that deliverable does not change."
- **Conclusion**
- The first wave of AI will hit subcontracted development work hours more directly than prime contractors, weakening the economic rationale for the multi-tier subcontracting structure.
- However, because development will not be fully automated and "human assurance of the deliverable" will remain necessary, the key issue in business redesign is not simply headcount reduction, but which processes to internalize and where to retain responsibility for quality assurance.
- Differentiation will come not from standardized AI itself, but from "differences in human input," meaning demand for highly capable engineers will not disappear.
## Continued Expansion of the SIer Market and M&A Aimed at Acquiring Upstream Capabilities

The source argues that the SIer market itself is still expanding for now, while also hypothesizing that AI could reduce the number of companies needed and accelerate consolidation. At the same time, it contends that as the competitive axis shifts from downstream execution to upstream functions, M&A targets will broaden from technology supplementation to acquiring strategy and planning capabilities.

- **Evidence**
- The source states that "the SIer market itself is expanding, and is expected to continue expanding."
- Growth drivers cited include "replacement of core systems and DX needs arising at individual worksites."
- It also notes that "the end of standard maintenance for SAP ERP 6.0 (ECC6.0) at the end of 2027 is also approaching, so continued growth is expected."
- The industry is seeing many moves such as "acquisitions of second- and third-tier subcontractors by major SIer companies," "business combinations among second- and third-tier subcontractors aimed at service complementarity and synergy creation," and "acquisitions by SIers of solution vendors they had previously handled as partners."
- It also points to an increase in "agile firms spinning out from major consulting companies," cases where operating companies "acquire SIer companies in order to expand in-house IT teams," moves by major IT companies to "acquire talent to launch consulting businesses," and examples of PE funds that "aim for short-term capital gains, increase value over a short period, and then sell."
- As a future hypothesis, the source argues that "as AI increasingly substitutes for software, the number of software companies will decline," "the number of personnel needed for operations and maintenance will also decline," and "the number of SIer companies required will also decrease, making consolidation among SIers a possibility."
- Going forward, what will be required of SIers is "more upstream functions (strategy and planning)," and the source says that "designing the optimal IT architecture for formulating company-wide IT strategy and business strategy" as well as "industry knowledge in each sector" will become important.
- As a result, it says, "M&A by well-capitalized software companies and SIer companies to acquire upstream capabilities is also expected to increase."
- **Conclusion**
- Market expansion and industry restructuring can happen at the same time; the presence of demand does not mean consolidation will stop. AI is not shrinking the market so much as it is reshuffling the functions the market requires.
- The key issue in acquisitions is shifting from simply supplementing development capacity to absorbing upstream functions equipped with strategy formulation, requirements definition, and industry knowledge.
- Accordingly, the unit of competition for SIers is changing from "how many developers can they secure?" to "how deeply can they engage with the client’s business and IT strategy?"
## What M&A Valuations Must Examine: Identifying Upstream Functions and Analyzing Capabilities

The source warns that some companies claiming to handle upstream work are in reality still centered on second- and third-tier subcontracting, and it sets out specific criteria for M&A evaluation. It argues that what is needed is a multifaceted review that goes beyond financials to include process coverage, templates, personnel skills, and even prime-contract and outsourcing ratios.

- **Evidence**
- The source states that "even if a company claims to have upstream capabilities, in reality it may mainly operate as a second- or third-tier subcontractor, and may lack personnel with the consulting skills needed to analyze issues from an overall, bird’s-eye perspective."
- Because the IT domain spans a wide range of areas such as "ERP, DWH, AI, etc." and "security, networks, servers, etc.," it says there is a need to "carefully determine whether the company possesses capabilities in areas that have synergy with one’s own business."
- In market research and due diligence, in addition to "financial aspects such as sales and profit margins," it is necessary to analyze "organizational capabilities (which processes, solutions, and templates can be handled, etc.)," "human capabilities (skills, certifications held, etc.)," and "prime-contract ratios and outsourcing ratios, among other factors, from multiple angles."
- **Conclusion**
- A simple claim that a company "can do upstream work" is not enough to justify an acquisition; unless both the profit structure and the real state of the workforce are examined in depth, the expected integration effects will not materialize.
- In the AI era in particular, the presence or absence of repeatable assets such as templates, process design, architecture design, and personnel certifications and skills will become more important valuation criteria than simple revenue scale.
## AI-Driven Slimming of Internal IT Departments and the Redesign of SSCs and Outsourcing

The source extends AI’s impact beyond SIers to in-house corporate IT departments. As the number of software products declines and inquiry handling and operations and maintenance are automated, it argues, fewer IT personnel will be needed, accelerating externalization, consolidation, and a shift toward higher value-added work.

- **Evidence**
- In internal corporate IT departments, the source states that "AI disruption will reduce the number of software products handled," and that "as AI-driven automation advances in IT inquiry response and operations and maintenance as well, the number of personnel required in IT departments will decline."
- It says this is similar to what is happening in indirect functions such as "accounting, HR, and general affairs," where "there is a need to promote business rationalization and efficiency improvements in-house."
- For companies that do not retain transformation capabilities internally, the source says there are increasing cases where they "work with external consulting firms and outsourcing vendors, and in some cases establish JVs that include capital alliances and personnel transfers."
- Overseas, rising labor costs in Western Europe are driving more companies to "set up SSCs (shared service centers) in Central and Eastern Europe."
- As global business outsourcing locations, "India" is the most widely used, but in Europe, "Poland" is described as "consistently ranking in the Top5 in recent years," with "the use of Romania, Hungary, Bulgaria, and others" also identified as a recent trend.
- IT work is described as "an area where SSCs and outsourcing are easy to use," and the source states that "Deloitte’s global research shows that 53% of work is handled through SSCs/outsourcing."
- It adds that "with the use of AI, even more work will become capable of being externalized."
- **Conclusion**
- Internal IT departments are being redesigned on the assumption that they will shift from organizations focused on sustaining operations to organizations that selectively externalize functions and redeploy in-house personnel to higher value-added work.
- The issue is not simple cost cutting, but portfolio design: which functions to retain and which to move to SSCs, outsourcing, or JVs.
- In the source’s view, the more AI adoption advances, the less—not more—human importance declines; instead, "securing excellent engineers and optimally allocating human resources" becomes an even more prominent management challenge.
