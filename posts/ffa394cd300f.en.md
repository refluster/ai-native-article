## Executive Summary

On June 20, 2026, Anatoli Kopadze (@AnatoliKopadze) posted an X article titled “Loops explained: Claude, GPT, Mira and what actually works” on X, drawing 9.5M views. The post’s claim was simple and sharp: although AI has been in everyone’s hands for years, most people who use it every day are still relying on the slowest possible workflow — “type a request, wait, fix it, ask again” — entirely by hand. What that sentence targets is not the capability of the tools, but the structure of how they are used; its central point is that the line dividing who benefits from AI has shifted from “access” to the pattern of operation.

## The core claim — What determines speed is not the model, but the “pattern”

The names in the title — Claude, GPT, and Mira — are all proper names of individual models or tools. Even so, what the opening of the post treats as the issue is not which model is better, but how people are running them.

- The majority workflow described by the source is a sequential, manual cycle: “type a request, wait, fix it, ask again, all by hand.” The source explicitly calls this “the slowest way there is.”
- The title’s “Loops” is positioned as the concept set against this manual, sequential cycle. Following the source’s wording, what matters is “what actually works” — that is, the way of running things that functions in real operation, not the novelty of the tool itself.
What the source clearly asserts here is that the gains from AI are not determined by “which model you have.” Once we assume a world in which everyone can access the same models, what creates differences is the operating pattern — how requests and revisions are structured. Since the specific procedures inside the source text cannot be verified from the preview attached to the post, this briefing does not attempt to reconstruct them. What is certain is only the direction indicated by the opening sentence: the center of gravity has moved from “getting the tool” to “how you run the tool.”

## Implications — The line between “people who can use it” and “people who can’t” is being redrawn

The source itself is making an operational argument, but its reach extends to the boundaries of job roles. That is also why this briefing reads it in the context of Category B (Role Blurring).

- In a world where access to AI has become universal, what separates outcomes is no longer whether you have access, but whether you have a pattern. When the source points out that “even people who use it every day” are still using it in the slowest possible way, it is observing that a large gap remains between possessing the tool and truly wielding it effectively.
- That gap does not line up with the traditional divide between “technical” and “non-technical” roles. The difference between someone who stops after one manual back-and-forth and someone who has a way of running loops appears not along job titles, but along operating habits. The broad response — 9.5M views and 19K bookmarks — is circumstantial evidence that this is no longer an inside-baseball conversation among engineers, but a concern spreading to workers in general.
