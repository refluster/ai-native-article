A prototype now runs before the PRD, agents show up as customers, and TSMC becomes a shareholder of its own customers. Three seemingly unrelated moves can be explained by one principle: **when the cost of execution falls, what becomes scarce is not explanation but collateral, and documents move to the back, recording the judgment that collateral produced.**

## Introduction: three reorderings

Take three events side by side. In a blog post dated 15 April 2026, two Uber PMs looked back on a year of bringing AI prototyping into product development. In an article dated 11 November 2026, a16z's Olivia Moore and Justine Moore argued that AI agents are becoming the main users of the internet. TSMC's board approved up to USD 1 billion in investments in Anthropic and OpenAI.

The fields differ: product management, e-commerce traffic, semiconductor supply relationships. Yet each reads as a story about how a promise gets made. Traditionally, you explained first, agreed, and only then did the product or the deal move. In all three, the product or the deal moves first, and words and valuations catch up afterwards. This piece compresses that shared pattern into one hypothesis, and says what would prove it wrong.

## Uber: when the prototype comes first, the PRD becomes a record of judgment

The L2 "Why Uber chose a prototype before the PRD" identifies the core of Uber's observation as a change of order, not of speed. As prototyping starts earlier, exploration, agreement and the start of execution all move earlier. The persuasive job the PRD used to do ("we should build this") is taken over by a working prototype. What remains for the PRD is judgment, intent and trade-offs.

Note that the conclusion is not abolishing the PRD. The L2 concludes that prototype and PRD should be paired. The PRD does not vanish, but it shifts from a document that persuades readers into agreement to one that lets people reread a judgment already made. Explanation has moved behind the collateral.

## a16z: when agents become customers, the question is not the shop that explains but the shop that gets bought from

According to the L2 "The day agents become customers", a16z argues that companies must move from "locking bots out" to "winning bots' purchases". The evidence it cites: AI-originated traffic to Shopify stores up 8x year over year (Q1 '26), agentic-browser traffic up 8x between August and October, and coding agents as a precedent.

A shop built for human customers moved buyers with explanation: photos and copy. If agents buy more, the question becomes whether an agent can actually complete a purchase there. It is telling that a16z's argument moves from the entrance question of whether to exclude bots to the transaction question of winning their purchases. Here too, what works first is not persuasion in words but the collateral of a transaction that actually closes.

## TSMC: a supply relationship fixed by equity, not by contract language

The L2 "TSMC's board approves up to USD 1 billion for Anthropic and OpenAI" reports approval of up to USD 500 million for shares of Anthropic at its IPO price and up to USD 500 million for OpenAI preferred shares, up to USD 1 billion in total. It calls this "equitising the supply relationship". By becoming a shareholder of its customers, the manufacturer gains a second revenue path, the rise in their valuations. It also takes on concentration risk, since those valuations depend on the outlook for AI demand.

A supply contract promises future trade in words. Adding equity secures that promise by having the party bear the outcome too. The relationship is fixed not by adding more clauses but by sharing results. Again, collateral sits outside the explanation (the contract).

## The shared principle: Why → So What

These facts suggest the following.

**Why.** In all three cases, the step of making a promise in words has left center stage. The PRD records judgment after the prototype, the shop's explanation yields to whether an agent's purchase completes, and the supply contract is supplemented by equity. What they share is that verifiable artifacts or shared profit and loss move before, or alongside, explanation in words.

**So What.** Where execution gets cheap, building a prototype or trying a transaction becomes cheap. Explanation was supposed to be cheaper than execution, but the cheaper execution gets, the lower the relative value of explanation. What becomes scarce is collateral: it really runs, it really gets bought, you really bear the profit and loss. Documents do not become unnecessary; they shift to recording, afterwards, the judgments that collateral produced.

**Why this is not just "faster".** One could lump the three together as "faster" or "cheaper". But Uber's L2 states explicitly that the core is a change of order, not speed. If it were only faster, the order would not change. a16z's measurements show not just more traffic but a different kind of buyer. TSMC's investment has nothing to do with speed or cost. What the three share is not speed but a structural change in the order in which a promise is backed, and by what. That is what separates this hypothesis from a story about efficiency.

The principle can be refuted in these ways. If PRDs became longer and more persuasion-centered after prototyping was introduced, Uber would be an exception. If shops that won agent purchases differentiated themselves by richer explanation alone, a16z's "winning" does not depend on completed-purchase collateral. If TSMC's equity has no effect on the strength of supply commitments, "equitisation" is merely a financial add-on. If any one of the three breaks, the principle weakens.

Note also that the three L2s present each company's observations or press reports, not research that tests the principle itself. What I am doing is induction, not proof.

## Forecast and implications

If the principle holds, the following may happen, with rough confidence.

First, in product development, proposals without a working prototype will find it harder to win agreement. Whether Uber's case generalizes beyond one company is not yet known. I put the confidence at medium, because the L2's observation that prototyping moves exploration, agreement and the start of execution earlier has a structure that is not confined to one domain.

Second, where agent purchasing grows, "can an agent complete a purchase?" becomes a yardstick for shops. a16z's 8x figures show the direction, but whether the growth lasts is a separate matter. Medium confidence, with durability uncertain.

Third, supply relationships fixed by equity may appear beyond TSMC. This cannot be said directly from the three L2s and is conjecture. Confidence is low. The concentration risk TSMC itself takes on, valuations that depend on the outlook for AI demand, shows this arrangement is not a one-way gain.

The practical takeaway for readers comes down to one question. If your work has a step of "explain and win agreement", ask first whether something can be made to run, or a profit-and-loss share formed, before it. Once collateral stands first, documents can concentrate on their role: recording what has been decided.
