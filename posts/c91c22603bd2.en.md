The value of a career is determined by “how much experience you’ve accumulated” — that premise is collapsing simultaneously beneath three seemingly unrelated facts. A former Chief Product Officer at Slack is seriously looking for a PM internship, developers are taking their hands off the keyboard and beginning to communicate intent in natural language, and Meta has forcibly reassigned 30〜50% of its core engineers to data labeling. At first glance these look unrelated, but in fact they can all be explained by the same principle. The moment AI shifts where scarcity resides, experience — once a source of value — turns directly into debt.

## Those at the top return to the entrance

What “A former Slack Chief Product Officer looks for a ‘PM internship’” (Role Blurring) captured is the paradox of someone who reached the peak of a career deliberately seeking an entry-level role. In a case posted by Brandon K. Hill of btrax on June 29, 2026, a former Chief Product Officer at Slack is seriously looking for a PM internship. The very premise that organizational hierarchy is something you climb in one direction is beginning to collapse, and the redefinition of roles itself is becoming the thing to observe.

Why would someone with a top-tier title go back to the entrance? The answer is that the scarcity signaled by that title now exists somewhere else. The role of CPO was a pricing mechanism for the scarce skill of product judgment. But when part of that skill is absorbed by AI, or the underlying premises are rearranged, accumulated executive experience no longer guarantees “closeness to today’s scarcity.” So the rational actor does not remain at the top of an old experience curve, but voluntarily descends to the entrance of a new one. A “career downshift” is not a fall — it is a return to where scarcity currently lives.

## From the skill of “writing” to the skill of “guiding”

The same repricing is happening in software development. What the WSJ reported as the “spread of vibe coding” (Developer Tools) is a scene in which programmers are rapidly adopting interactive, iterative development with tools like Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex. The key point is that the center of gravity in development has shifted: away from hand-writing code and toward communicating intent in natural language, then repeatedly generating and revising.

What is losing value here is the skill itself of “typing code quickly and accurately.” For years, that was central to a developer’s value. But once AI absorbs that portion of the work, scarcity moves toward the ability to define intent, verify generated output, and give direction — deciding what should be built. Experience gained by moving one’s hands can no longer remain an asset on its own; unless it is reconfigured into experience in guiding intent, it steadily depreciates. Structurally this is the same as in the first case, where the premise of hierarchy broke down: the axis of value is being replaced, shifting from quantity accumulated to “closeness to today’s scarcity.”

## From asset to cost — by force

The third fact shows that this repricing does not always wait for individual choice; sometimes it comes down from the organization itself. In “Why did Meta’s engineering culture collapse?” (Big Tech), Pragmatic Engineer’s Gergely Orosz (June 16, 2026) argues that since Meta’s June 2025 acquisition of Scale AI, the company has been dismantling its own engineering culture through a restructuring that places AI strategy above all else. 30〜50% of engineers from core teams were forcibly reassigned to the ADO organization responsible for data labeling, keystroke monitoring was made mandatory, and token counts were introduced into performance evaluation. Even amid record profits, a 10% reduction across all employees has been announced.

Orosz sees this as a structural shift in which engineering is being redefined from a “profit center” to a “cost center.” Here too, scarcity has moved. What was once scarce was the skill of engineers who could write the product itself. What Meta now treats as scarce is the data needed to train AI, and so people are mobilized to the supply side of that need — labeling — while those on the writing side are compressed as cost. Unlike the first case, where an individual voluntarily moved back to the entrance, or the second case, where the axis of skill quietly shifted, at Meta the same dynamic is being imposed from the outside through surveillance and forced reassignment. That is what makes it so stark.

## Shared principle: the moment scarcity moves, experience turns into debt

These three facts — a voluntary move from the peak back to the entrance, a shift from the skill of writing to the skill of guiding, and a forced redefinition from asset to cost — appear unrelated on the surface. But together they suggest a single principle. Namely: **the value of a profession is not determined by “how much experience has been accumulated,” but by “how close it is to the current location of scarcity.” And experience, while an asset, becomes debt the moment scarcity moves.**

Why can we say that? Experience is capital accumulated by repeatedly exercising something that was scarce in the past. Its value depends entirely on whether that thing is still scarce now. AI is precisely a mechanism that rapidly shifts where scarcity resides. Product judgment, writing code, product development itself — as these are absorbed one by one into AI, the experience capital tied to them loses its field of application and becomes debt that only incurs maintenance cost. What is breaking across all three cases is the career’s implicit premise that “if you accumulate experience, your value increases monotonically.”

So what follows from that? If this principle is correct, then the rational career strategy in the age of AI is not to “accumulate more experience,” but to “keep moving yourself closer to the current location of scarcity.” The former CPO descending to the entrance is not irrational at all; it is the optimal behavior if one is faithful to this principle. By contrast, Meta’s engineers remained on the old axis after scarcity had moved, and were therefore processed from the outside as debt. What separated the two was not the amount of experience they had, but the speed of their response to the movement of scarcity.

## Forecasts and implications

If we extend this principle, several changes in circumstances become foreseeable.

First, the normalization of “career downshifts” (confidence: high). The faster scarcity moves, the shorter the cycle in which top-level experience turns into debt. The former Slack CPO case is not an exception but a leading indicator, and over the next several years we are likely to see more senior people visibly returning to entry points on their own. The reason is simple: the frontier of AI capability continues to move in units of months — and as long as that movement continues, “intentional demotion” for the sake of relearning will remain rational.

Second, pressure to make evaluation axes visible (confidence: medium). Meta’s token-count metrics and keystroke monitoring are crude attempts to measure the movement of scarcity. Similar indicators aimed at measuring “closeness to the new scarcity” will spread to other companies as well, but many of them will come with the same side effect shown in the third case: the normalization of surveillance, at the cost of cultural disintegration. The design of metrics themselves — deciding what axis to measure people on — will increasingly become part of competitive advantage.

Third, the spread of the idea of a skill’s half-life (confidence: medium〜high). What vibe coding shows is that the half-life of the “skill of writing” has become shorter than that of the “skill of guiding.” Decisions about where to invest in learning will shift from “how deeply should I master this?” to “is the scarcity this skill points to still there now?”

There is one practical takeaway for readers. When you take inventory of your own experience, do not ask “how much have I accumulated?” Ask instead: “Does the scarcity this experience points to still exist there now?” If the answer is “it has already moved somewhere else,” then protecting your accumulation is slower than moving closer to the current location — even if that means going back to the entrance. Experience is an asset in a world where premises do not move, but in an era when scarcity itself moves, it remains an asset only when paired with the courage to let go of it.
