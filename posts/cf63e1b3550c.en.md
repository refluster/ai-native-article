## Executive Summary

On July 8, 2026, Databricks published the results of an internal benchmark evaluating models and harnesses on coding tasks actually handled by its own engineers. It concluded that the open model GLM 5.2 was statistically tied with Opus 4.8 on quality while costing less per task at $1.28 versus $1.94, and that token pricing is an inadequate proxy for real task cost. It also showed that even with the same model, differences in the harness alone could more than double per-task cost, meaning efficiency cannot be solved by model selection alone.

The analysis as a whole can be distilled into four main conclusions. First, the Pareto frontier for coding tasks—the best quality available at a given cost—includes models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and open source alike. In other words, delivering frontier performance today requires a combination of multiple tools. Second, open models, especially GLM 5.2, have now reached the point where they can handle even the hardest tasks. Third, a model’s token price is an inadequate measure of the real cost incurred in end-to-end tasks, and larger models can be more token-efficient and therefore cheaper overall. Fourth, the harness used to call the model has a major impact on both cost and quality, and in many cases a simple harness like Pi produced the best results.

## Why it built a benchmark from its own PRs instead of relying on public benchmarks

Databricks points to two limitations in public benchmarks such as SWE-Bench and TerminalBench. First, because the tasks are public, the answers leak into training data over time. Second, for its own codebase—which spans more than 10 languages including Scala, Go, Rust, Java, Python, Bazel, and Protobuf—the results were not representative.

So the company built its benchmark from its own merged pull requests. Its engineers merge thousands of code changes a day, and PRs with commit history, human review, and tests make strong source material. It then applied the following quality filters:

- **Recency**: sampled from recent history so the benchmark reflects the frameworks and conventions currently in use
- **Human written**: excluded bot commits, service accounts, and fully AI-generated or auto-generated changes
- **Backed by a high-quality test suite**: limited to PRs that included tests capable of validating the change
- **Self-contained**: changes had to be confined to a small number of modules
- **Representative of typical tasks**: selected from a full-stack distribution including Scala backend work, Rust systems code, React/TypeScript frontend work, protobuf/gRPC, and Bazel configuration
To turn PRs into tasks, it interpreted the intent of each PR and converted it into a prompt, stripping away any explanation of the solution and leaving only the problem, goal, and constraints. Test files were split out and held out, so the model had to reproduce the non-test-file changes on its own. Each sample was evaluated manually, and when necessary the tests were rewritten without using AI. No LLM judge was used for pass/fail grading, because, in the company’s words, that would “reward sounding right over being right.”

## Three capability tiers and a design built around routing tasks

The results showed a clear split between models and harnesses into three capability tiers. The smartest models at the top tier worked across every kind of problem, but were extremely expensive; mid-tier and lower-tier models remained highly effective on common tasks while being much cheaper.

Inside Databricks, the default model had traditionally always been the most expensive one. Based on this analysis, the company concluded that common operational tasks such as toggling flags or updating configuration should instead be routed toward cheaper Haiku- or GPT 5.4 Mini-class models. Log analysis also showed that roughly one-quarter of tasks were tagged low-complexity and about 60% were tagged medium-complexity, leaving substantial room for efficiency gains.

## Look at cost per task, not cost per token

The core finding is that token pricing cannot predict the cost of a task end to end. Inference efficiency varies too much across models.

- **GLM 5.2**: landed in the top tier and was statistically tied with Opus 4.8 on quality, while costing $1.28 per task versus Opus at $1.94.
- **Sonnet 5 vs. Opus 4.8**: Sonnet 5 is about 1.7x cheaper than Opus on a per-token basis. But on real tasks, Sonnet cost $2.09/task versus Opus at $1.94, and its task completion rate was also 6 points lower at 81% versus 87%. That was because Sonnet 5 ran longer, read more, and consumed 1.9x as many tokens.
In other words, open models have now reached the point where they can serve as a “daily driver” even for coding, and there are cases where a larger model wins on token efficiency and lowers total cost.

## The harness has a major impact on cost efficiency

Databricks ran the same model at the same thinking effort through two harnesses—Claude Code/Codex and Pi—and found that while quality stayed the same, per-task cost could differ by more than 2x. The main reason was the amount of context passed to the model on each turn. Pi sent only about one-third as much context per turn, narrowed the working set, and finished tasks with fewer execution steps.

As a result, Databricks’s conclusion is not that “one harness is always cheaper,” nor that “native harnesses are inferior,” but rather that model selection is only one piece of the puzzle. The company says it is investing in Omnigent to make it seamless to swap both models and harnesses.

## The loophole that distorted the benchmark: sealing off Git history

In early experiments, some models’ scores looked “too good to be true.” A manual review of the traces revealed that in the original setup, the “correct” implementation could still be recovered from the worktree’s Git history. Because each task was derived from a merged commit, an agent with shell access had nothing preventing it from walking Git history and arriving at the answer. To fix this, Databricks completely detached the working copy from the repository during execution and sealed off Git history.

The episode highlights a pitfall in building benchmarks from internal data, while also showing that the method is reproducible. Any team with a backlog of merged PRs already possesses a benchmark that no model has trained on and that is graded using tests written by that team itself—that is the company’s argument. Databricks says it will continue adding more difficult tasks and will run every new agent and harness through this benchmark.
