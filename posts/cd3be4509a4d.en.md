## Executive Summary

In October 2026 Atlassian published its "Forward Deployed Engineering Playbook," drawn from 100+ enterprise customers and 80+ production AI agents. Its conclusion is that enterprise AI stalls not because of model capability but because of what surrounds it: problem selection, governance, workflow integration and economics. Its four lessons are to pick the right problem, treat governance as part of the architecture, design AI to become part of how work gets done, and optimize the system rather than just the model. Every figure in the document is the vendor's own claim, not independently verified.

## What was published

The playbook is a 22-page PDF dated October 2026. Atlassian's Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) are senior engineers who combine software engineering and applied AI in one role, embedding with customer teams to build production AI use cases. The document says the program has covered 100+ enterprise customers and 80+ production AI agents.

Engagements typically last about 12 weeks and follow four phases:

- **Discover**: identify high-value business workflows and map system context and permissions
- **Build**: co-engineer, test and deploy production-ready workflow and AI solutions grounded in the organization's context
- **Adopt**: drive sustained usage, establish operational governance, and equip internal engineering teams with repeatable patterns to continue building autonomously
- **Value**: measure business impact, demonstrate ROI, and optimize for cost, speed, revenue and reach
The stated goal is to leave customers with working AI in production and a repeatable approach their teams can carry forward. FDEs are also described as remediating blockers directly within the Atlassian platform rather than applying temporary workarounds.

## The claimed results (all self-reported)

The headline cases are:

- A technology company saved $20M+ in direct support costs. AI triages and resolves tier-1 and tier-2 issues end to end, cutting mean time to resolution from 18 days to 10 minutes while maintaining customer satisfaction. Elsewhere the document cites 135,000+ issues resolved autonomously.
- A semiconductor manufacturer cut engineering-services triage effort by approximately 50% with a custom triage agent, unblocking production AI across 40,000 employees.
- A travel company expects to save 6,700+ hours a year across 20+ teams, via two production Rovo agents: one archived approximately 600,000 pages, the other automates work-item triage.
On the product side, Atlassian says grounding Rovo in Teamwork Graph context produced 44% higher-quality answers using 48% fewer tokens with no reduction in speed, tested across 10 complex scenarios with and without that context. It also says teams whose AI draws most heavily on the Teamwork Graph ship 64% more work.

None of these come with methodology, and customers are unnamed. Read what follows with that in mind.

## The four lessons

### 01 Pick the right problem

Hundreds of use cases are possible; far fewer are frequent, painful and measurable. FDEs work backward from a business bottleneck, looking for workflows that are:

- **Frequent**: often enough for small improvements to compound
- **Painful**: people genuinely want the work easier or gone
- **Measurable**: someone already owns an outcome that can prove whether the agent works
Common starting points by industry: developer productivity in software (Jira + GitHub workflows); governance-first AI in financial services (Rovo Search and Chat before agents); PII identification and sanitization in healthcare; ticket triage in IT/service management (JSM).

Cases: a telecom built an agent that turns planning discussions into Jira stories and hierarchies, saving $1M in custom development. Another telecom replaced an assistant that frequently misrouted tickets with a Rovo triage agent grounded in JSM and Teamwork Graph context. A financial institution went from uncertainty to a working agent in a secure sandbox in under three weeks.

Field lesson: focus on high-frequency, measurable friction over novel use cases.

### 02 Treat governance as part of the architecture

Traditional software is relatively predictable; AI is nondeterministic. What data can an agent see, which permissions does it inherit, what actions can it take? The playbook reports technically successful pilots that stalled because these questions were answered after the build, and off-the-shelf agents that failed in enterprise environments for lack of context, integrations and guardrails.

FDEs build security, permissions and risk controls in from day one, then widen autonomy as trust grows, in three steps:

1. **Read**: search, retrieval and summarization 2. **Draft**: AI recommends an action, a person approves it 3. **Act**: automate well-understood, bounded actions once the organization is comfortable with the controls

Cases: a finance team's commercial-operations agent drafts orders with human review and a full Jira audit trail, cutting order creation from hours to minutes at 95% accuracy before human review (about 11.8k hours a year). A bank enabled Rovo Chat and Search for roughly 35,000 users, starting read-oriented while building the auditability and governance needed for custom agents. A healthcare company removed PII before data entered Teamwork Graph and built agents on the sanitized data.

Field lesson: design governance into agentic workflows from the start.

### 03 Design AI to become part of how work gets done

Adoption spikes, then novelty fades. The playbook separates two signals: a successful launch shows curiosity; sustained usage shows value. Its data says agents triggered automatically by real-world events are 12x more likely to become part of sustained workflows than agents that rely on manual prompting. FDEs therefore:

- **Trigger automatically** on work events: a ticket enters a queue, a pull request opens, a sprint ends, an incident begins
- **Meet people where they work**: inside Jira, JSM, Confluence, Slack, Bitbucket rather than a new AI destination
- **Measure after launch**: AI-enabled users, workflow outcomes and customer-defined business metrics
Cases: an onboarding agent cut 30/60/90-day plan creation time by 40% (about 2,500 manager-hours a year). A travel company with 120+ agents and low adoption got far better uptake from one ticket-triage agent embedded in live work. A motorsports organization went from zero to six production agents in under five months. Another travel company, with missing data across about 1,200 Jira projects, used a Slack assistant that suggests missing fields and writes approved updates to Jira in one click, after automated reminders had failed to change behavior.

Field lesson: don't ask people to remember to use AI; put AI inside work they already do.

### 04 Optimize the system, not just the model

A workflow that suits 100 people may look very different at 50,000. The playbook admits long-term enterprise-agent economics are still early to measure, and names three levers:

1. **Reuse context instead of rebuilding it**, via a structured upstream context layer 2. **Match compute to the task**, evaluating models on complexity, latency, accuracy and economics rather than defaulting to the most powerful 3. **Measure against the status quo**, comparing AI cost with the manual effort it removes

Cases: an e-commerce company's daily AI brief consumed over 1 million tokens per run searching Slack, Jira, Gmail and Calendar separately; connecting Teamwork Graph to its LLM workflows significantly reduced tokens and latency. A telecom's triage agent cut initial response from days to minutes with significantly higher accuracy. A technology company extended AI search to third-party sources at what leadership estimated to be a fraction of competing enterprise-search cost.

Field lesson: manage AI economics across context, compute and the value of the work.

## The four compound

A wrong use case won't sustain adoption; an ungoverned agent won't reach production; AI outside the workflow won't become habit; and scaling usage without understanding economics can raise cost without raising value. The closing claim: enterprise AI often stalls because everything around it isn't ready to put the capability to work.

## What Atlassian says it is still figuring out

- How much of today's custom work can become tomorrow's starting point, by turning cross-engagement patterns into reusable agents, connectors and use cases
- How to help customers scale what works without Atlassian, with the aim of self-sufficiency rather than long-term dependency
- What the next bottlenecks are as building gets dramatically faster: the document observes constraints moving to planning, review, cross-team dependencies, governance and operations
The third is notable: a vendor selling AI deployment writes that implementation is no longer the binding constraint.

## Assessment: what this supports and what it doesn't

**Evidence.** The value lies in structure more than numbers. The frequent/painful/measurable test, the Read→Draft→Act ladder, the gap between event-triggered and manually prompted agents (12x), and context reuse are concrete design decisions. The cases cluster around bounded, repetitive work: triage, data hygiene, routine reporting.

**Implication.** Durable value comes less from autonomy than from repeatability and measurability of the work, permission design, and placement inside existing tools. Widening autonomy as trust grows lowers adoption friction more than starting with autonomous agents.

**Caveats.** First, every outcome figure ($20M+, 18 days to 10 minutes, 44%, 64%, 12x) is Atlassian's own, with unnamed customers and no stated comparison baseline or method. Second, the FDE offer presumes Atlassian's platform (Rovo, Teamwork Graph, Jira, JSM, Confluence), so general lessons need separating from platform-specific advantages. Third, the playbook itself calls long-term economics measurement "still early." It is best read as a vendor's organized set of design hypotheses from its own field work, not as proof that enterprise AI succeeds.
