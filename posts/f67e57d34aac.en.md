## Executive Summary

An analysis published on June 11, 2026 by recruiter seb (@hiiinternet, Head of Talent Engineering at @fonzi) collected 5,306 LinkedIn profiles listing Anthropic as their current employer, then closely examined the backgrounds of the 1,680 who were actually engineers, covering 7,986 prior roles. The conclusion was “Builders, not researchers” — only 13.7% held PhDs, median years of experience before joining was 12.2, and the biggest source of hires was neither OpenAI nor DeepMind but Google. The image of a frontier AI lab as a “research institution packed with PhDs” does not match reality, at least for the engineering organization.

## Data source and methodology — A study using LinkedIn self-reports as the population

This analysis is not an academic study but a primary aggregation by a practitioner in recruiting. The assumptions matter, so it is worth stating them precisely.

- Population: 5,306 people on LinkedIn listing Anthropic as their “current employer.” From that pool, the analysis narrowed to 1,680 people identified as actual engineers.
- Scope of analysis: 7,986 prior job entries listed by those 1,680 people.
- Nature of the data: The dataset is based on LinkedIn self-reporting, and role classifications depend on the skills and titles individuals chose to list. Accordingly, this is a proxy for “who Anthropic hires,” not the company’s official internal HR data.
Even with those limitations, the sample is large enough to cover nearly the entire engineering organization, which makes the direction of the trends visible. All figures below are based on that aggregation.

## A giant organization built in about 18 months

As evidence, the speed of organizational expansion stands out.

- Of the engineers still at the company today, only 15 had been there since before 2021.
- In 2025, the organization roughly tripled in size, hiring 686 people. In 2026 it was moving at a similar pace, with 455 already hired by June.
- Half of the current engineering organization has been there for less than 1 year. Fully 53% joined in the last 12 months. Median tenure is 10 months.
As a conclusion, seb describes this as “A giant org, built in about 18 months.” On the median, the typical engineer looks like “12 years of experience, 10 months of tenure,” meaning most of the organization’s knowledge is being carried by very recent hires.

## They hire almost exclusively senior people — New-grad hiring is basically nonexistent

Evidence:

- Median pre-hire experience is 12.2 years. The middle 50% falls between 8.8 and 16.5 years.
- Of the 1,680 people, only 50 had less than 3 years of experience. Those with 13 or more years account for 44%.
- “New-grad hiring is basically nonexistent.”
Conclusion: hiring is clearly weighted toward the senior end, and junior candidates enter only through exceptional pathways, discussed below. This suggests a hiring design centered less on “develop and grow people” and more on “bring in immediate contributors.”

## Backgrounds skew heavily toward infrastructure, not research

The strongest support for the claim that these are “builders, not researchers” is the distribution of specialization across prior experience.

- Infrastructure appears in 40% of backgrounds. Backend, distributed systems, databases, and security each appear at roughly 20%.
- By contrast, reinforcement learning — the “RL” in RLHF — appears in only 3.3%.
- Self-reported skills point in the same direction: Python 585, Java 566, C++ 443, JavaScript 376, SQL 302, Linux 230, Distributed Systems 189, AWS 154.
Conclusion: the typical Anthropic engineer is someone who has spent the past decade building large-scale production systems at a hyperscaler or an infrastructure-heavy startup. Glamorous model-training work exists, the analysis says, but it is rare.

## The biggest talent source is not rival labs but Google

Evidence:

- “Everyone assumes Anthropic poaches from OpenAI and DeepMind, but the biggest pipeline by far is Google.”
- Companies people had worked at at some point in their careers (all-time): Google 405, Meta 273, Amazon 197, Microsoft 171, Stripe 124, Apple 87, Stanford 68, DeepMind 62, Airbnb 51, OpenAI 48.
- Half the organization (50%) has FAANG somewhere in their background.
- Among direct moves from competing labs, OpenAI ranks 5th and DeepMind 6th. About 94 people came directly from a frontier lab.
- Anthropic hires above average from companies known for engineering rigor, including Stripe, Databricks, Snowflake, Palantir, and Airbnb.
Conclusion: the talent flow differs from the conventional story of an inter-lab poaching war. In practice, Anthropic is drawing primarily from large-scale engineering organizations. For anyone competing with Anthropic in hiring, the real rivals are not so much other AI labs as hyperscalers and companies with strong infrastructure reputations.

## The “PhD myth” and title flattening

Evidence:

- Only 13.7% hold PhDs. “One in seven.”
- Fields of study: Computer Science 819, Mathematics 78, Physics 70, Computer Engineering 69. Philosophy makes the top 20 (13 people; seb adds the note “safety-related?”).
- Schools attended (all-time): Stanford 144, Berkeley 118, MIT 80, CMU 73, Harvard 42, Cambridge 39, UW 36, Waterloo and Cornell at 35 each, Oxford 33, Princeton 32. The top 4 schools alone account for about one-quarter of the organization.
- 80% share the same title: “Member of Technical Staff (MoTS).” Former Instagram CTO, former Adept founder, and even a former Stanford faculty member are all uniformly labeled “MoTS.” “The title is flattened on purpose.”
Conclusion: the median engineer is not a research scientist but a senior engineer with a bachelor’s or master’s degree. The picture of a “lab packed with PhDs” is, for the engineering organization, broadly wrong. The flattening of titles reflects a design philosophy that intentionally obscures seniority and function.

## The only path in for juniors — not titles, but “substitutes for experience”

There are exceptional pathways for younger hires, but they differ from ordinary mid-level hiring.

- 172 people have less than 6 years of experience, and 50 have less than 3. The structure is polarized, with very few “ordinary mid-career” profiles.
- Differences from the broader organization: a higher PhD rate (19% vs 13.7%), 3x the rate of product/SWE titles (15% vs 5%), and less FAANG experience (32% vs 50%).
- Internship pathway: 50% list internships at Meta 16, Google 10, DeepMind 6, Microsoft 5, Amazon 5, plus internships at Jane Street, Two Sigma, HRT, Optiver, Nvidia, and others.
- Quant → lab: 9% came through elite trading firms such as Jane Street, Two Sigma, Five Rings, HRT, Optiver, and Citadel.
- Alignment fellowships: 6% were involved in one of MATS, SERI, Redwood, or ARC. This is a junior-only entry path that is almost nonexistent among senior hires.
- Typical profile: MIT graduate, IOI silver medalist, Codeforces 2900+, and by year 4 of their career already moving directly into RL and safety. Selection happens not by seniority but by competition ranking and papers.
- School backgrounds are also more international: Berkeley 15, Stanford 14, Cambridge 10, MIT 7, Tsinghua 7, Oxford 6, plus Imperial, NUS, Shanghai Jiao Tong, and ETH Zürich.
Conclusion: for junior candidates, the gate is pedigree that substitutes for years of experience — top internships, competitive programming rankings, or papers. The organization is not bringing in juniors as a “development cohort,” but as people who can replace years of tenure with other forms of evidence.

## Conclusion — What the hiring data says about Anthropic’s self-image

What this analysis shows is the gap between the outside image of a “frontier AI lab = a collective of PhD researchers” and the company’s actual hiring behavior. The reality of the engineering organization is much closer to a group of senior builders who have spent the past decade building large-scale systems. seb’s own advice is straightforward: if you want to join Anthropic as an engineer, write a resume for an infrastructure company, not a research lab, and show the systems you have actually built and scaled. Juniors are the only real exception, and there the criteria are top internships, competition rankings, or papers. This dataset, however, is still a primary aggregation based on LinkedIn self-reporting, so it is best read as evidence of directional trends.
