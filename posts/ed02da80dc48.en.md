Cheaper components do not destroy value. Value does not disappear; it shifts to the layer above that bundles those components together. That is the core argument of this article. Consider three developments that seem unrelated at first glance. Even as open-source AI has taken the lead in token throughput, Anthropic’s share of spending has barely been dented. Anthropic is discussing in-house chips with Samsung, yet frames the effort not as replacing Nvidia but as “diversification.” And the majority of people who use AI every day are still using it manually in the slowest possible way. These three facts may look disconnected, but they are all explained by the same principle. Commoditization arrives wrapped in the story of substitution, but what is actually happening is a re-layering of value.

## Why the Story of “Replacement” Misses the Mark

Let’s begin with the contradiction highlighted by L2, “Why the Rise of Open-Source AI Still Hasn’t Hurt Anthropic.” On 2026年7月7日, Decagon CEO Jesse Zhang pointed to a striking reality: even as mature AI deployments shift toward lightweight, open-source models, total spending on frontier models has barely declined. Real-world data from Vercel and OpenRouter backs this up—while open-source models such as DeepSeek now lead in processed token volume, Anthropic still accounts for more than half of spending.

The naive expectation was that “cheap models would eat expensive models.” If the flow of tokens shifts, then the flow of money should shift with it. But the money did not move. What makes Zhang’s interpretation so sharp is that he sees this not as a temporary lag, but as a structural feature. Frontier models handle “discovery” (the stage of solving difficult, unknown problems), while open-source models handle “production” (the stage of running established processes cheaply at scale). For the time being, that two-layer structure may be a stable feature of the AI economy.

What must not be overlooked here is the fact itself that token volume and spending are diverging. If the two were in a simple substitution relationship, then share of volume and share of spending would move together. The fact that they do not means the two layers are delivering different kinds of value and being priced separately. Even when the cheap layer absorbs the volume, the expensive layer continues to earn on the density of judgment.

## Not Replacement, but Distribution

The same pattern—something that looks like substitution but is not—appears again at the hardware layer in L2, “Anthropic Discusses In-House AI Chip With Samsung.” On 2026年7月2日, The Information reported that Anthropic was in talks with Samsung to develop a new custom AI chip. The move came about one week after OpenAI announced its inference processor “Jalapeño” with Broadcom, against a backdrop of semiconductor supply constraints and a push to reduce dependence on Nvidia.

It is tempting to read this as “moving away from Nvidia.” But Anthropic’s own position is clearly different. While saying that use cases and specifications are still undecided, the company states that “a diversified hardware stack including Google, Amazon, and Nvidia is central to our compute strategy.” In other words, an in-house chip is not a piece meant to drive Nvidia out; it is a piece that adds one more option. Here too, the one-to-one story in which “A replaces B” does not apply. What is happening is a shift away from concentration in a single vendor and toward a portfolio that bundles multiple layers together.

Put these two L2 stories side by side, and it becomes clear that the logic is identical even if the layer is different. Whether in models or in chips, when lower-level components become cheaper and more diverse, the winner is not the party that makes those elements at the lowest cost, but the party that combines them most effectively. Anthropic is not trying to become a chip manufacturer. It is trying to seize control over the architecture that bundles diverse compute resources together.

## From Access to Operation

The third L2, “AI Used in ‘Loops,’” reflects the same principle at the level of individual usage. On 2026年6月20日, Anatoli Kopadze’s X post “Loops explained” drew 9.5M views. Its claim was simple and sharp: AI has been in everyone’s hands for years, and yet the majority of daily users still rely on the slowest possible manual pattern—“type a request → wait → revise → ask again.”

What matters here is that the post does not attribute the problem to the performance of the tools. The models are already fast enough. The real differentiator is the operating pattern—how they are run. Access has been completely commoditized. So the dividing line for who benefits has shifted from “who can access it” to “who knows how to run it.” The first two L2 stories were making this point at the organizational and corporate level; this post restates it at the individual level. Once cheap, widely available tools—access to frontier models, GPUs, chat interfaces—have fully diffused, value resides not in the tools themselves, but in the patterns used to run them.

## The Shared Principle: Commoditization Does Not Erase Value, It Moves It Up a Layer

Put the three facts together, and one principle comes into view. Even when open-source gets cheaper, spending on frontier models does not fall. Even as chips diversify, the strategic core shifts away from any individual chip and toward the overall configuration. Even when access becomes universal, the benefits are determined not by access but by the operating pattern. Taken together, these facts suggest that **when a capability becomes commoditized, value does not disappear, nor does it move wholesale into the cheapest substitute; it shifts instead to the next layer up, the layer that bundles and operates that capability**.

The mistake we make over and over is to read commoditization as a story of “substitution.” The cheap thing eats the expensive thing, and volume drags spending along with it. But the facts we observe all point instead to “re-layering.” The cheaper and more abundant the lower layer becomes, the more scarce the layer becomes that correctly combines it, assigns division of labor across it, and runs it. The place where value is captured slips away from the cheapest component and toward the orchestration layer that organizes the components.

This principle is falsifiable. If pure substitution were actually happening, then Anthropic’s spending share should fall in proportion to the rise in open-source token share—but it has not. If access were really the differentiator, then as access became universal, the benefits should become evenly distributed—but instead, “the minority who run loops” are pulling away. Conversely, if it could be shown that the frontier premium is merely a temporary lock-in that will erode within a few years, or that custom silicon is in the end nothing more than a lagged replacement for Nvidia, then this principle would collapse. That would be the moment the substitution camp was proved right.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, then the central question for every organization quietly shifts. It moves away from “Which model, which chip, which tool should we buy?”—questions at the component layer—and toward “How do we combine them, and how do we operate them?”—questions at the orchestration layer. The more commoditization advances, the less differentiation there is in the former; the more commoditization advances, the wider the gap becomes in the latter. I would rate this as highly likely. The fact that the same sign is being observed simultaneously across three independent layers—the model economy, semiconductor procurement, and individual patterns of use—is itself evidence that this is not a coincidence confined to a single domain.

For agent-native organizations, the implication is direct and weighty. Value lies not in the model, but in the operating model. Which LLM you use will, before long, resemble the choice of which electric utility to buy from: a procurement decision that can be switched. What will continue to create differentiation is how roles, authority, workflows, and evaluation loops are designed and run—in other words, the upper layer that bundles the components together.

The practical implication can be reduced to one point. Treat the components—models, chips, access—as commodities, aggressively, and keep them swappable. Then focus investment and attention on the layer that bundles them together: the design of division of labor, the patterns for running them, the orchestration. Use what gets cheap as cheaply as possible, and make money from how you bundle it. In the end, that is the single posture all three L2 pieces were expressing in different words.
