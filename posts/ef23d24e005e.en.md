## Introduction

Three announcements from the past week look unrelated. The U.S. Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) issued its first AI guidance for bank examiners and declined to make AI "its own risk category." The AI-safety nonprofit METR assessed that Anthropic's models are improving about 1.9 times faster than before. SAP's "AI Release Highlights Q3 2026" put forward a structure in which an assistant coordinates many agents. They are in fact explained by one principle: the unit by which we judge AI is shifting from the capability of the model itself to where, and how, it is placed.

## Regulators drop the "AI box" and look at use and size of harm

According to "OCC issues its first AI guidance for bank examiners — and declines to make AI a separate risk category," the OCC published on November 4 its first framework for how examiners should assess banks' use of AI. The notable choice is that AI was not carved out as a standalone risk category. Two axes carry the assessment: how AI is used in day-to-day operations, and how large the damage is when the AI is wrong. The same piece is explicit that this is not a binding new rule.

What did the examiners give up? The habit of drawing lines by type of technology, such as "does this bank use AI" or "which model." What they brought in instead is how deeply the AI is embedded in a workflow and how far an error spreads. The object of examination moved from "AI as a thing" to "where the AI sits."

### What non-binding means here

One might ask whether a non-binding framework is enough. This design works as a tool for aligning examiners' eyes without waiting for a rule: it applies existing supervisory yardsticks to how AI is placed. The decision not to create a new risk category is itself a statement of position, namely that AI is measured by placement rather than treated as exceptional.

## Capability measurement is struggling to keep up

"METR assessment: Anthropic model progress about 1.9× faster than before, with evaluation methods struggling to keep pace" shows another side. METR assessed that the pace of Anthropic's capability gains has accelerated to roughly 1.9 times the earlier rate since October 2025. The estimate range is wide, 1.5 to 2.5 times. The acceleration is reportedly not due to growth in training compute, and AI has not yet reached the stage of contributing autonomously and substantially to key training steps. The challenge named in the headline is evaluation methods keeping pace.

The numbers carry a double meaning. One is that capability is rising quickly. The other is that the estimate of that speed spans 1.5 to 2.5 times. The measurement is less firm than the point estimate of "about 1.9" suggests. If ranking and fixing absolute levels of capability is hard, capability figures are a fragile sole basis for safety or trust.

### Implication of the cause not being compute

That the cause is not growth in training compute suggests capability gains are no longer a matter of the model's scale alone. If what drives capability is spreading to how the model is built and used around its edges, an evaluation that measures only the model captures only part of what is happening. Here the limits of capability evaluation and the OCC's shift toward placement fall on the same line.

## Enterprise software now sells placement as product structure

According to "SAP's Q3 AI release shows a new structure for business software: assistants coordinating agents," SAP stated in its October 26, 2026 release that 45,000 cloud customers use SAP's AI solutions. The centre of the third quarter is a structure in which a Joule assistant per business domain coordinates many specialised agents, which SAP positions as a step toward the "Autonomous Enterprise."

What makes this structure interesting is that it presents AI's value not as the intelligence of an individual model but as a bundle organised by business domain. Single-agent performance is not the selling point; a design close to an org chart, which domain's assistant oversees what, is the face of the product. The same piece points out that every effect figure is either SAP's expectation ("見込み") or a customer's self-report. The placement design is spoken of, but no third-party measurement of its results is yet shown.

## The common principle: why the unit moves

Lining the three facts up again:

- The OCC does not make AI a separate category; it looks at use and size of harm ("OCC issues its first AI guidance for bank examiners").
- In METR's assessment, capability acceleration is about 1.9 times but the range is 1.5 to 2.5 times, and evaluation methods struggle to keep pace ("METR assessment").
- SAP foregrounds domain assistants coordinating agents, while effect figures remain expectation or self-report ("SAP's Q3 AI release").
These facts suggest the following principle. **When model capability moves fast and its measurement carries a wide margin, capability becomes an unstable unit to which responsibility and trust can be tied; that role passes to placement: where the AI sits, with what authority, and how much an error there would hurt.**

The Why has two parts. First, capability is a moving target. If, as METR shows, even the speed of improvement can only be stated within 1.5 to 2.5 times, an approval based on today's capability evaluation is stale tomorrow. Second, placement is observable. Where AI is embedded in operations and how large the damage from an error would be can be checked without knowing the model's internals. That is exactly what the OCC's examiners look at.

The So What: organisations adopting AI must be able to explain where they place it before which model they use. A vendor like SAP that can describe placement as product structure has a foundation for that explanation. But having a foundation is not the same as showing results on top of it, and the wall the SAP piece points to, expectation and self-report, is what placement-first vendors must clear next.

The principle can be disproved. If, when a placement is kept the same and a more capable model is swapped in, the assessment of the size of harm changes fundamentally, then placement is not a unit independent of capability. In that case the move to placement would not replace capability evaluation, and both would have to be measured at once.

## Predictions and implications

Several changes follow from the principle.

**1. Regulation and supervision will lean toward yardsticks by use and by impact (confidence: fairly high).** The OCC's decision not to make AI its own category marks a turn away from creating a new category for each new technology. The ground is that category updates cannot keep up with how fast capability moves. Since the framework is non-binding, nothing firm can be said until we see how it is applied in actual examinations.

**2. Capability evaluation will shift from a single metric to practice that includes the evaluation method's own catch-up (confidence: moderate).** Because the METR piece names evaluation methods keeping pace as a challenge, evaluators are likely to report how far a score can be relied upon alongside the score itself. The 1.5-to-2.5-times range is an early sign.

**3. Competition in business software will move from "whose agent is smarter" to "whose placement design is verifiable" (confidence: moderate).** The SAP piece shows placement becoming the face of the product while effects remain expectation and self-report. If you sell placement, making its results measurable by a third party is the next differentiator.

Three practical takeaways for readers. When explaining an AI deployment, write where it sits in the workflow and how you cap the damage of an error before you name the model. When reading a vendor's effect figure, distinguish expectation, self-report and third-party measurement. And attach a review date to any approval that rests on a capability number. In a period when the unit of measurement is moving, what matters is less that something was measured than what it was measured in.
