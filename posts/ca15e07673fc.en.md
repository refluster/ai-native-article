## Executive Summary

The June 2026 edition of the Anthropic Economic Index, “Cadences,” links Claude usage logs with self-reported survey responses from about 9,700 people and shows that AI use has a structured “cadence” shaped by time of day, day of week, and occupation. Its biggest finding is the automation paradox. The higher the share of work users delegated to AI, the less pessimistic they were about the impact on their own jobs the following year; if anything, they were more optimistic. People’s self-reported sense of AI capability was also consistently higher than the “observed exposure” captured in usage data.

## Study Design: Linking Usage Logs and Self-Reports Across 9,700 People

Previous editions of the Economic Index relied on classifying usage logs. What is new this time is that the survey’s self-reported responses were tied to actual usage behavior. Logs tell you “what was used,” but not “how people felt” about it.

- “The final linked sample consists of about 9,700 survey respondents (about 9,700 survey respondents).”
- The classifier determined that “93% of Claude conversations produced artifacts.”
- The top artifact categories were “explanations (17% of conversations), documents and reports (15%), and guidance (11%).”
- The occupational composition of respondents diverges from U.S. employment overall. Computer/Mathematical accounts for 30% of respondents (versus 4% in U.S. employment), and Management 23% (versus 7%), while transportation, food service, and construction are underrepresented.
Conclusion: This skewed composition means the report’s figures represent a cross-section of people who already use AI. They should be read not as a picture of the full population, but as data on the psychology and behavior of early adopters.

## “Cadences”: Usage Moves With the Clock and the Calendar

As the report’s title suggests, usage is not evenly distributed. It reflects the cycles of life and work.

- The share of personal conversations rises from about 35% on weekdays to about 50% on weekends.
- Recipe-related requests are 2.3 times the average at 6 p.m.
- “On April 14, tax-related clusters were eight times as common as on an average day in May.”
- Autonomy differs by product surface. Claude Code has autonomy that is on average “0.37 points” higher. 54% of Claude Code conversations were handled by Opus, compared with 10% for chat and Cowork (“54% ... against 10%”).
- Use cases are split differently between work and personal contexts. Marketing and content creation is 80% work-related, blog and article writing is 81% work-related, and database queries are 82% work-related. By contrast, more than 80% of creative writing is for personal use.
- Token consumption also has a wage gradient. High-wage marketing managers consume about 2.5 times as many tokens as editors, apps and websites use 3 times the median conversation, and explanations use about one-fifth as much. 44% of the wage gradient in token consumption is explained by output mix.
Conclusion: AI is no longer a one-off tool. It is becoming an ambient layer woven into the rhythms of life, work, and the calendar. If use cases switch by weekday and hour, and the types of artifacts produced are tied to wages, then product design and evaluation based on a single “average use case” will miss how AI is actually being used.

## The Automation Paradox: The More People Delegate, the More Optimistic They Become

The most counterintuitive finding is the relationship between the degree of automation and expectations for the future.

- “People with a higher share of automated sessions feel more optimistic about AI’s impact on their own job outcomes over the next year.”
- Looking ahead 12 months, 86% reported improvements in speed, 82% in scope, and 69% in quality (86%, 82%, and 69%, respectively).
- 57% said “my skills will become more valuable.”
- At the same time, self-reported exposure consistently exceeds observed exposure. About 60% expect task automation to increase over the next 12 months.
Conclusion: The simple equation of automation = job insecurity does not, at least among early adopters, match lived experience. Those who have delegated more appear to be experiencing expansion rather than loss. That said, this is not a causal claim; it remains possible that more optimistic people are simply more likely to delegate in the first place.

## Who Uses It, and Who Fears It

There are demographic gradients in both adoption and anxiety.

- Women are “just 12% of the linked respondent sample.” Their automation rate is “0.33 standard deviations lower (7.3 percentage points),” while their usage is more collaborative, repetitive, and associated with longer chat times.
- Respondents with more than 15 years of experience report perceived exposure about 10 points lower than those early in their careers.
- Only 10% rated their own job loss as “likely/very likely.” By contrast, about 37% rated the probability of job loss for junior or younger colleagues at 60% or higher. People tend to worry more about others’ jobs than their own.
- An income paradox also appears. High-income countries rate AI’s task performance ability about 10 points lower, while low-income countries rate it more highly. The IMF has pointed to shortages of complementary skills and infrastructure in low-income economies.
Conclusion: Levels of AI adoption, more autonomous styles of use, and anxiety about the future are unevenly distributed by gender and years of experience. If company-wide rollout is treated as “uniform exposure,” it will miss the difference between users who work collaboratively with AI and those who delegate to it, as well as the anxiety concentrated among early-career workers. The gap between perceived and observed exposure is itself the next metric that needs to be measured.
