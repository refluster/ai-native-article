Will OpenAI's annualized revenue reach $70 billion, or fall about $20 billion short? Were three safety researchers fired for voicing safety concerns, or for violating a policy on handling sensitive information? When a long-running AI agent is entrusted with a customer relationship for months, what counts as the "outcome"? The three topics look unrelated, but one principle explains all of them. What is being contested is not the facts themselves but the right to name those facts and to decide how they are counted: ownership of the definition.

## One number, two values: the annualized revenue dispute

The L2 explainer "OpenAI's annualized revenue: $70 billion or $50 billion?" describes what looks like a fight over a number and is not quite one. The Financial Times reported that OpenAI would end 2026 about $20 billion short of $70 billion in annualized revenue, and tech stocks fell sharply. That shortfall is a little under 30% of the $70 billion figure. Bloomberg, citing anonymous sources, countered that OpenAI would reach $70 billion or more, and described the lower figure as "merely a forecast."

What stands out is that the article attributes part of the discrepancy to differences in how revenue is calculated. The two outlets may not be looking at different facts; they may be counting the same business activity with different rulers. It is also telling that analysts pointed to strong demand as an indicator of industry health. The market chose its story by choosing a definition before it could verify which number was right, and the share price moved accordingly.

## One dismissal, two reasons: "serious breach of trust" versus "raising concerns"

The L2 explainer on OpenAI's account of the three dismissals makes the structure sharper. On October 9, 2026, OpenAI explained that the three safety researchers were let go not for raising safety concerns but for a "serious breach of trust" through violating its policy on handling sensitive information. The day before, the three had written to the board and the safety committee, arguing that the company's handling of the dismissals was chilling former colleagues' speech. The article frames the dispute as narrowing to the real reason for the dismissals.

The two sides are not disputing everything about what happened. That the three were dismissed, and that raising concerns and handling information are both entangled in the story, is common ground. What splits them is whether to call the same event retaliation for raising concerns or discipline for a policy violation. Which side is right is outside the scope of this piece, but the side that issues its official account first does set the default label.

## Pay-for-outcome models need the definition of "outcome" first

In the L2 explainer "Long-running agents win the relay race," a16z's David Haber argues that AI agents have moved beyond one-off tasks to where they can carry customer journeys lasting weeks or months. The Red Ventures model, which owned the entire journey and was paid on outcomes, is structurally threatened, and he sketches its AI-native successor in three go-to-market forms: platform sales, service sales, and full stack.

This argument seems to sit on a different layer from the first two. But a contract that pays for outcomes cannot exist without settling who counts the outcome and how. When an agent has guided a customer for months, at what point and in what form is the outcome recognized, and how much of the contribution is credited to the agent? Whichever of the three forms is chosen, that question gets written into the contract somewhere. The moment an outcome is defined, the line between payment and responsibility is drawn there.

## The common principle: what to call it is settled before what happened

These facts suggest one structure. When the facts are complex, the parties many, and money, treatment or compensation is at stake, the dispute shifts from what happened to what to call it and how to count it.

Across the three cases the shift takes three forms. In revenue, there are several rulers, and depending on the ruler the figure can be $70 billion or $50 billion. In the dismissals, the same event carries two labels: raising concerns and policy violation. In outcome-based pay, naming an outcome that has not yet occurred becomes the core of the contract. In each, checking the facts means something only after the definition is fixed.

From this follows the principle: **whoever writes the definition holds the baseline for judgment.** This is not merely a story about manipulating information. Definitions are indispensable for compressing a complicated reality into something that can be judged, and someone always writes them. The problem is that the writer is often a party with a stake, and a written definition easily circulates as an invisible premise. The revenue method hides beneath a headline, the dismissal label is buried in a statement, and the definition of an outcome sits in a footnote of a price list.

The three cases share one more feature: definitions diverge even when nobody is lying. Multiple revenue methods are an accounting reality; the entanglement of concerns and information handling reflects the complexity of the event; the difficulty of separating contributions is a property of a long journey. That is why simply demanding "show the correct number" or "state the true reason" does not end the dispute. Unless there is agreement on which definition is used, the same facts can yield different conclusions indefinitely. Conversely, parties who share a definition can move quickly to the real work of verifying facts.

The principle is falsifiable. If most disputes arose from simple factual error rather than definition, publishing definitions would not reduce them. And if disputes arose just as rarely in areas where definitions are not published, the principle would be wrong. The claim here is a contestable proposition: the higher the stakes in a field, the more the tug-of-war over who owns the definition becomes the center of the dispute.

## Implications and forecasts: the first disputes over outcome pricing will be about definitions, not performance

Several changes follow. First, if outcome-based pricing for AI agents spreads, the first disputes to surface will probably concern the definition of the outcome rather than shortfalls in performance, because contributions across a months-long customer journey are inherently hard to separate. My estimate is roughly 60% that, over the next twelve months, a majority of publicly reported disputes over outcome-priced agent contracts will be definition-driven. The ground is that precedents such as Red Ventures were premised on owning the entire journey.

Second, key metrics of AI companies will increasingly be expected to come with their calculation methods. If even part of the gap between the FT and Bloomberg stems from method, the next lesson for the market is to read the definition before the number. Whether disclosure actually advances is a separate matter; I put the probability below even, since companies retain an incentive to keep definitions vague.

Third, internal organizational disputes will also feature a fight over how to name events before any investigation of facts. The exchange of a letter and a rebuttal over the three dismissals is a miniature of this.

Fourth, the longer the work an agent carries extends, from weeks to months, the more what humans can check is a summary against a definition rather than the final result. The long-running agents a16z describes create value on the premise that humans cannot follow the whole journey step by step. What humans then effectively hold is the definition of success they give the agent and the form in which results must be reported. Designing the definition becomes the central means of supervision.

There are two practical takeaways. When you receive a report of a number, a disciplinary action or an outcome, first ask who wrote the definition. And if you are on the side writing a contract or a policy, write the definition and share it with the other party before a dispute arises, not after. Because whoever writes the definition first holds the baseline, objecting later costs far more than writing it first.
