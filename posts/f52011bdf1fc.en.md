In October 2026 three apparently unrelated events landed side by side. Microsoft Chairman and CEO Satya Nadella proposed treating frontier models as "insider risks". McDonald's was hit with a proposed nationwide class action in a Chicago federal court, alleging that an AI pricing system aligned prices across company-owned and franchised stores in violation of antitrust law. And Samsung Electronics and TSMC posted record figures in the same week on AI chip demand. They can in fact be explained by one principle: the more the supply of intelligence concentrates in a few nodes, the less the contest is about performance and the more it is about who is allowed to do what with that intelligence.

## First fact: a design that does not trust the model (Nadella)

According to the L2 article "Designing Not to Trust the Smart Model: Nadella's 'Models as Insider Risks' and the Separation of Intelligence Supply from Authority", Nadella argued in an essay posted on X on 10 October 2026, "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era", that frontier models should be treated as insider risks. The core of the proposal is to separate the "supply of intelligence" from the "authority over it", and to place the controls that decide what a model can access and execute outside the model.

The closing line matters. The most trustworthy superintelligent system is not "the system with the model it trusts most" but "the system that needs to trust the model least". The source of trust moves from how smart the intelligence is to the authority design that surrounds it. However capable the model becomes, the right to use that capability is managed separately.

## Second fact: does a shared price recommendation align independent decisions? (McDonald's)

The L2 article "McDonald's Sued: Is an AI Pricing Tool 'Price Coordination Among Franchises'?" describes a case whose central question is whether a central price recommendation, built on non-public data, in effect aligns decisions that are supposed to be independent. The plaintiffs allege that prices at company-owned and franchised stores were coordinated through an AI pricing system, in violation of antitrust law. The case is a proposed nationwide class action, and the company is contesting it in full.

What is at issue is not whether the AI's recommendation is accurate. It is where authority sits when one intelligence in one place produces recommendations that many independent parties follow. The more intelligence supply gathers at the centre, the more the premise of each store's "independent judgment" wavers. This is the same structure as Nadella's argument, restated in the language of law.

## Third fact: the supply layers set records together, and the market asks if it lasts (Samsung, TSMC)

According to the L2 article "Samsung Operating Profit KRW 107.4 Trillion, TSMC Quarterly Revenue NT$1.49 Trillion: 8 October, When AI Chip Demand Broke Records Together", Samsung Electronics estimated its third-quarter 2026 operating profit at KRW 107.4 trillion (USD 80.2 billion), up 782.5% year on year. On the same day TSMC reported September revenue of NT$511.86 billion, up 54.6% year on year. Memory and foundry, two layers of the AI supply chain, hit record highs in the same week.

Yet Samsung shares fell 2.42% that day. The market had already begun to ask whether the demand will last. The larger the supply-side figures grow, the more attention shifts to the side that sustains the demand, that is, to the design of who may use the intelligence and at what price. Record profits are also the flip side of supply being gathered into a few layers.

## A side note: why the three do not look like one principle

The three facts sit at different layers. Nadella's essay is a design proposal, the McDonald's case is a dispute after deployment, and the Samsung and TSMC numbers are results from the supply chain. Because the layers differ, they are normally read as separate stories. But ask what is concentrated and the answer takes the same shape: intelligence itself for models, a recommendation built on non-public data for the pricing tool, and the compute that runs intelligence for chips. Whatever is concentrated, the consequence is the same: it becomes unclear who holds authority. Nadella's choice of a personnel term, "insider risk", can be read as a way to separate the authority question from the performance debate.

## The common principle: concentration of supply forces a separation of supply and authority

Set side by side, the three facts follow the same order. First, the supply of intelligence, or the compute that runs it, gathers into a few nodes (frontier models, a central pricing recommendation system, memory and foundry). Second, the concentration creates value; the record figures at Samsung and TSMC are evidence of its size. Third, the larger the value, the more the question "who may make this intelligence do what" comes to the surface.

Nadella's proposal answers that question by design: put authority outside the model and build a structure that does not need to trust it. The McDonald's suit shows what happens when the same question enters operation undesigned: a court comes to decide where authority sits. Samsung's share-price fall shows that when concentration is discussed, the market starts to measure its durability from the side of authority and demand.

This view runs against the naive expectation that "the smarter the model, the safer". The most trustworthy system in Nadella's account is one that works even if the model is not trusted. However smart the intelligence becomes, the separate job of designing authority does not shrink. As supply concentrates and one node's output steers many decisions, that job gets heavier. The McDonald's plaintiffs are asking about exactly this growth.

These facts suggest one thing: the axis of AI competition is shifting from the intelligence itself to the design of where authority over it is placed. Supply-side intelligence can be piled up, but authority is decided by the demand side and by institutions. So the more concentration advances, the more the issue becomes authority rather than performance.

The principle can be refuted. If intelligence supply concentrates and the design of authority still does not become an issue, with no growth in lawsuits or control debates, the principle is wrong. And if authority design does become an issue but has no effect at all on the record results of the supply side, then "concentration invites the authority problem" is economically meaningless.

## Outlook and implications

Three changes follow from this principle. First, legal inquiries into companies that use central recommendations or automated decisions will increase. The McDonald's suit is a proposed class action and its outcome is unsettled, but the likelihood that the same question reaches price tools in other industries is high, because the structure of independent parties following a shared intelligence is not limited to pricing. We put the probability at moderate.

Second, controls placed outside the model, that is, mechanisms that fix the range of access and execution, will carry as much weight in procurement as model selection. Having said plainly that authority belongs outside the intelligence, Nadella makes it likely that large buyers' criteria move in the same direction. But the 10 October essay is a proposal, and there is no evidence yet that it has become a standard.

Third, record supply-side figures and market doubts about durability will coexist for some time. Samsung shares falling on a day of record profit is the first sign of this tension. Whether numbers can rise while valuations do not depends on whether demand-side authority design catches up.

The practical takeaway is simple. Organisations adopting AI should spend part of the time they give to comparing model performance on writing down what the model may and may not be permitted to do. It is cheaper to write the authority design yourself than to have a court or the market ask for it after deployment. To restate the principle in one sentence: the more the supply of intelligence concentrates, the more the issue moves from how smart it is to who holds authority over it.
