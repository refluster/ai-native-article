## Designers’ AI Tool Stack Reorganized: 91% Usage, 7 Tools on Average, Claude Overtakes ChatGPT

## Executive Summary

In design practice in 2026, AI has moved beyond the experimental stage and is now embedded across the full process, from design through implementation and handoff. According to the source, 91% of designers use AI at least once a week, the average number of tools has increased from 3 in 2025 to 7, and Claude has overtaken ChatGPT, at 78% versus 65%.

At the same time, even as the performance of individual tools improves, the standard toolset has actually become less stable. The source shows a design tool stack in flux, shaped by Figma’s changing role, the centralization of coding tools, and the growth of internal and self-built tools.

## AI Use Is Moving From “Part of the Process” to “the Entire Workflow”

Brief summary: The central shift in the source is not simply a sharp rise in AI usage frequency, but an expansion in the scope of use. In 2025, usage leaned more toward exploration and creation; by 2026, it had spread across the full workflow, including delivery.

- Evidence
- “91% of respondents now use AI in their design work at least weekly, up from 54% in 2025—a 37-point jump year over year.”
- “75% of surveyed designers use it daily.”
- Breakdown of usage frequency:
- Daily (75%)
- Several times a week (16%)
- Once a week (4%)
- A few times a month (3%)
- Rarely or almost never (2%)
- The source notes that in 2025, “Only 39% used it in delivery.” But in 2026, it says that “every workflow we asked about shows meaningful AI adoption.”
- It identifies “ideation, prototyping, and UI copy” as major use cases.
- The following areas are explicitly identified as showing large year-over-year changes:
- Code generation and front-end implementation +31pts
- Wireframing +27pts
- Design systems and components +24pts
- UI copy and content writing +18pts
- Design QA and accessibility +16pts
- Developer handoff +12pts
- User research and synthesis +9pts
- Image or video generation +9pts
- Stakeholder presentations -4pts
- “50% of survey respondents say they’ve shipped code to production”
- Conclusion
- AI is no longer being treated as a supplementary ideation aid, but as an operational foundation used continuously across production, validation, and handoff.
- The fastest-growing areas in particular are “code generation,” “design QA,” and “developer handoff,” which have traditionally been treated as peripheral to design, suggesting that the designer’s scope is moving closer to implementation.
- The fact that “50% ... shipped code to production” shows that the endpoint of designers’ output now extends beyond mocks and spec documents to production code.
## The Average Number of Tools Has Grown From 3 to 7, and the Standard Stack Is Breaking Down

Brief summary: The source portrays the advance of AI adoption not as straightforward consolidation, but rather as dispersion. Tool usage has increased, but it is not converging around a standard set.

- Evidence
- “The average toolstack has more than doubled, from 3 tools to 7.”
- “Designers we surveyed now use an average of 7 off-the-shelf AI tools, up from 3 last year”
- This trend appears “across respondents from different roles, company sizes, and geographies”
- The source further states that “this tally doesn’t take into account the full number of bespoke tools they build for themselves or internally built tools their company provides.”
- It also says, “We saw a major rise in these ‘internal’ company tools”
- At the start of the chapter, it notes that “Nearly half of designers say they're still searching for their go-to tools.”
- In the same context, it adds that “it’s easier to build custom software, making the stack much more fluid than we’ve ever seen it”
- It then raises the question: “Will we ever go back to a ‘standard’ design toolset?”
- Conclusion
- The increase in the number of tools is less a sign of maturity than a sign of continued role differentiation and experimentation.
- Since the figure of 7 on average includes only commercial tools and excludes internal or self-built tools, the actual operating stack is even more fragmented.
- The observation that many designers are still looking for their “go-to tools” suggests that the AI design environment in 2026 is defined more by variability than by standardization.
## Claude Has Taken the Lead in General-Purpose AI, and Coding Tools Have Moved to the Core of Design

Brief summary: The power shift described by the source is not just a reshuffling of popularity rankings. The handoff in general-purpose AI leadership and the spread of coding tools are happening at the same time, changing the center of gravity of the design process itself.

- Evidence
- “78% of respondents use Claude, compared to 65% for ChatGPT, which led in 2025.”
- “Gemini comes in third at 48%”
- “Perplexity has dropped from 34% to 13%”
- “65% of overall respondents are using Claude Code, which didn’t even make it onto our 2025 survey because it hadn’t launched to the public yet.”
- “In 2026, 76% of all respondents say they’ve used an AI coding tool like Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Cursor, or GitHub Copilot.”
- “85% have used these and/or an app builder like Lovable, Replit, or Bolt.”
- The source also includes a disclosure:
- “Disclosure: Anthropic, the creator of Claude, is a partner of the AI in Design 2026 survey. The survey was conducted independently of our partners.”
- Conclusion
- In addition to the shift in general-purpose AI leadership from ChatGPT to Claude, the rapid adoption of Claude Code is blurring the line between conversational AI and implementation AI.
- In a landscape where “76%” use an AI coding tool and “85%” use those and/or an app builder, coding assistance is no longer a peripheral feature, but is moving toward being a standard designer behavior.
- That said, when reading figures related to Claude, they should be considered alongside the partner disclosure provided by the source itself.
## Figma Remains Central, but Its Role Is Splitting From “Starting Point” to “Scalpel”

Brief summary: The source does not argue that Figma is fading. Rather, it shows that while Figma remains the most-used tool, its role is no longer singular.

- Evidence
- “According to the UX Tools State of Prototyping survey, Figma remains the most-used design tool in 2026. But how designers use it may be changing.”
- Some designers use Figma as the starting point for AI-enabled work.
- “They explore multiple directions in the canvas before going deep in code”
- “they create wireframes in Figma and pass them to AI”
- “it’s still the best tool for team collaboration”
- In another mode of use, Figma has become a “scalpel” or finishing tool.
- “They prototype in a dev environment, then flip back to Figma for high-fidelity execution or precise tuning like perfecting radii and padding.”
- Code-first workflows are also emerging.
- “At Maze, designers work in Cursor from day zero.”
- “At Watershed, the design team has fully transitioned to working directly in the codebase.”
- Direct quotes:
- Phil Vander Broek, Head of Design, AI, Superhuman
- “Figma has shifted from being the primary design tool to a canvas for quick exploration and polishing details as input for Claude Code.”
- Mark Boyes-Smith, Head of AI Design, Miro
- “Traditional prototyping took two weeks on average. Now, using vibe-coded prototypes, it's completed in hours.”
- “We use Figma as a scalpel now—pull an area out for specific cuts and push it back into the coded prototype.”
- Shali Nguyen, Head of Consumer Experience Design, DoorDash
- “Fine grain control is the biggest gap. It's the tuning. It's the 'I know what I want in my head, but you're just not giving me what I want.´ So I need to go into Figma and tell you what I want and then put that back.”
- Conclusion
- Figma is no longer “the only center,” but a flexible node that handles exploration, collaboration, and fine-tuning.
- As AI-first workflows spread, rough concept generation and implementation-oriented prototyping are moving into coding environments, while Figma is increasingly reintroduced for high-fidelity execution and control of details.
- At the same time, as the examples of Maze and Watershed suggest, time spent in Figma itself is falling sharply in some organizations.
## The Real-World Examples Point to the Value of Connected Workflows, Not Standalone Tools

Brief summary: The case studies in the source show that results are coming not from individual AI features alone, but from workflows that connect multiple tools and internal agents.

- Evidence
- Typography audit
- “Joey Banks, founder of Baseline Design, combined Claude, the Figma MCP Server, Claude Design, and Claude Code in one morning to audit typography across a large component library, consolidate styles, and prototype a token-reference site.”
- Research queries
- “At Abridge, an AI platform for clinicians, any designer or PM can query research directly in Slack, says Anisha Jain, their VP of Design.”
- “An agent pulls from the repository, returns data-backed answers, and suggests next studies.”
- Marketing animation
- “Independent designer Hunter Thompson used Flora AI and Claude Code to generate a custom animation in an afternoon that previously wouldn't have been worth the effort: ‘This type of interaction would have taken days and would never have been justified.’”
- Quote from an anonymous respondent:
- “AI collapsed the distance between idea and execution. I can now own and drive projects end-to-end at a scale that would've required a full team before. The experimentation ceiling basically disappeared.”
- Attribute: “Individual contributor”“Growth-stage company”
- Conclusion
- The unit of productivity gain lies not in the intelligence of a single model, but in the operational design that connects design assets, repositories, Slack, and coding environments.
- The examples of typography auditing, research queries, and marketing animation show that work which previously would have been hard to justify because of time and labor costs can now be completed quickly enough to become worth doing.
- As the distance between “idea and execution” shrinks, the range of projects that one individual can carry has expanded.
