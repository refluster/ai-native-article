What OpenAI emphasized in the general release of GPT-5.6 was not absolute performance, but “results per token” and “results per dollar.” In the same week, the White House requested a limited release of that GPT-5.6, creating a precedent in which the government approves access on a customer-by-customer basis. And the U.S. government also lifted export restrictions on Anthropic’s Mythos and Fable without any meaningful new safety measures. These three developments may look unrelated, but they can in fact be explained by the same principle: in frontier AI, what is scarce now is no longer capability itself, but “who gets to run it” and “how much it costs to run.”

## The Three Facts Show Competition Happening Somewhere Other Than Capability

What “OpenAI Begins General Availability of GPT-5.6” tells us is that the contest is not over absolute performance, but efficiency. The company says its flagship “Sol” “outperforms competing frontier models with fewer tokens and at lower cost,” while laying out a three-tier lineup with “Terra” for everyday work and “Luna” as the lowest-cost option. The decisive move was making a new setting, “ultra,” which runs multiple agents in parallel by default, into a central axis of product differentiation. What is being discussed here is not “how smart is it,” but “how cheaply and quickly can it produce the same result”—in other words, unit cost and execution efficiency. The key point is not a claim to the peak of performance, but a shift to competition over lowering the cost per unit once near-peak performance is taken for granted.

By contrast, what “The White House Requests a Limited Release of OpenAI’s New Model GPT-5.6” shows is control over distribution rather than capability. During the preview period, the government would “approve access on a customer-by-customer basis,” and both the Office of the National Cyber Director and the Office of Science and Technology Policy called for the limited release. This marks a turning point: an administration that initially championed a “hands-off” approach, following the June 2 executive order, has now begun intervening in the very decision of whether frontier models should be made public. The point at issue is no longer “can the model be built,” but “into whose hands does it go.” And the core of this fact lies in the form of intervention: not banning capability itself, but selecting recipients of distribution.

### “Safety” as the Name for What Is Actually a Negotiation Over Distribution

The third development, “U.S. Government Lifts Export Restrictions on Anthropic’s Mythos and Fable,” is the reverse side of the same control over distribution. The U.S. government had added Mythos and Fable to the export control list on June 12, but then allowed general availability to resume from July 1. Yet what Anthropic promised in return was almost identical to the safety measures it had already been voluntarily pledging before the restrictions. As multiple cybersecurity experts have pointed out, this was not a substantive strengthening of “safety measures,” but a regulation that functioned as political leverage. And the direct trigger for the easing was competitive pressure: the rise of Asian models approaching Mythos-class capability. A regulation meant to contain capability was neutralized—and then undone—by improvements in others’ capabilities. That causal chain gets to the heart of this essay before the argument is even fully made.

## When Capability Saturates, the Battleground Shifts to “Access” and “Cost Basis”

What these three facts have in common is the premise that superiority in capability is no longer the main battleground. GPT-5.6 is being sold not on “beating competitors,” but on “beating them more cheaply” (“GPT-5.6 Begins General Availability”); the government is not stopping models, but selecting who receives them (“Requests Limited Release”); and export controls failed to contain capability and were lifted in the face of rising Asian competition (“Mythos and Fable Restrictions Lifted”). None of these are being settled by “a smarter model.” On the contrary, it is precisely because differences in capability among top-tier models no longer determine outcomes that each actor is moving to another axis—price and distribution.

These facts suggest that frontier capability is beginning to be commoditized, at least among the top players. If capability were scarce, companies would still be clubbing each other with performance benchmarks, and governments could preserve their advantage simply by blocking capability exports. But in reality, OpenAI is differentiating on unit cost rather than performance, and export controls were rendered ineffective once Asian competitors closed in on the same level. That gives us one step of induction: when three independent developments are all being settled on dimensions other than capability, it suggests that capability itself is ceasing to be the determining variable in competition and governance. In a world where capability is starting to become abundant, what determines value is no longer “smartness,” but who gets to use that smartness (distribution and access control) and how much it costs to run one unit of it (unit cost and execution efficiency). That is the common principle running through all three events.

At first glance, GPT-5.6’s unit-cost competition—a business story—and government access approvals and export controls—a governance story—may look like different layers. But they are two sides of the same premise. Unit cost becomes the competitive axis because capability has flattened out and “smartness” no longer creates meaningful separation; governments shift to selecting recipients because capability embargoes stop working once that same flattening takes hold—especially with Asian challengers catching up. In other words, a single tectonic shift—the commoditization of capability—appears in one form for companies as pressure to reduce cost basis, and in another form for governments as pressure to tighten the gate. That is why these three developments, though they seem unrelated, actually share the same root. Put differently, if capability were to become scarce again, then both unit-cost competition and approval-based distribution would recede at the same time. That symmetry is itself evidence that this common principle is not just a story imposed after the fact.

## “Who Gets It” and “At What Cost” Will Decide the Next Phase of Competition and Governance

If this principle is correct, then competition and regulation will proceed along two axes.

First, competition among firms is highly likely to converge on a battle over unit cost and execution efficiency. As symbolized by GPT-5.6’s “ultra” parallel execution, the contest is shifting away from the quality of a single response and toward how much output can be stacked in parallel within the same budget. The falsification condition for this claim is clear: if the next generation brings another discontinuous leap in capability, and the performance gap between top-tier models widens enough to make unit-cost differences irrelevant, then the axis of competition will swing back to capability. But the fact that the major companies’ current messaging centers on “cheaper, faster, more parallel” suggests that such falsification is unlikely in the near term. Once unit cost becomes the main battleground, model providers’ advantage will be determined by the efficiency of their inference infrastructure—chips, parallel execution, token design—and competition will quietly descend into the infrastructure layer.

Second, the focus of governance will shift from regulating capability to controlling distribution. Rather than preventing models from being built or stopping them outright, governments will hold onto approval power over who receives them—the White House’s customer-by-customer approvals and the lifting of export controls that had lost their effectiveness are two sides of the same phenomenon. Once competitive pressure has made capability containment unworkable, the remaining governance lever is selective access. As Anthropic’s Project Glasswing (Claude Mythos) already suggested through its limited rollout, “limited release” may become not the exception but the default mode of distribution. If capability can no longer be used to stop other countries, then the only remaining option is to control who gets access at home. That logic of elimination is what may normalize approval-based distribution.

The practical implication for readers is this. Competitive advantage for those using frontier models will no longer be determined by whether they can access “the smartest model.” Assuming multiple providers can supply roughly equivalent capability, advantage will be decided by two things: (1) how effectively a company can redesign its workflows around unit cost and parallel execution, and (2) whether it can secure the access it needs under approval-based and limited-distribution regimes. Procuring capability will become easier within a few years. What will become harder is designing systems that run that capability cheaply—and negotiating to be allowed to receive it. That is what it means for scarcity to shift away from capability and toward distribution and unit cost.
