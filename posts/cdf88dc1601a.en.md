In 2026, three numbers around AI appeared at the same time. Claude now writes more than 80% of the code merged into production, designers’ AI adoption has reached 91% with an average of 7 tools per person, and Google has remade Gemini into an assistant built to “organize your day.” At first glance these seem unrelated, but in fact they can be explained by the same principle: the human work that creates value has shifted from personally “executing” tasks to “orchestrating” — selecting, combining, and directing the outputs of multiple AIs.

## From People Who “Write” Code to People Who “Direct” It

In the article “When AI builds itself,” Marina Favaro and Jack Clark of the Anthropic Institute published internal company data. In Q2 2026, code output per engineer reached 8 times the 2024 level, and Claude wrote more than 80% of the code merged into production.

What is happening here is more than simply “things got faster.” The reason one person can produce 8 times as much is that the person is no longer typing code line by line. Human work has shifted from writing code to deciding what to have written, which outputs to accept, and what to have revised. Claude is now the primary agent of execution, while the human has become an orchestrator who controls direction and acceptance. The authors say AI has not yet reached — and is not fated inevitably to reach — “recursive self-improvement,” in which it fully designs its own successor autonomously, but they warn that it may arrive sooner than many organizations are prepared for. Turned around, that means that while humans are still the lead actors for now, their role has already finished shifting from “executor” to “orchestrator.”

## Designers Are Conducting “Seven Tools”

The same pattern is appearing in design work, which ought to be far removed from code. According to the State of AI Design survey (“The Reorganization of Designers’ AI Tool Stacks”), 91% of designers use AI tools, they use an average of 7 tools each, and Claude has overtaken ChatGPT to become the top choice. Generative AI has entered every stage of the workflow: ideation, prototyping, and scaled production.

The most striking number here is “an average of 7 tools.” As generative AI has spread into each stage of the process, the designer’s job has stopped being about operating a single tool and become about using different tools at different stages and binding them together. And within that bundle, the leader has already changed — the fact that “Claude has overtaken ChatGPT” shows how easily loyalty to a tool collapses in the face of results. For an executor, switching tools carries major friction. For an orchestrator, swapping a tool is just one move in the orchestration. The fact that the same shift toward “work of bundling things together” is happening in design — a domain entirely separate from code generation — suggests that this is not a domain-specific phenomenon.

## Platforms Are Fighting Over the “Place Where Things Get Bundled”

The third fact is visible from the supply side. At Google I/O 2026, Google reportedly revamped the Gemini app to compete head-on with OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Anthropic’s Claude (“The Gemini App Revamped at Google I/O 2026”). The new features highlighted were “Daily Brief,” “Gemini Spark,” and “Gemini Omni” — none of them just a tool for answering one-off prompts, but all of them aimed at serving as an “orchestrator” that organizes the user’s day and the flow of information itself.

“Daily Brief” is especially symbolic. This is not a tool the user employs to carry something out; it is a function in which AI selects, summarizes, and presents information — in other words, orchestrates on the user’s behalf. What platform companies are now competing over is no longer “the smartest single response,” but who gets to occupy the fixed position that organizes the user’s daily life. On the demand side, in code and design, humans are becoming orchestrators; on the supply side, platforms are beginning to sell the “place of orchestration” itself as the product.

## The Shared Principle: The Point Where Value Is Created Has Moved From “Execution” to “Orchestration”

These three facts — AI writes 80% of code, designers use 7 tools in parallel, and Gemini is competing through features that organize daily life — look on the surface like stories from separate worlds: development, design, and consumer products. But all of them show the same movement: the place where humans (or platforms) create value has shifted from executing tasks directly to “orchestration” — selecting, combining, and directing the outputs of multiple AIs.

The induction runs as follows. In coding, the amount humans write has gone down, while the amount they choose and revise has gone up. In design, productivity is determined less by mastery of one tool than by the ability to deploy 7 tools appropriately, and as a result the top-ranked tool can change hands easily. In consumer products, the condition for winning is shifting from “a good response” to “the fixed position that organizes daily life.” Together, these facts suggest that the source of productivity is moving from individual execution capability to orchestration capability.

There are two important implications. First, for an orchestrator, any individual AI becomes a replaceable component — which is exactly why Claude was able to overtake ChatGPT among designers. Once a tool is chosen not as an instrument of execution but as one move within an orchestration, loyalty becomes fragile in the face of outcomes. Second, for that very reason, the main battleground on the supply side shifts from “the smartest model” to “the fixed position of orchestration (the orchestration layer).” Gemini’s “Daily Brief” can be read as an attempt to claim that position early.

This principle should be stated in a falsifiable form. If, going forward, performance gaps between standalone models become decisively wide, users revert to a single AI, the number of tools used in parallel declines, and turnover in the top-ranked tools stops — then this article’s claim that “orchestration creates value” would be wrong. Conversely, if performance remains closely matched and tool-switching continues to become an everyday behavior, the principle will be supported. The three facts we have now point to the latter, but the issue will only be settled once convergence in performance stops.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, then over the next 12〜24 months the following changes are highly likely.

First, the axis of competition among AI companies will shift clearly from the intelligence of standalone models to “ownership of the orchestration layer.” Gemini’s attempt to compete through functions that organize daily life can be read as the opening shot. The more model performance converges, the more the winner will be the one that controls the user’s fixed position — the IDE, the daily brief, the tool a designer opens first. Confidence in this is high, because designers’ “average of 7 tools” and the turnover at the top already show that standalone performance alone cannot create lock-in.

Second, the more realistic recursive self-improvement becomes, the more the rare human capability that remains will converge on “orchestration and judgment.” As Anthropic warns, if the stage where AI designs its own successor arrives sooner than organizations are prepared for, the scarce resource will not be people who can write code, but people who can decide what to have AI build and which outputs to let into production. In an era when a single engineer can produce 8 times as much, competitive advantage lies not in coding speed but in the quality of orchestration.

There is one practical implication for readers. Reallocate your investment in skills away from “the ability to execute faster” and toward “the ability to select, combine, and direct multiple AIs.” Tools will be swapped out — as Claude overtook ChatGPT in design. What will not be swapped out is the position of being on the side that does the orchestrating.
