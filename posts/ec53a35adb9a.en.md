## Executive Summary

Y Combinator’s latest “Requests for Startups (RFS)” (Summer 2026) opens with a clear declaration: “AI has stopped being a feature and started being the foundation.” It places the rebuilding of software, services, and silicon—as well as AI’s expansion into the physical world—at the center of its call for ideas. Each theme is individually signed by a YC partner, and the collection puts particular weight on software for agents (“Software for Agents”), inference chips optimized for agent workloads, the semiconductor supply chain, and the replacement of service industries themselves. The page explicitly notes that RFS is YC’s tradition of sharing “ideas we'd like to see founders tackle,” and that working on these topics is not a requirement for applying.

## The declaration that “AI has become the foundation” and the structure of the call

The page itself states the role of RFS very plainly.

- Definition: “RFS is our tradition of sharing ideas we'd like to see founders tackle.” It also makes clear that these ideas are only “just a fraction of what we fund,” and that “you don't need to work on these ideas to apply to YC.”
- Lead text for the Summer 2026 edition: “AI has stopped being a feature and started being the foundation. We're excited about a new wave of startups rebuilding software, services, and silicon— and pushing AI into the physical world.”
- Each theme is signed by a YC partner. Contributors include Garry Tan, Jon Xu, Gustaf Alströmer, Ankit Gupta, Tom Blomfield, Tyler Bosmeny, Diana Hu, Jared Friedman, and Aaron Epstein, among others, with Diana Hu signing 3 themes on her own.
This is not an evaluation of specific startups, but YC’s attempt to articulate the frontier it sees. Two ideas recur throughout the Summer 2026 edition: that the moats of incumbent companies have eroded, and that AI is beginning to replace the work itself. The through-line across the whole document is a shift from supplying tools to performing the work on the user’s behalf.

## A cluster of themes about “rebuilding” software and services

The largest cluster is built around the argument that existing software and services should be rethought from first principles.

- **Software for Agents** (Aaron Epstein): “The next trillion users on the internet won't be people, they'll be AI agents.” Agents, the piece argues, need not “forms, buttons, and dashboards” but “machine-readable interfaces like APIs, MCPs, and CLIs,” under the slogan “Make Something Agents Want.”
- **AI-Native Service Companies** (Gustaf Alströmer): “they don't sell software—they sell the service. Instead of giving you a tool, they just do the work.” Areas of interest listed include Insurance brokerage / Accounting, tax, and audit / Compliance / Healthcare administration, with the argument that spending on services is “many times larger” than spending on software.
- **SaaS Challengers** (Jared Friedman): “AI has collapsed the cost of producing software by 10-100x.” Targets worth attacking include “chip design software, ERPs, industrial control systems, supply chain management,” and the piece points to open-source alternatives to products priced at the “$50K per seat” level.
- **Company Brain** (Tom Blomfield): proposes a new layer that structures the tacit knowledge scattered across a company and turns it into “an executable skills file for AI.” “We need Garry's G-Brain, but for every business in the world.”
- **The AI Operating System for Companies** (Diana Hu): argues for making the whole company “queryable” and moving from “open loop” to “closed loop.” It says a connective layer tying together systems such as “Slack, Linear, GitHub, Notion” is missing, and reports that teams already doing this have “cut sprint time in half and ship twice as much.”
- **Dynamic Software Interfaces** (Ankit Gupta): envisions a future in which advances in coding agents turn users themselves into “forward deployed engineers,” allowing interfaces to be individually optimized.
The implication of this cluster is that one of SaaS’s core advantages—the assumption that custom software was prohibitively expensive—has broken down. YC goes so far as to say, “The moat that once protected legacy SaaS — millions of lines of code, built over decades — is gone,” positioning the AI-native opportunity not as “better tools” but as replacing services and the existing stack itself.

## The compute foundation: inference silicon optimized for agent workloads and the semiconductor supply chain

The second cluster turns to the compute foundation that underpins the software above.

- **Inference Chips for Agent Workflows** (Diana Hu): argues that agent workloads are built around a “loop: calling tools, branching, backtracking,” and that “Current GPUs hit 30 to 40 percent of peak utilization on these workloads.” As supporting evidence it cites “NVIDIA bought Groq for $20 billion” and “Google built TPU v7 for inference specifically,” arguing that memory for “KV caches” that persist across the full execution graph is a key bottleneck.
- **Supply Chain 2.0 for Semiconductors** (Diana Hu): “A single advanced AI chip goes through about 1,400 process steps, crosses a dozen countries, and takes five months to build.” “In 2021 a $300 chip held up a $50,000 car, and $210 billion in vehicles didn't get built.” It further states that “TSMC's advanced packaging is the single biggest bottleneck,” that “NVIDIA has locked up over 60 percent of it,” and that “HBM memory is booked through 2026.” New fabs are coming online in “Arizona, Texas, Ohio, and New York” under the CHIPS Act.
- **Electronics in Space** (Philip Johnston): argues that “reusable rockets from SpaceX and Stoke Space” are expanding demand for computing in space. Inference chips for space will need to be optimized for “mass,” “thermal,” and “radiation.”
- **Industrial Capabilities in Space** (Adi Oltean): focuses on extracting “silicon, aluminum, iron, and titanium” through “electrolysis” on the Moon or in space, and on 3D-printing complex structures from “molten regolith.”
The very shape of inference demand is shifting from “prompt in, response out” to the looped execution of agents. That, in turn, forms a chain of themes stretching from silicon design itself—chips and compilers—to the supply chain that supports manufacturing them, including packaging, HBM, domestic fabs, and even space. The fact that Diana Hu signs multiple compute-foundation themes signals just how central this area is within the RFS.

## AI’s expansion into the physical world: agriculture, medicine, defense, and hardware

The third cluster covers domains that connect AI intelligence to physical action.

- **AI for Low-Pesticide Agriculture** (Garry Tan): AI identifies individual weeds and pests in real time, while robots treat them plant by plant. It describes “The company that cuts pesticide use by 90% and helps farmers grow more food” as a “generational company.”
- **AI Personalized Medicine** (Ankit Gupta): proposes using “an agent harness like Claude Code” to analyze diagnostics, genomes, EHRs, and wearable data. It argues that the cost of genome sequencing is falling “faster than Moore's law,” and that “n of 1 genetic therapies” will become possible through delivery systems such as mRNA.
- **AI-Native Discovery Engines** (Jon Xu): argues that frontier models have reached “PhD-level performance on many scientific reasoning benchmarks,” and are moving toward closing the “design-make-test-analyze loop.”
- **Counter-Swarm Defense** (Tyler Bosmeny): “A Patriot missile costs three million dollars. An FPV drone? Five hundred bucks.” Starting from this cost asymmetry, it argues that drone defense is becoming “more like Cloudflare than Raytheon.”
- **Hardware Supply Chain** (Nicolas Dessaigne): says that in Shenzhen, the loop from design to new components can take “a day,” whereas in the United States the same loop can take “weeks.” It cites “Hlabs (W26)” and “Prototyping.io (P26)” as examples.
- **Startups That Want to Sell to Huge Companies** (Harshita Arora, Brad Flora): notes that more companies are now closing pilots and even “multimillion dollar deals” with F100-scale enterprises during a YC batch itself.
What unifies this physical-world cluster is its repeated use of cost asymmetry as evidence of market size—Patriot $3M versus FPV $500, or a 90% reduction in pesticide use.

## What this implies for this site

This RFS directly overlaps with this site’s core theme of AI-native organizations and ways of working. In particular, “Software for Agents,” “Company Brain,” and “The AI Operating System for Companies” all call for a software foundation in which agents are treated as first-class citizens, pushing for a rebuild of the existing stack that was designed around human-facing UIs. In YC’s framing, the next-generation opportunity lies on the side of infrastructure built on the assumption of “agents as first-class citizens.”
