The axis that determines hierarchy is now being quietly rewritten. At first glance, there are three unrelated facts. First, 6 Big Tech companies issued $182 billion in investment-grade bonds in 2026, an increase of about 1,300% from about $13 billion in the same period the year before. Second, AI capital expenditure by 5 major companies is projected to reach about 3.2% of U.S. GDP in 2027, surpassing defense spending, estimated at about 2.7% that same year, for the first time in history. Third, a former Chief Product Officer at Slack is seriously looking for a PM internship. Capital markets, national budgets, individual careers—the scales and domains are all different. But in fact, all of them can be explained by the same principle. Existing hierarchies are being remeasured along a single axis: proximity to the AI capability frontier.

## The hierarchy of capital: from creditworthiness to “AI capital demand”

What “The bond market driven by the AI arms race” captured was a reshuffling of hierarchy in the most conservative of markets: corporate bond issuance. 6 companies—Amazon, Alphabet, Nvidia, Meta, Oracle, and SpaceX—issued $182 billion in investment-grade bonds in 2026. Compared with about $13 billion in the same period a year earlier, the roughly 1,300% increase is not just more issuance. These 6 companies alone account for about 15% of U.S. corporate bond issuance year to date, and more than half of this year’s increase in issuance has come from these 6 alone.

The corporate bond market is, by nature, a place where issuers are ranked by creditworthiness—their ability to repay. But now a second axis is being inserted into that hierarchy: how much willingness and need a company has to invest capital in AI. Big Tech firms that should be flush with cash are choosing to raise debt anyway. That means capital demand is emerging on the AI infrastructure side at a scale that available cash on hand cannot keep up with. The ranking of issuers is beginning to be overwritten—not by the health of not needing to borrow, but by the scale of what they are willing to invest even if they have to borrow to do it. The concentration represented by that 15% market share is proof that this rewiring of the axis is happening not at the margins, but at the center. And the fact that these 6 companies account for more than half of the increase shows not merely that the top of the hierarchy has been reshuffled, but that the ruler used to measure hierarchy itself is being pulled toward those 6 companies. Issuers that excelled under the old axis of creditworthiness are sinking in relative terms under the new one. Once the market begins placing “issuers betting on the frontier” above “safe issuers,” the very meaning of hierarchy changes.

## The hierarchy of the state: the symbolism of surpassing defense

What “AI capital expenditure to reach 3.2% of U.S. GDP in 2027” shows is a sign that the same rewiring is emerging at the scale of the state. AI capital expenditure by 5 companies—Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle—is projected to reach about $1.1 trillion in 2027, expanding further from more than $800 billion in 2026, and to account for about 3.2% of U.S. GDP. That level would surpass defense spending, estimated at about 2.7% that same year, for the first time in history.

Defense spending has long sat at the top of the hierarchy of budget allocations that express what a state considers its highest priority to protect. The prospect of capital expenditure by a handful of private companies exceeding it represents a reordering of hierarchy in two senses. First, AI capital expenditure is no longer just the budget of a single industry; it has been elevated into a macroeconomic component discussed as a share of GDP. Second, the relative scale of “what the state protects” and “what the private sector is willing to bet on” is reversed. Alongside the old top-priority axis of national security, a new priority axis appears: the scale of investment in the frontier. And the latter begins to overtake the former. What happened in the hierarchy of capital is being repeated in the hierarchy of the state.

## The hierarchy of the individual: from the top back to the entrance, deliberately

The third fact shows that the same principle still applies even when the scale shrinks down to the individual. “Former Slack Chief Product Officer looks for a ‘PM internship’” focused on the paradoxical move of someone who reached the pinnacle of a career deliberately seeking an entry-level role. Brandon K. Hill of btrax sees this as a sign of a quietly spreading “career downshift” in Silicon Valley.

Career hierarchy has traditionally been measured as climbing a ladder of titles in one direction. CPO sits close to the very top of that ladder. And yet the choice to return to the entrance cannot be explained by a logic of maximizing one’s position on that ladder. It can only be explained by maximizing a different axis—how close you are to the field that AI is transforming most rapidly. Instead of choosing the prestige of a top title, the person chooses a position from which they can touch the frontier and relearn. The individual is remeasuring their own hierarchy not by title, but by proximity to the frontier.

## The common principle: AI is not competing within hierarchies, but redrawing the axis of hierarchy itself

So what do these three facts suggest? Traditionally, these three hierarchies were governed by mutually independent logics. Bond-market hierarchy was determined by creditworthiness, the hierarchy of national budgets by national security, and career hierarchy by title and years of experience. Different domains meant different axes of ranking—that was the default assumption. But now a common gravitational pull is acting across all three: proximity to the AI capability frontier.

Let me make the inductive step explicit here. These facts suggest that AI is not competing for rank “within” existing hierarchies; it is redrawing the very axis by which rank is measured. In other words, the ruler of hierarchy is being replaced—from “your place on an established ladder” to “your distance from the frontier.” In the bond market, issuers are being remeasured by the size of their capital demand—that is, the scale of their frontier investment. In national budgets, allocation priorities are being remeasured by bets on the frontier. For individuals, rank is being remeasured not by title but by proximity to the frontier. The scales differ by orders of magnitude—capital, state, individual—but the phenomenon of axis replacement takes the same form.

This principle is falsifiable. If this rewiring of the axis is real, then the same directional movement should persist across different scales. Conversely, if at any one scale hierarchy reverts to the old axis—for example, if the share of the AI 6 in the bond market starts shrinking, or if CapEx fails to surpass defense spending—then it can be judged a temporary bubble rather than a permanent redrawing of the axis. This is not being offered as a platitude, but as a claim that can be beaten up by the numbers.

## Predictions and implications

If this principle is correct, then we can predict the following. First, in capital markets, “AI exposure” will become established as a second rating axis alongside credit ratings. The probability is high—the foundation is already there in the fact that 6 companies account for 15% of the market and more than half of the increase. Investors will begin screening issuers not only for repayment capacity but also for their commitment to investing in the frontier. Second, in national budgets, AI infrastructure will become a category in permanent competition with defense. The probability is medium—because it depends on the 2027 forecast, whether the crossover becomes reality still needs to be watched, but the debate has already moved once it began being framed as a share of GDP. Third, in professional careers, the choice of “frontier proximity” over “title” will spread. The probability is medium to low; for now it remains at the case-study stage, but if the rewiring that began in capital and the state descends to the level of the individual, it will move in this direction.

What must not be overlooked, moreover, is that this axis is self-reinforcing. If capital is ranked by frontier proximity, then the actors already near the top will be able to gather the next wave of capital more cheaply and in greater volume, further reducing their distance to the frontier. If national budgets tilt toward the same axis, then public money joins that race for closeness. If individuals choose proximity over title, talent flows toward the frontier as well. These three rewiring processes are not occurring independently; they accelerate one another. That is precisely why the question of whether this is a bubble or a structural transformation should not be judged by a single indicator, but by whether all three scales continue pointing in the same direction.

The practical implication for readers is clear. Stop measuring your organization, your career, or your investment decisions by where you stand on the ladder, and start remeasuring them by their distance from the frontier. The height of your status no longer guarantees safety in the hierarchy if it does not come with proximity to the frontier. Conversely, even a position that looks like an entry point can rank high on the new axis if it is in contact with the frontier. What these three sites are simultaneously telling us is simple: what matters is not which rung of the ladder you protect, but your distance from the frontier.
