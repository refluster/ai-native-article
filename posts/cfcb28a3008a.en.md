As the very power to “generate” rapidly becomes cheaper, where will competitive advantage shift for companies and individuals? Three developments that appear unrelated at first glance all return the same answer to that question. In the midst of record profits, Meta forcibly reassigned 30〜50% of engineers on core teams into a data-labeling organization, redefining engineering “from a center of profit to a center of cost” (L2 “Why Meta’s engineering culture collapsed”). Palantir CEO Alex Karp argued that frontier AI labs competing on model performance “don’t know how hated they are,” and pointed out that the layer where companies actually derive value is “implementation” (L2 “‘Frontier AI labs are hated’”). And the entrepreneur who sold a product built in 4 months of solo development through vibe coding for 8,000万ドル ran into the wall that even if AI can compress generative labor into a single person, judgment and oversight cannot be compressed all the way. In fact, all of these can be explained by the same principle: the more generation becomes commoditized, the more value moves to the adjacent layer of “judgment, implementation, and oversight.”

## When the Core Falls into a “Center of Cost”

The most striking example is Meta’s reorganization. According to L2 “Why Meta’s engineering culture collapsed,” after its acquisition of Scale AI in 2025年6月, Meta pushed through an organizational restructuring that made AI strategy the top priority, forcibly reassigning 30〜50% of engineers on core teams into the “ADO organization” responsible for data labeling. It also made keystroke monitoring mandatory, introduced processed token counts into performance evaluations, and announced a 10% reduction in headcount across the company even under record profits.

What is happening here is not simply restructuring. What that L2 article points out is a structural shift in which engineering as a function has been redefined from a “center of profit” into a “center of cost.” The marginal value of writing code—that is, “generation”—has declined, and labor has instead been redirected toward the fuel that makes models smarter: labeling, or the “supply of judgment.” The irony is that some of the world’s highest-level engineers were shifted from being agents of generation to being agents of judgment used to train the generators. What was once core became cost, and labeling, once peripheral, was elevated to the center of strategy.

## Generated Capability Does Not Become Value on Its Own

L2 “‘Frontier AI labs are hated’” captures the same shift from the demand side. The core of CEO Karp’s criticism is that while labs are pouring resources into competition over model performance, they are neglecting the layer of “implementation” where companies actually extract value.

This argument is not a matter of emotion; it is backed by numbers. According to Gartner estimates cited in that same L2 article, only 28% of AI use cases fully met ROI expectations. In the remaining more than 70%, companies may have high-performance models on hand—that is, the ability to “generate” well—but are unable to connect them to the context of actual business operations. That is precisely why even OpenAI, itself a supplier of generators, acquired the consulting firm Tomoro and began bringing deployment support in-house. The supplier of generation is itself descending into the adjacent “implementation layer.” Here too, what is scarce is not generative capability, but the judgment required to bridge it into a specific business workflow.

## Labor That Can Be Compressed into One Person, and Judgment That Cannot

The third L2 piece, “Entrepreneurs Running ‘One-Person Companies’ with AI,” reflects the same principle at the scale of the individual. Maor Shlomo left the 100-person company he had spent 7 years building, launched the vibe coding platform Base44 in 2025年2月 after building it in just 4 months, generated approximately 150万ドル in revenue in 1 month, and sold it to Wix in 6月 for 8,000万ドル. This was an economy made possible because AI agents compressed generative labor that had once been distributed across PMs, QA, and developers into a single person.

But that same L2 article makes clear that there is a very real ceiling to that compression. Compute costs reaching hundreds of thousands of dollars per month, the absence of specialized judgment, and the physical burden of operating alone 24 hours a day—all of these constrain the scale of the model. What matters here is the boundary between what could be compressed and what could not. Generative labor—writing code, running tests, drafting specifications—could be folded into one person. What could not be folded in was the expert judgment about what should be built and when, and the responsibility of overseeing a system that must keep running. Generation lost its scarcity, while judgment and oversight remained as the new bottlenecks.

## The Common Principle: When Generation Becomes a Surplus, Scarcity Moves to the “Adjacent Layer”

Put these three facts side by side, and while their surface themes—big-tech restructuring, criticism of vendors, solo entrepreneurship—look disparate, the underlying dynamic converges on one thing. Namely, **when AI makes the “generation” step in a process cheap and abundant, the marginal value of that step itself declines, and scarcity and profit move to adjacent layers that generation still cannot supply on its own**. In economic terms, when the price of one complement falls, the relative value of the other good paired with it rises. In a world where generation becomes almost infinitely cheap, what rises in price is the judgment and oversight required to turn that generation into meaningful outcomes.

At Meta, code generation became relatively cheaper, and value shifted to the supply of labeling＝judgment used to train it. In the AI market, model generation became plentiful, and value shifted to implementation＝connection to context, where only 28% are satisfying ROI. In the one-person company, once product generation could be folded into a single individual, the bottleneck shifted to expert judgment and 24-hour oversight. What all three share is a reversal: “being able to make things well” is no longer the competitive advantage; competitive advantage lies instead in “deciding what should be made, connecting it to real-world context, and keeping it running.” These facts suggest that generative capability itself is being commoditized, and that value is systematically moving one layer over, into judgment, implementation, and oversight. Meta’s phrase that engineering has shifted “from a center of profit to a center of cost” is nothing less than a slogan that anticipated this migration within a single company.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, then the changes likely to unfold over the next few years can be predicted with fairly high confidence. First, job roles will be redefined more broadly. Functions that were responsible for generation itself—coding, writing, first-draft design—will be repositioned as “cost centers,” just as Meta has already foreshadowed, and the axis of evaluation will shift from “how much did you produce?” to “what judgments did you make, and what did you oversee?” Evaluating people by token counts should instead be read as a transitional mistake of this period of change. Second, suppliers of generators will continue to move into the implementation layer. OpenAI’s acquisition of Tomoro is not a one-off; it is the leading edge of a trend in which labs go and fill for themselves the more than 70% of the market, in Gartner’s terms, that has “not been able to extract value.”

Third, there are headwinds to this migration, and they require some discounting of confidence. The layers of judgment, implementation, and oversight retain value because they are harder to automate than the layer of generation, but they are also the next frontier AI will target. If implementation support like Tomoro is eventually agentized, then scarcity will move one layer deeper still—to the entity that actually bears responsibility. In other words, this is not the claim that “the judgment layer is safe forever.” It should instead be read as a dynamic claim: value moves from generation to the adjacent layer, and then repeatedly onward beyond it. The claim is also clearly falsifiable. If AI use cases that meet ROI rise substantially from 28%, and if the scarcity of implementation talent simultaneously declines, then this principle should be judged to have weakened.

The practical implication is straightforward. For organizations and for individuals alike, what deserves investment is not faster generation, but the three adjacent layers next to generation: the expert judgment that decides what to build, the implementation capability that connects it to business context, and the oversight that watches over a system that keeps running. What Base44 demonstrated is that even when generation is automated to the limit, these are still the three layers left at the end. In a world where generation is in everyone’s hands, what remains scarce is not the capacity to keep generating, but the judgment that directs generation correctly. If AI does the “making,” then human value will converge on “deciding what to make and why, and taking responsibility for the consequences.”
