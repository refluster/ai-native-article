The better generation gets, the more value escapes to what lies outside it. A practical guide to reproducibility in slide generation, Palantir CEO’s criticism that companies “hate” frontier AI labs, and the story of Base44—a company built by one person in 4 months and sold for $80 million—may look like three unrelated topics spanning design theory, industry politics, and startup lore. In fact, they can all be explained by a single principle: as the act of generation becomes commoditized, scarcity and bottlenecks migrate to “structures externalized outside generation.” Each of the three illuminates that shift from a different angle.

## Reproducibility resides not in individual skill, but in externalized specifications

What “Decoding the Practical Guide to SLIDE.md” addressed was an instability peculiar to generative AI: slides created with Claude Design would vary wildly from run to run, like a roulette wheel of design outcomes. The guide’s solution was not to refine instructions given on the spot. Instead, it externalized slide-specific specifications—SLIDE.md / SLIDE-PATTERN / SLIDE-DECK.md—*before* generation, and broke the process down from installation to completion into 4 steps. In other words, it moved judgment forward from the moment of generation to the stage before generation. Its core claim was simple: design reproducibility does not reside in the quality of prompting at generation time, but in specifications externalized beforehand. The fact that the article itself drew 979,939 impressions and 4,508 bookmarks speaks to how many creators share the same pain point.

### The evaluation axis shifts from “speed” to “can you keep producing to the same standard?”

What matters here is that the guide makes this shift in evaluation criteria explicit. The question is no longer whether you can produce a single slide quickly, but whether you can keep producing to the same standard. Models will increase speed on their own. Consistency, by contrast, cannot be obtained unless someone writes the standard down externally as structure. Prompting skill is personal and non-reproducible, but specifications remain behind as assets and continue to work for others and for the next iteration. The source of value is moving from skills that disappear to structures that accumulate.

## Value lies in the implementation layer—the thing labs overlook

The same shift appears from the enterprise adoption side in the critique by Palantir CEO Alex Karp. In the article “Frontier AI labs are hated,” he sharply criticizes labs such as Anthropic and OpenAI, saying they “don’t realize how much they are hated.” At the heart of his critique is the claim that while labs focus on competing over model performance, they neglect the implementation layer where companies actually derive value.

### The fault line revealed by the figure 28%

This criticism is not emotional rhetoric; it is backed by numbers. According to Gartner estimates, only 28% of AI use cases have fully met ROI expectations. The reason more than 70% fall short is not that the models are weak. It is that the structure of implementation and deployment bridging models and real-world operations is missing. Tellingly, the lab side has also begun to acknowledge this divide. OpenAI acquired the consulting firm Tomoro and formed a deployment support unit. The party with the top-performing model deliberately moved to internalize the structure of implementation—this was not merely words, but a statement made through capital allocation that value cannot be extracted from the model alone. Here too, what is scarce is not generation but the externalized structure that connects generation to actual work. The pattern that SLIDE.md demonstrated in individual creative work is being replayed at the much larger scale of enterprise adoption.

## Aggregation will advance, but what cannot be externalized becomes the ceiling

The third story, Base44, shows this principle in its most dramatic form. Maor Shlomo left a company of 100 people that he had built over 7 years and, in just 4 months, created the vibe coding platform Base44. Within only 1 month of its launch in February 2025, it generated roughly $1.5 million in revenue, and in June it was sold to Wix for $80 million. It is hard to imagine a clearer example of AI agents consolidating labor that had been distributed across PMs, QAs, and developers into a single person. Once generation becomes cheap and abundant, even the structure of an organization itself can be folded into one individual.

### The ceiling is created by what cannot be fully externalized

But the same article also coolly notes the limits of this model. Compute costs reaching hundreds of thousands of dollars per month, the absence of specialist judgment, and the physical burden of running everything alone 24 hours a day—these still constrain how far such a setup can scale. This is the core point. What could be folded into one person was labor that could be externalized and automated, such as specifications and code. What cannot be folded in, and therefore creates the ceiling, is what cannot be fully externalized: context-dependent specialist judgment, the stamina that sustains operations, and the economics of cost. If SLIDE.md shows the value of “structure that can be externalized,” then Base44, from the reverse side, shows that “structure that cannot be externalized” becomes the new scarce resource.

## The shared principle: as generation becomes commoditized, scarcity shifts to structure

Set these three facts side by side, and a single line runs through them. SLIDE.md says reproducibility resides in pre-generation specifications; Karp says value lies in the implementation layer; Base44 shows that the ceiling on aggregation lies in judgment and operations that cannot be externalized. Together, these facts suggest one underlying principle: the more the act of generation itself becomes commoditized, the more scarcity and bottlenecks move to “structures externalized outside generation.”

The inductive chain is this. What all three share is the observation that the quality and speed of generation are no longer producing meaningful differentiation. Slides can be generated, code can be written, models are smart. And yet reproducibility still wavers, ROI remains stuck at 28%, and a one-person company still hits a ceiling. What creates differentiation is what comes before and outside generation: specifications externalized in advance, implementation layers that connect generation to business operations, and the structures of judgment and operations that cannot be fully automated. The moment generation becomes an abundant good available to anyone, value shifts to what remains scarce. And what is scarce is no longer generation, but structure. This principle contains two sides at once: “structures that can be externalized capture value as assets, while structures that cannot be externalized become the next bottlenecks.”

## Forecasts and implications

If this principle is correct, the axis of competition will shift from “generate better” to “structure better.” Three high-confidence predictions follow.

First, the phase of competing over prompting skill will be short-lived. Just as SLIDE.md shifted prompting skill forward into specifications, advantage will be determined by “what you externalized before generation.” The implication for individuals is clear: what you should refine is not one-off instructions, but reusable specifications, templates, and standards. Skill evaporates, but externalized structure accumulates.

Second, providers responsible for the “implementation layer” between AI labs and user companies will grow. As Gartner’s 28% and OpenAI’s acquisition of Tomoro show, the implementation layer is where value is captured. The stronger models become, the higher the relative value of this layer rises—when generation approaches free, people will only pay for structuring.

Third, “one-person companies” will increase, but their ceiling will be determined by the degree of externalization. Among the compute cost, specialist judgment, and operational burden that Base44 ran into, the ceiling will rise in the same order that those constraints can be cut out and turned into structure. Put differently, the scarce professional capability in the AI era will be the ability to handle judgment and operations that are difficult to externalize. This prediction also has a falsifiable edge: if, going forward, even specialist judgment and operations can be sufficiently externalized to general-purpose agents, then the ceiling will disappear and scarcity will move elsewhere again. That is precisely when the reach of this principle will be tested.

For practitioners, the implication converges to one point: spend less time on “how to generate faster” and more on “what to write down externally as structure.” Generation will eventually become a commodity. What remains is only the structure you have externalized.
