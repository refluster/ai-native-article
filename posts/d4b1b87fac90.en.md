## AI Agents × Operational Transformation

## The Principle of “Workflow Embedding” That Connects the Proliferation of Design Tools, SRE Automation, and Practical Knowledge from agentic AI Implementations

## Introduction

Among designers, **AI usage has reached 91%**, and they are using **an average of 7 tools** in parallel. PagerDuty is strengthening its SRE Agent in an effort to move incident response earlier **“without waking people up.”** McKinsey’s observations from real-world agentic AI deployments suggest that success or failure is not determined simply by model performance.

At first glance, these facts may seem unrelated. In reality, they can all be explained by the same principle: **the value of AI is determined not by how smart a standalone tool is, but by where—and how deeply—it is embedded in the workflow.**

## Analysis 1: What’s Happening in Design Is Not “AI Tool Selection” but a Redesign of Task Decomposition

The first thing that stands out in the article on design is the strikingly high **91% AI usage rate**. That indicates the debate over whether to use AI is essentially over, and the field has already moved on to the question of how to integrate it. The figure of **7 tools on average** is especially revealing. If a single general-purpose model could solve everything, there would be no need for 7 tools.

What this suggests is that **work is not a single act of “generation,” but is broken down into many smaller steps such as exploration, summarization, ideation, editing, validation, and implementation handoff.**

The article title also notes that **“Claude overtook ChatGPT.”** The important point here is not which one is better in general. In actual design work, Claude may have been relatively better suited for certain kinds of tasks involving long-context handling, consistency of reasoning, instruction-following, or organizing context.

In other words, users are beginning to choose tools not by brand, but by **fit for a specific step in the process**. That is also a rebuttal to the simplistic notion that “the AI market will be winner-take-all.”

What we can infer from this is that designers are not adopting AI wholesale as a single “assistant.” Rather, they are **reorganizing it into a set of optimized microservices for each step of the workflow.**

These facts suggest that the competitive axis in AI is shifting from **model performance → the ability to eliminate friction within the workflow**.

## Analysis 2: PagerDuty’s Strengthened SRE Agent Marks a Shift from “AI That Answers” to “AI That Takes the First Action”

The PagerDuty article shows the same change, but in a context much closer to operational reality. The core of the announcement is an expansion of SRE Agent into **automatic triage triggered by Incident Workflows**, with the goal of pulling response forward **“without waking people up.”**

What matters here is that AI is no longer merely “explaining” the cause of an outage; it has been **connected to the starting point of the operational flow**.

Much of traditional operations AI remained at the level of a “smart copilot”: summarizing alerts, searching related logs, recommending Runbooks, and the like. But what PagerDuty is trying to strengthen is **automatic investigation, automated decision support, and automated suppression of unnecessary escalations**, all initiated from Incident Workflows.

The phrase “without waking people up” is symbolic. In SRE, value is not measured by the quality of generated explanations, but by improvements in operational KPIs such as **nighttime pages, MTTA, initial-response delays, and false escalations**.

Here too, what matters is not the intelligence of the AI in the abstract, but **which events trigger it, what data it can access, and how much authority is delegated to it**.

For example, if AI is embedded into a sequence such as alert receipt → matching against past incidents → dependency verification → impact estimation → automatic suppression when risk is low, then outcomes can vary dramatically even when the same foundation model is used.

These facts suggest that, in operations, the essence of AI is moving from **“intelligence you consult via chat” to “intelligence that acts first inside the flow.”**

## Analysis 3: What McKinsey’s Practical Lessons on agentic AI Show Is the Importance of “Operational Design Under Constraints” More Than “Expanding Autonomy”

McKinsey’s “One year of agentic AI,” while the full text was not available here, is described as organizing **six lessons from one year of practical experience**. That alone carries an important implication. agentic AI has moved beyond proof-of-concept and become **something organizations learn from while operating it**.

The phrase “teams actively deploying and operating these systems” also suggests that attention has shifted away from demo performance and toward the **practical work of deployment, supervision, and improvement**.

The issues that repeatedly surface in practical agentic AI deployments are likely to be the following.

First, agents are most stable not in isolation, but when they are given **clear task boundaries**.

Second, the key success factors are not model IQ, but **tool connectivity, guardrails, evaluation design, and human approval points**.

Third, the impact of adoption is measured not by “how much smarter” the system is, but by **how many tasks it can move forward earlier and which tasks it can perform without human intervention**.

This aligns with the PagerDuty example, and it is also consistent with the proliferation of design tools. The reason designers use 7 tools on average is not simply that no all-purpose agent exists, but that **the constraints differ from one step of the process to another**. And in SRE operations, automatic triage creates value because under the hard constraints of nighttime response, shortening the first few minutes makes a significant difference.

These facts suggest that the essence of agentic AI is not “making it as autonomous as possible,” but **designing units of action to fit real work with strong constraints**.

## Presenting the Shared Principle

### The Value of AI Is Determined Not by “How Intelligent It Is,” but by “How Deeply It Is Embedded in the Workflow”

So what is the underlying principle that runs through all three articles? The most plausible hypothesis is this: **AI has moved past the stage where it is introduced as a standalone smart tool, and it generates substantial economic value only when it becomes “institutionalized” as part of the workflow.**

Let’s look at it inductively.

In design, **91% are using AI**, they are switching among **7 tools on average**, and a dynamic reshuffling is taking place in which **Claude overtook ChatGPT**. That means users are not satisfied with simply putting one general-purpose chat AI in place. They are moving toward toolsets optimized for each step—idea generation, mood board organization, copy drafts, UI text, research summaries, and so on.

In SRE, PagerDuty is advancing automatic triage from the **Incident Workflows** starting point, aiming to move the first response earlier **“without waking people up.”** That shows AI becoming not just a “consultation partner,” but part of the execution layer of the operational workflow itself.

McKinsey’s practical findings on agentic AI likewise show that, when lessons are being extracted from a year of live operation, the center of value creation has shifted away from pure model capability and toward **orchestration, supervision, evaluation, and connectivity**.

These facts suggest that as AI adoption spreads, the source of competitive advantage moves away from the model itself and toward **workflow design, data connectivity, authority design, and evaluation metrics**.

The answer to why is straightforward. What matters to companies is not “what AI can say,” but **how much it can reduce operational waiting time, decision burden, rework, nighttime response, and switching costs**.

So what? The companies that will win the next phase of AI strategy will not be those that “signed a contract for the highest-performing model.” They will be **the ones that identified points of friction in real operations and embedded agents there safely**.

## Future Outlook and Implications

The future implied by this principle is relatively clear. First, **usage centered on standalone chat UIs will decline, and AI built directly into business applications is likely to become the norm**. The probability of this is roughly **70-80%**. The reason is that design teams are already fragmenting into an average of 7 tools, while in SRE, workflow-triggered automation has already begun. Users will stop “opening AI” and instead use AI inside Figma, PagerDuty, IDEs, CRM, and ERP.

Second, **competition between models will continue, but switching costs will decline relative to today**. The very observation that Claude overtook ChatGPT is evidence that users are already swapping tools flexibly depending on the use case. Future differentiation is likely to move away from model brand and toward **connections to operational data, permission controls, auditing, and evaluation dashboards**. The probability here is around **65-75%**.

Third, full-scale deployment of agentic AI is more likely to advance through **limited-authority and staged-approval models** than through fully autonomous ones. This too seems likely at roughly **80%** probability. The reason is that in high-trust domains like PagerDuty’s, **autonomy limited to initial response**, such as automatic triage or candidate suggestions, offers a better balance of ROI and safety than immediate end-to-end autonomy.

There are four practical takeaways for readers.

The first is: do not begin AI adoption planning by asking which model to standardize across the entire company. Start by identifying the steps where **waiting time, judgment dependent on individuals, nighttime load, and re-entry of information** are greatest.

The second is: define PoC KPIs not in terms of accuracy, but in terms of **reduction in processing time, first-response automation rate, escalation reduction rate, and rework rate**.

The third is: do not give agents broad freedom. Give them **narrow responsibilities, clear inputs, and clear stopping conditions**.

The fourth is: assume the field will use multiple tools, and make sure your company controls the **orchestration layer**. In an era of 7 tools on average, competitiveness will depend less on which individual tool is better and more on how the whole system is connected.

In short, the next AI competition will not be about “who has the smartest AI.” It will be decided by **who has the best-designed flow of work**.
