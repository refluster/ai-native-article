The Trump administration asks OpenAI to release GPT-5.6 in stages; an RBC survey finds that more than half of enterprises say “AI is already in production”; John Jumper leaves Google DeepMind for Anthropic. At first glance, these are three unrelated developments in regulation, markets, and talent. But in fact, they can all be explained by the same principle. The moment frontier AI capability crossed a certain threshold, it ceased to be merely a “product feature” and became a “scarce strategic resource,” and the struggle for control over its allocation has erupted simultaneously across three layers: the state, the market, and talent. This essay reads three facts that appear to belong to separate streams of industry news as symptoms of a single phase transition.

## The State Has Begun Intervening in “Release” Itself

What “The Trump administration asks OpenAI for a phased release of GPT-5.6” recorded was the first case in which the U.S. government sought advance restraint on model release from a domestic AI company. The substance of the request was that general release of the next model, GPT-5.6, should be limited to government-approved partners and proceed in stages. What matters most is the point at which the intervention begins. National security concerns are now reaching not a specific product or use case, but the very decision of whether to release a model that exceeds a certain capability threshold.

In other words, what the government is treating as the issue is not “what will it be used for?” but “how much capability, into whose hands, and in what order will it be distributed?” This resembles an export-control logic that activates not at the level of finished goods but at the threshold of capability itself. Traditionally, the point of contact between the state and AI lay in ex post regulation—privacy, copyright, discrimination. But this request is qualitatively new because the state has put its hand on the “release valve” before the product reaches the world. The state has begun to take a seat in planning the allocation of capability not merely as a regulator, but as a direct stakeholder—and that single fact runs through the market and talent dynamics discussed below.

## The Market Is Hoarding the Resource Through “Production Dependence”

“The shift in enterprise AI spending from proof of concept to production, as reflected in RBC’s CIO survey,” captures the same phenomenon at another layer. In its semiannual spending survey of more than 100 CIOs and technology leaders, RBC Capital Markets found that more than half of respondents said “AI is already in production,” while 35% expected production deployment within six months. 100% of respondents had budgeted for AI/LLM initiatives, and 91% had created net-new budget lines for them. This spending is no longer experimental spend; it is becoming the operating cost of core systems.

What matters here is concentration. By vendor, the most commonly used tool was ChatGPT at 57%, far ahead of Claude at 12%. During the proof-of-concept stage, it was normal to compare multiple models side by side. But production deployment creates dependence on specific platforms. Once capability is embedded in a production system, it becomes enclosed by switching costs—re-evaluation, re-integration, re-training. The market is not buying ownership of capability itself; it is buying continuing access to capability that works in production, and the allocation of that access is beginning to converge around a small number of vendors.

### “From Proof of Concept to Production” Is an Irreversible Lock-In

Proof of concept is reversible; production is nearly irreversible. The 57% versus 12% gap is not just a popularity contest. It is a record of which vendors have installed capability inside the core processes of the enterprise. If capability is a scarce resource, then whoever installs it first in production gains priority rights over its allocation. What the RBC survey shows is not the mild fact that “AI has spread,” but a map of power: access to capability in production is quietly being concentrated in the hands of a few suppliers.

## Talent Is Being Fought Over as the “Manufacturing Apparatus” of Capability

The third development, “What John Jumper’s move to Anthropic shows about the collapsing boundaries between research, product, and strategy in AI labs,” is a struggle over the very source of the resource. Reuters reported on 2026年6月19日 that John Jumper was expected to leave Google DeepMind and join Anthropic. What this move makes visible is both the intensity of the competition for top researchers and the fact that the boundaries between research, product, and strategy—once treated as separate roles—are beginning to dissolve.

Capability as a scarce resource is, in the end, designed inside the minds of a very small number of people. That is precisely why the movement of a single researcher, as a “manufacturing apparatus” for that resource, can redraw a company’s competitive structure in one stroke. The dissolution of role boundaries is not accidental. Once capability becomes a strategic resource, the researchers who create it are forced to stand simultaneously at the center of product judgment and strategic judgment, and the movement of one person can shift all three layers at once. The fact that Jumper’s move becomes news rather than a routine personnel matter is itself the clearest proof that talent is beginning to be treated as a supply node for the resource.

## The Common Principle—Capability Has Shifted From a Question of “Existence” to a Question of “Allocation”

Set these three facts side by side, and they converge on a single principle. **Once frontier AI capability crossed a certain threshold, the central question shifted from “does the capability exist?” to “who gets to allocate that capability, and how?”**

Until now, the main battleground in AI was the existence of capability—could you build a smarter model? But the three developments now in view—the government intervening in release order (the GPT-5.6 release request), enterprises enclosing capability through production dependence (the RBC survey), and labs competing for researchers (Jumper’s move)—are all struggles not over the “manufacture” of capability, but over its “allocation.” Together, they suggest that capability is no longer simply a source of product differentiation. It is starting to be treated as a scarce, strategic resource—something closer to oil or advanced semiconductors.

Scarce resources obey a common logic. The moment society recognizes something as both scarce and strategic, struggles for control erupt simultaneously across every layer that touches it—the state that authorizes supply, the market that consumes it and becomes dependent on it, and the talent that produces it. That was true of oil. It was true of advanced semiconductors. And now AI capability is following the same path. The three events observed in 2026 are not separate items of industry news; they are symptoms of this single phase transition breaking out across three layers. That is why they “can all be explained by the same principle.”

This principle is falsifiable. If capability becomes commoditized and all models become interchangeable, governments will stop caring about release order, enterprises will switch vendors freely, and the movement of a single researcher will vanish into statistical noise. If the struggle for control subsides across all three layers, then this hypothesis should be rejected. Conversely, so long as the struggle continues to intensify, capability remains a resource. The indicators to watch are straightforward: the institutionalization of state intervention, the degree of oligopoly among production vendors, and the market impact of researcher moves. Whether those three all continue moving in the same direction will determine whether the principle lives or dies.

## Forecast and Implications

If this principle is correct, then the changes likely over the next year can be anticipated. First, intervention at the state layer will not end as a one-off. Since the request regarding GPT-5.6 was triggered by a “capability threshold,” the next model to cross that threshold is likely to face a more institutionalized form of review. This is the process by which export-control logic is being transplanted into decisions about software release, and the probability is high—because once a line has been drawn, it tends to be applied to the next model as well.

Second, concentration at the market layer will deepen further. The 57% versus 12% gap widens as production dependence hardens. But this could also reverse. If the government narrows the release of a particular vendor’s model—the logic of state intervention—then the companies that depend most deeply on that vendor in production will take the most direct hit from supply constraints. Market concentration and state intervention will collide in the areas with the deepest production dependence. That creates a practical implication for CIOs: dependence on a single vendor in production should no longer be evaluated merely as a performance risk, but as an “allocation risk”—that is, the danger that access to supply is controlled by the state or by competitive dynamics. A procurement strategy for capability will start to resemble a national-security procurement strategy.

Third, competition over talent will accelerate the dissolution of roles. If capability is a resource, and if the movement of a single researcher at its source can shift all three layers, then every lab will treat researchers not as a matter of “hiring” but of “securing resources.” The collapse of boundaries is not a side effect but a necessary consequence of resource-ification. The bargaining power of individual researchers, as supply nodes for a scarce resource, will likely reach a historically unprecedented level.

If there is one implication to leave readers with, it is this: the era in which AI decision-making could be measured by “which model is smarter?” is quietly coming to an end. The real question now is: who currently controls the allocation of that capability? In a three-layer structure in which the state controls release, the market controls production, and labs control talent, the first essential literacy of the age in which capability has become a resource is understanding exactly which dependencies you stand on top of.
