On July 30, 2026, Okta agreed to acquire Permiso Security, an identity monitoring company for AI agents, for about $200 million. Two days earlier, Peter Ludwig, co-founder and CTO of Applied Intuition, wrote that the rate-limiting factor in physical AI is not model intelligence but engineering throughput. Around the same time, The Kobeissi Letter pointed out that U.S. data center power demand could reach 194 gigawatts by 2035. An acquisition, a technical essay, and a power forecast—the three seem to have nothing in common. In fact, they can be explained by the same principle. All three are reporting, from different layers, that the constraints surrounding AI have shifted from “a gateway you pass once” to “throughput that must be continuously supplied or everything stops.”

## Verification, from something you pass to something you sustain

What “Okta acquires AI agent identity monitoring company Permiso for about $200 million” records is not a change in corporate shopping lists, but a shift in the center of gravity of the identity management industry. The article’s formulation is clear: from “verifying users at login” to “continuously monitoring what users, applications, and AI agents are doing after they have been granted access.”

This shift follows inevitably from the nature of agents as actors. Human employees log in a few times a day and perform a finite number of actions in between. Agents authenticate once and then take thousands or tens of thousands of actions. Authentication is a discrete event; monitoring is a continuous process. As the number of actions rises, the scope of what is guaranteed by having passed a gateway once becomes relatively thinner.

What matters is that Okta chose to obtain this capability through acquisition rather than building it internally. The valuation was just under $200 million, structured almost entirely in cash—the article explicitly notes that Okta itself did not disclose the deal terms, and that the figure came from TechCrunch citing sources. That is the level of certainty around the number. But the form of the deal itself carries information. The biggest company in authentication did not conclude that it already had enough capability to keep watching post-authorization behavior on its own.

## Not intelligence, but the ability to keep deploying intelligence

“The Next AI Moat Isn’t the Model: Applied Intuition CTO Points to the Real Bottleneck in Physical AI” describes the same transition at the engineering layer. In Ludwig’s formulation, deployed physical AI is the product of two variables: “model capability” and “the engineering throughput around the model.” The industry is pouring everything into the first variable, while the second remains stuck at a roughly 10-year-old level built around quarterly releases and integration teams of around 100 people.

The multiplicative form has an implication of its own. If this were a sum, a shortfall in one term could be offset by an excess in the other. In a product, it cannot. If the second variable is close to zero, then no matter how much the first variable rises, output stays close to zero. The hope that “if the model gets one more step smarter, this problem will be solved” does not hold under this formulation.

There is only one piece of quantitative support. The company’s internally built agent platform, Dana, sped up development cycles by about 20x. The article itself positions this as “the only quantitative support for this claim,” and that should be taken at face value. It is a single-company internal case study, so as evidence for generalization it is weak. But what matters here is not the size of the multiple. It is what was improved. What got faster was not the model, but the cycle of validating, integrating, and deploying the model.

## Power is not inventory, but a rate

“Data center power demand to reach 194 gigawatts by 2035” places the same structure at the physical layer. That would be a 253% increase from 2026 levels, about 20% of total U.S. electricity consumption, with the load concentrated in a handful of transmission regions including PJM.

A gigawatt is not a unit of quantity but a unit of rate. Electricity is not something you simply stockpile and use later; it has to be generated at the same moment it is consumed. So the correct reading of this forecast is not “prepare this much electricity,” but “build a system that can keep delivering power at this rate.” This also explains why concentration matters. Throughput constraints are determined by the narrowest cross-section, so if demand converges on a handful of regions, then even if total generation capacity across the United States is sufficient, the transmission capacity of those regions can still become the rate-limiting factor for the whole system.

## Presenting the common principle

These three facts suggest that AI’s constraints have shifted from a “gateway-type” form to a “throughput-type” form. Gateway constraints only have to be satisfied once: pass login verification, finish training the model, build the power plant. Throughput constraints must be satisfied continuously: keep monitoring behavior, keep integration and validation cycles turning, keep sending electricity. The three articles are reporting the same transition in different vocabularies from mutually unrelated places: the authorization layer, the engineering layer, and the physical layer.

Why has this shift happened? Because the unit cost of intelligence has fallen, and its output has changed from a discrete event into a continuous process. In the era when generation was expensive and rare, the act of using AI was naturally segmented: submit, execute, receive the result. In that world, verification, deployment, and power only needed to be arranged at each boundary. Once the assumption changes to agents running continuously, those boundaries disappear. In a system where the boundaries disappear, cost, guarantees, and capacity all have to be recalculated not “per request” but “per unit time.”

This principle is falsifiable. If constraints were still fundamentally gateway-type, then what we should observe would be problems solvable through one-time investments: one authentication design, one training run, one construction project. What we are actually observing is the opposite: the acquisition of continuous monitoring capability, a shortage of continuous integration capability, and a shortage of continuous transmission capacity. Conversely, it would be easy to show that this principle is wrong. It would be enough for one of the following to happen: an agent operating model that does not require post-authorization monitoring becomes dominant; deployment cycle constraints are removed by improvements in model capability alone; or energy storage effectively turns electricity into a stock rather than a flow. The principle is betting that none of those things will happen.

## Forecasts and implications

First, pricing will be reorganized around units of throughput. The shift in software pricing from seat counts toward execution volume is already underway, but this principle explains it not as a fashion in revenue models but as a structural consequence. Confidence here is high. The fact that Okta paid about $200 million for a continuous monitoring company is itself evidence that paying for flow capability is already a real thing.

Second, products that sell “a one-time gateway” will lose differentiation. Authentication, model evaluation, initial screening—products that issue certificates of passage will increasingly be demoted to accessory functions of products that monitor what happens after passage. Confidence here is moderate. The reason is that the direction of acquisitions is consistently moving from the gateway side toward the throughput side, though this should be discounted because it is still an extrapolation from a single case.

Third, geography is coming back. Throughput constraints are determined by the narrowest cross-section, so regions with thin transmission capacity cannot be rescued by software ingenuity. The observation that data center demand is concentrating in a handful of regions such as PJM implies that the location of the AI industry is beginning to subordinate itself to the shape of the power grid.

The practical implication can be summarized in one sentence. When inventorying the constraints your organization faces, recount them in two categories: things that are finished once they are satisfied, and things that must keep being satisfied. The former can be solved with investment. The latter can only be solved through operations, and the moment they stop, the system reverts. Many AI adoption plans are still written in the form of the former. What these three articles show is that the constraints that actually bite are now almost all of the latter kind.
