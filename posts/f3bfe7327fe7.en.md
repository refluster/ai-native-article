## Executive Summary

In his essay “You just hired a million bad employees.” published on July 14, 2026, Hebbia CEO George Sivulka argues that “AI was supposed to replace human labor, but the opposite happened.” The central thesis of this article is that the swarms of AI agents companies have adopted are, if left alone, “a horde of immature new hires” that create waste and confusion—and that the key lies not in model performance, but in reinventing management to coordinate them.

## The paradox: a world where humans have become cheaper than software

Sivulka begins with a deliberately provocative line: “For the first time in history, humans have become cheaper than software.” AI agents can be deployed cheaply and at scale, but when set loose without clearly defined objectives, they end up creating new kinds of work instead—monitoring, coordination, and evaluation. That is why, he says, “AI is creating more jobs than it destroys.”

To explain this structure, he invokes a railway collision from 1841—the case of the Western Railroad. As more trains were added to a single-track system and complexity exceeded what any human mind could keep track of, the railroad was forced for the first time to invent the role of “manager” and the organizational hierarchy that went with it. Sivulka’s point is straightforward: swarms of AI agents have reached the same critical threshold, and what is needed now is a reinvention of modern management structures.

## Token waste — “tokenmaxxing” and “loops”

Sivulka explicitly identifies forms of operational waste specific to running AI agents.

- **tokenmaxxing** — throwing computational resources (tokens) at a problem without understanding the amount of work actually required. He presents it as the analogue of “brute-force manpower” or overhiring in human organizations.
- **loops** — situations where agents keep calling themselves in an attempt to fix themselves, spinning in place. He describes this as “meetings about meetings,” and locates the root cause in “humans failing to clearly articulate the task.”
- **organizational bloat** — wasteful token consumption, he argues, is no different from overstaffing. Here he cites Elon Musk’s mass layoffs at Twitter as an example of cutting back an overgrown organization.
The key point is his reframing of AI waste not as a technical problem, but as a **management problem**.

## Reinventing management — “evals are the new OKRs”

So what should serve as the new primitive of management? The pillars Sivulka names are essentially human organizational tools translated into AI.

- **evals are the new OKRs** — rather than ad hoc prompting, structured evaluation (evals) that measures outcomes becomes the core of goal-setting for an AI workforce. Domains like coding, where evaluation criteria are already built in, will move first.
- **100X token** — just as the previous era was defined by the “10X engineer,” the next era will be defined by patterns of “100X token,” where optimized context produces outsized results.
- **context hoarding** — employees who seek to protect their proprietary knowledge will resist sharing information with AI for political reasons. This appears directly as friction in organizational change.
## The implication: the bet on “transformation companies”

In the end, Sivulka bets that the biggest opportunity lies not in horizontal SaaS, nor in the much-discussed “neofirm,” but in **transformation companies** that implement AI integration inside enterprises—and that this will become a $1 trillion-scale market.

The essay’s broader implication is that it rebuts the conventional view that AI adoption is mainly a matter of “model selection.” What matters is identifying how to use high-value tokens and putting rigorous evaluation systems in place first—in other words, the dividing line for companies going forward will not be spending more tokens, but the ability to **manage** an AI workforce.
