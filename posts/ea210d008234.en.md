## The Real Battleground in AI Competition Is Not the “Model,” but Control of the Business Operating Base

## — The Common Principle Running Through Palantir’s Ontology, System of Work, and Services-Led Growth

At first glance, Palantir’s ontology, the critique of the AI app “Yellow Brick Road,” and Services-Led Growth centered on the Forward Deployed Engineer may look like separate discussions. But in fact, they can all be explained by the same principle. In the AI era, competitive advantage is shifting away from smart models themselves and toward **who controls the “execution foundation” into which company-specific business context continuously flows**.

## Analysis 1: What Makes Palantir Different Is Not That It Layers Context on Top of Applications, but That It Models the Enterprise Itself

The core of the Palantir argument is straightforward. While Salesforce, Microsoft, ServiceNow, and Databricks build up context layers from existing apps and data platforms, Palantir **starts by creating a “world model of the enterprise.”** In other words, instead of merely connecting CRM records, tickets, logs, and tables, it maps **the causal relationships of the real world itself** into software: parts, factories, suppliers, personnel, constraints, approval flows, and inventory changes.

This is not just an abstract distinction. In the Airbus A350 example cited in the article, Palantir’s deployment is said to have **accelerated deliveries by 33%**. What matters is that this result is hard to explain as a mere improvement in dashboards or faster reporting. In a complex system such as aircraft manufacturing, value does not come from “seeing information,” but from **being able to replan across material constraints, process dependencies, quality issues, and frontline judgment**.

These facts suggest that the true source of AI value lies not in automating a single application, but in **an integrated representation of operational state across multiple departments**.

Palantir’s ontology is powerful for another reason: it does not stop at supplying RAG-style context to “feed” enterprise knowledge into an LLM. The ontology manages **permissions, workflows, and relationships among real-world objects**, defining who can approve what, which changes affect which KPI, and which data is used in which decisions.

This is not merely a “context layer.” It is, rather, an execution environment that defines **the boundary conditions within which AI is allowed to act inside the enterprise**. These facts suggest that the scarcest asset in enterprise AI is not the model, but **an actionable representation of the enterprise**.

## Analysis 2: Avoiding the “Yellow Brick Road” Means Controlling the Flow of Work, Not Competing in Horizontal Models

The article on AI app strategy and avoiding the “Yellow Brick Road” argues that it is dangerous, in the AI era, to remain attached to the assumptions of traditional software development. As the title suggests, the “obvious path” everyone walks down—a horizontal race on model performance, superficial competition in chat UI, and a rush toward general-purpose copilots—easily becomes a red ocean.

The key point in that argument is that the defensibility of an AI app is determined not by **whether it owns a model**, but by **whether it controls a System of Work**. A System of Work is the end-to-end flow through which work is created, handed off, judged, executed, and audited. Put differently, what matters is not whether you can insert yourself into the moment when a user “learns something,” but whether you can insert yourself into the moment when they **move something forward**.

Here the connection to the Palantir argument becomes clear. Palantir’s ontology represents the enterprise in terms of objects and relationships, while the System of Work argument understands the enterprise as **a set of recurring operational loops**. The representation differs, but the target is the same: **the control point that transforms AI from something that answers into something that advances work**.

These facts suggest that what carries the most value in the AI application market is not the UI or the inference API, but software embedded at **the points where work begins, branches, gets approved, and gets executed**.

Take a basic sales-assist AI: it often stops at drafting emails or producing summaries. But if it can advance a deal across win probability, legal review, pricing approval, supply constraints, and deployment capacity, then it is no longer just a “feature”—it is a System of Work. Even if the model improves somewhat, that connection to operations cannot easily be replaced.

In short, **competitive advantage comes not from intelligence itself, but from controlling the seams in business operations where intelligence can be inserted**.

## Analysis 3: Services-Led Growth Is Not a Margin-Sacrificing Compromise, but a Strategy to Seize the Points Where Data Enters

The third article redefines how services should be positioned in enterprise AI. Traditionally, implementation support with a strong professional-services component has often been avoided due to concerns about scalability and gross margins. But this article makes the opposite case: startups can use **services as a strategic wedge**, deepening customer contact and discovering repeatable product opportunities. At the center of this is the **Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)**.

The value of the FDE is not simply that they help with implementation. It lies in working side by side with the customer on the ground to uncover which data is broken, which approvals are bottlenecks, which operational exceptions occur every time, and where AI can intervene safely. This is neither sales, nor customer success, nor R&D. It is **the role of entering the customer’s internal operations and transforming “unstructured reality” into something that can be productized**.

What matters here is not that services themselves generate revenue. Their true value is that, through service activity, they allow you to control **the points where data enters the system**. Customer exception handling, informal rules, and person-dependent judgment criteria do not appear in a SaaS specification document from the outset. They can only be captured through direct intervention in the field.

These facts suggest that the most valuable training material in enterprise AI is neither public data nor existing logs, but **tacit knowledge that can only be observed during the implementation process**.

When you connect this to the fact that Palantir has long treated an FDE-style organization as a strength, and to the reality that controlling the System of Work requires a deep understanding of frontline operating pathways, it becomes clear that services are not a cost center. They are **a scouting, embedding, and learning apparatus for gaining control over the business operating base**.

## The Common Principle: The True Unit of Competition for AI Companies Is Not the “Model,” but the Loop of Collecting, Representing, and Executing Business Context

Put all of these facts together, and the three articles point to the same conclusion.

The **33% acceleration in deliveries** seen in Palantir’s A350 case is the result of representing business activity in terms of objects and constraints, then tying decision-making to execution.

The “Yellow Brick Road” argument says that horizontal model competition does not create differentiation, and that the winners will be those who burrow deepest into the **System of Work**.

The Services-Led Growth argument says that entering the customer’s frontline environment through the FDE, and capturing tacit knowledge and exception handling that exist before productization, is what will decide the outcome.

These facts suggest that the unit of competition in the AI era has shifted from **model performance** to **the loop that collects company-specific business context, structures it, and connects it to execution**.

Put even more simply, the winners will not be the companies that have “good AI.” They will be **the companies that control a closed loop in which data continuously flows in from inside the customer’s operations, that context is structured, and AI actions are returned to those operations**.

There are three crucial points to this principle.

First, context cannot be a static data integration layer; it must be a dynamic model of operations that are constantly changing.

Second, that context must not exist merely for viewing; it must be usable for approval, execution, and replanning.

Third, creating that loop requires more than a product alone at the start; frontline intervention through FDEs and services is indispensable.

So what is clear. The barrier to entry in enterprise AI is not the ability to procure foundation models, but **how deeply you can observe the customer’s work, standardize it, and turn it into a continuously operating foundation**. In other words, the key to market dominance lies not in software sales, but in **the colonization of the business operating base**.

## Forecast and Implications: The Next Winners Will Not Be “AI Feature Companies,” but “Business OS Companies”

From this principle, three changes are highly likely to unfold.

### 1. General-purpose AI apps will commoditize rapidly on price

The probability is high. The reason is that differences in model performance are narrowing, and horizontal functions such as summarization, generation, search, and chat are easy to substitute. What readers should pay attention to is not the polish of the product demo, but **which business events that AI can trigger, and which systems it can write back into**.

### 2. The evaluation axis for enterprise software will shift from “seat count” to “depth of operational intervention”

This is also highly likely. For CRM, ITSM, data platforms, and collaboration tools alike, simply layering on an AI assistant will no longer be enough. The contest will shift to who can control the greatest number of approvals, exceptions, and execution flows. Palantir-style ontology, ServiceNow-style workflow, Microsoft-style work surface, and Salesforce-style customer touchpoint are all likely to converge into a broader **“battle for the System of Work.”**

### 3. Services-Led Growth will become not a temporary tactic, but the standard go-to-market for AI companies

The probability is medium to high. Especially in businesses dealing with complex operations, the FDE-like role will become not a post-sales function, but **a core capability spanning product discovery, implementation, and operational optimization**. Companies that cut services away because they are focused only on gross margin may look cleaner in the short term, but over the medium to long term they are likely to lose the most important learning loop.

There are four practical implications for readers.

First, do not begin your AI strategy with “which model should we use?” Start by designing around **which operational loop you intend to control**.

Second, internal data integration should not be pursued for RAG alone, but as **ontology design that connects decision-making with execution**.

Third, rather than launching PoC after PoC, you should use FDE-like talent to prioritize **the observation and standardization of exception handling**.

Fourth, when selecting vendors, do not focus on the number of features. Evaluate **how deeply they can write into frontline operations, how they handle permissions and auditability, and whether they can continuously learn business context**.

The conclusion is simple. In the AI era, the most valuable companies will not be those with the smartest models. They will be **the companies that can enter most deeply into the customer’s work, absorb its context, and control the loop that returns that context into execution**. Palantir’s ontology, the System of Work strategy, and Services-Led Growth all point to that same central truth.
