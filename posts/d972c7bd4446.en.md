Anthropic's year of hard-won lessons on commerce-agent design, a viral thread diagnosing "AI fatigue," and an open letter from over 100 companies demanding governments surge defenses against AI-driven cyberattacks. These three developments look unrelated, but they share a single underlying principle: **AI takes over execution, not judgment — and the burden of judgment doesn't vanish, it simply resurfaces wherever no one has paid for it in advance.**

## The engineering layer: prepaying judgment into the harness

Anthropic's "The Anatomy of Effective Commerce Agents" is a working example of prepaying judgment cost at design time. After a year validating the approach in retail, travel, and telecom, the company found that effective commerce agents don't converge on fleets of domain-specific subagents or an intent router — they converge on a single, shared skeleton: **one agent loop** equipped with skills and tools.

What matters here is the split: latency and cost — the "execution" side — are solved with an engineering lever, prompt caching (a 90–99% hit rate). Safety — the "judgment" side — is never delegated to how well a prompt is written.

### The judgment the harness absorbs

Concretely, safety is guaranteed by mechanisms outside the model itself: server-issued ID verification and staged approval flows. Anthropic embedded the answer to "can this agent's judgment be trusted?" into the system architecture, not into individual instructions. The judgment cost hasn't disappeared — it has been **prepaid** into the harness.

It's also telling that after a year of validation across three very different verticals, the skeleton converged on a single loop rather than fragmenting into per-domain subagents or routers. Building specialized subagent fleets per vertical, or routing requests by type, sounds like the natural instinct — but Anthropic's finding is that this complexity actually blurs where judgment lives and inflates verification cost. A single loop carrying skills and tools concentrates "where to verify what" into one place, which is precisely what makes approval flows and verification logic tractable to build. Prepaying judgment cost turns out to be inseparable from architectural simplicity itself.

## The individual layer: judgment is all that's left, and it becomes exhaustion

The same structure shows up in individual work. Product-development expert Shin's analysis of "AI fatigue" is a textbook case of what happens when that prepayment never happens. Using AI should shorten the time a task takes — yet by the end of the day, only a heavy fatigue remains. His conclusion: what AI automated was "work," not "judgment."

### The five burdens

The burdens he names are oversight (watching output continuously), decision-making (choosing among options), verification (checking facts and logic), incompleteness (picking up what AI left half-done), and updating (redoing assumptions every time circumstances shift) — all of them concentrated in the moment a human has to decide whether AI's output is actually right. Time-on-task shrinks, but judgment cost stays the same, or is amplified, and lands squarely on the individual.

This stands in sharp contrast to Anthropic's environment. Anthropic's agent externalizes oversight and verification into server-side ID checks and approval flows — judgment is moved out of any single person's head. The individual Shin describes has no such externalized receptacle: all five burdens are processed with one person's own cognitive resources. Tellingly, his prescription isn't to use AI less — it's to redesign judgment itself. That is exactly what Anthropic did at the harness level, attempted by an individual inside their own workflow.

## The institutional layer: the same gap where prepayment never happened

The same structure recurs at the level of the state. OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and more than 100 companies in total — including Broadcom, Capital One, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, General Motors, IBM, Mastercard, Oracle, Robinhood, Shopify, and Visa — jointly called on governments and industry to surge society-wide defenses against AI-driven cyberattacks. The letter asks governments to accelerate a "trusted access" program granting advance access to powerful models.

But the U.S. cyber-defense agency the letter is effectively addressed to, CISA, had roughly a third of its staff cut over the prior year. Offensive execution capacity scales exponentially with AI; defensive "judgment infrastructure" — the institutional capacity to assess threats, authorize responses, and own accountability — is shrinking in the opposite direction.

It's also worth noting the signatories span far beyond AI developers: financial firms like Capital One, Mastercard, and Visa; security firms like Cloudflare and CrowdStrike; manufacturers like General Motors. That breadth confirms vulnerability to AI-accelerated attacks isn't confined to one sector — any entity lacking judgment infrastructure is equally exposed, regardless of industry. The gap between the letter's urgency and the defensive reality on the ground is the same evidence, at national scale, that Anthropic's engineering layer prepaid for and the state has not yet paid.

## The unifying principle: accelerating execution doesn't erase judgment cost — it transfers it

The engineering layer's system design, the individual's cognitive load, and the state's defensive posture — all three show the same structure: as AI dramatically lowers the cost of execution, the remaining cost of judgment, verification, and trust concentrates somewhere in the system. Anthropic's case proves that cost can be absorbed by prepaying it into the design layer, the harness. Meanwhile, the exhausted individual Shin describes and the CISA stripped of a third of its staff occupy the same structural position: the side to which judgment cost's payment has been deferred.

These facts suggest that the raw acceleration of execution AI makes possible is a benefit to everyone in isolation — but unless someone, somewhere, deliberately redesigns the infrastructure for judgment, verification, and trust, that unpaid cost will always land on someone else, whether an individual or a state institution. AI's progress doesn't shrink the total amount of judgment cost in the system. If no one chooses who prepays it, it simply concentrates wherever the capacity to pay is weakest.

## Forecast and implications: investment in judgment infrastructure becomes the dividing line

If this principle holds, the following shifts should become observable over the next one to two years.

First, what separates organizations that succeed with AI adoption from those that don't will increasingly be investment in "judgment infrastructure," not raw model capability. Anthropic's harness investment is an early example; organizations that make the same kind of design choice — automated verification, explicit approval flows — should be able to run AI agents in production more reliably than those that don't.

Second, at the individual level, "use AI less" as a fatigue remedy will keep failing to stick, and tools and practices that address the redesign of judgment itself — the granularity of delegation, the timing of approval, automated verification — should keep growing over the coming year. Organizations that measure success purely by time saved will keep reproducing the exhaustion Shin describes.

Third, at the policy level, the gap between shrinking defensive staffing (as at CISA) and growing AI-driven threat capacity is unlikely to close on its own. Mechanisms like "trusted access" that delegate judgment between public and private sectors will likely get built — but there is no guarantee that build-out will outrun the actual acceleration of attacks, which carries real risk that the cost of a slow start shows up as concrete harm before the infrastructure catches up.

The practical takeaway is simple: when measuring AI's impact, add "how much judgment increased" as a metric alongside "how much work-time decreased." And organizations should spend less effort polishing prompts and more building the judgment infrastructure — approval and verification flows — that actually carries the weight. Judgment cost doesn't disappear. The only real choice is whether you prepay it, or push it onto someone else.
