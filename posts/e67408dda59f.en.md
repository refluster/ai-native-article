In the first two weeks of November 2026, three announcements arrived that look unrelated. Google added "TPU 8tx" and "TPU 8i Max" to its eighth-generation TPU line and said it would ship four TPU systems this year (9 November). The US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) published its first framework for how bank examiners assess AI use, and chose not to create a separate AI risk category (4 November). Olivia Moore and Justine Moore of a16z argued that AI agents are becoming the main users of the internet (11 November). Semiconductors, bank supervision and consumer commerce are rarely discussed together. Yet the same principle explains all three: **the unit by which value and responsibility are measured is moving from the part or the technology itself to how it is used and what it causes.**

## Semiconductors: not the chip's speed, but a system fitted to the job

The article on Google's four-system TPU line makes a point that is not about how fast any single chip is. The 8tx is for confidential inference, protecting data even while it is in use, in hardware. The 8i Max uses two chips to double memory bandwidth for very large contexts. The two do not sit on one axis of "faster chip". One is built for the use "run inference on data nobody may see"; the other for "hold a very long context and respond".

According to the same article, Google showed it can rearrange package, network and software to ship a dedicated system within months. The unit of design has moved from the chip, a part, to the whole system that finishes a specific job. If that reading holds, the buyer's metric changes too. Chip-level benchmark figures matter less than which system is assigned to your own job, confidential data or long context.

## Financial supervision: not the kind of technology, but the use and the weight of an error

The article on the OCC's first AI guidance for examiners shows the same structure from the regulator's side. The OCC did not create AI as a standalone risk category. It looks at two things instead: how AI is used in day-to-day work, and how large the damage would be if the AI is wrong. This is not a binding new rule; it is a framework for examiners.

The notable choice is the decision *not* to create the category. Grouping risk under the label "AI" lets an examiner check only whether the technology is present. The OCC declined that. Whether the same AI is used for light assistance or for a decision where an error is costly, it adjusts how deeply it looks. The unit of evaluation moves from AI, the part, to its use and the consequences of its errors. The match with the semiconductor case is clear. Google ships "systems fitted to a job" rather than "chips"; the OCC measures "use and consequence" rather than "technology".

## Commerce: from firms that block bots to firms that win bot purchases

In the article on agents as customers, a16z argues that companies must move from "blocking bots" to "winning bots' purchases". Its evidence is measured: AI-referred traffic to Shopify stores up eightfold year on year (Q1 2026), agentic-browser traffic up eightfold between August and October, and coding agents as an earlier precedent.

Traditionally a bot was classified by a technical attribute, "not a human", and was easily excluded. a16z's claim is to drop that classification and ask whether the visitor produces a purchase. The unit of the customer moves from a kind of being, human, to the party that carries out the purchase, human or agent. Here too the habit of measuring by part (is the source a person or a bot?) is replaced by measuring by use and outcome (does that visit lead to a purchase?).

## The principle: a part's attributes are becoming a weak unit of judgment

Set side by side, the three facts share a form. Google split its product by job, not by chip superiority. The OCC set supervisory depth by use and weight of error, not by the AI label. a16z told firms to see customers by whether a purchase happens, not by whether the visitor is a person or a bot. Each takes "what is it?", the attribute of the part, out of the primary axis of judgment and puts "how is it used and what does it cause?", the attribute of the context, in front.

Why would that happen? When parts multiply and their combinations change every few months, a part's attribute has a short shelf life as a guide to judgment. If TPUs can ship as dedicated systems within months, a chip generation's ranking is out of date once it appears on a spec sheet. If AI is used across everything from routine tasks to consequential decisions, the question "is AI in use?" loses information. If more access comes through agents, "human or machine?" says nothing about the likelihood of a purchase. These facts suggest that use and outcome are a more stable unit of judgment than a part's attributes.

The principle can be refuted. If chip generation, the presence of AI and the type of visitor keep determining what buyers choose, how deeply supervisors look and how firms set traffic policy, the principle is wrong. All three sources are announcements or commentary, and whether practice follows cannot be confirmed from them alone.

## Predictions and implications

If the principle holds, three changes are plausible. First, system vendors' announcements will shift from lists of specifications to "which job gets what". As with the 8tx and 8i Max, use names move closer to product names. The ground is the pace at which Google splits by use, four systems within the year. I judge this likely, but whether other firms adopt the same form cannot be told from these three articles.

Second, regulation and supervision will probably be tiered by use and weight of harm rather than by technology name. Because the OCC framework is not binding, its effect depends on how examiners apply it. Uncertainty is large here. If calls for a standalone "AI risk category" strengthen, this outlook breaks.

Third, firms will move away from cutting access by human or bot and toward welcoming behaviour that leads to purchases. a16z's measurements show that demand in this direction is already moving. The need to defend against harmful bots does not vanish, though; the task is to redraw the line between "blocking" and "winning" by outcome.

One practical implication: when choosing anything in your own work, replace the unit of evaluation "what is it?" with "what does it do?". If you choose a chip, write the job first, confidential inference or long context. If you adopt AI, write the use and the damage if it errs. If you count customers, count the party that causes the purchase. Swapping that order alone brings differences onto the page that spec sheets and labels could not show.
