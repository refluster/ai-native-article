## Executive Summary

The essay “You just hired a million bad employees.,” published by a16z.news on 2026年7月14日, flips the premise that AI replaces labor and argues that “for the first time in history, humans became cheaper than software.” At its core, the argument is about management, not technology: wasted tokens are a problem of instruction quality, not model capability, and organizations of agents break down in the same ways human organizations do. The author’s prescription is Eval (evaluation suites) to measure that dysfunction, and the essay concludes that implementing them company by company as “AI transformation” is the next great business opportunity.

## A Reversed Premise — Humans Became Cheaper Than Software

The essay opens by rejecting the conventional view. It presents two core facts.

- “AI was supposed to replace human labor. Instead, the opposite happened” — “for the first time in history, humans became *cheaper* than software.”
- AI is “creating *more* jobs than it destroys.”
The essay supports this claim with two charts. One is “Token spend per employee at top firms,” and the other is “Headcount growth after AI adoption.” In other words, the observation is that the more companies adopt AI, the more both spending and headcount rise.

From there, the author draws a central conclusion: the problem with AI is not “performance” but “management.” “We have effectively given every employee — including the worst ones — unlimited headcount and budget.” If you have handed out unlimited headcount, then of course the real question is how you make people use it.

## Management Born of Railroads — The Lesson of the 1830s

The author compares today’s confusion to railroad construction in the 1830s. This is the section of the essay with the most concrete historical detail.

- In the 1830s, the arrival of railroads triggered one of the largest infrastructure buildouts in the world, and total railroad track in the United States “120Xed” in 10 years.
- But the system broke down. On October 5, 1841, on the Western Railroad in Massachusetts, two trains collided because of a simple coordination failure, killing passengers.
- As growing complexity outstripped the ability of individual conductors to manage it, railroad companies began a decades-long effort to “hire managers by region, define roles in writing, and build clear hierarchies with reporting lines.” In this way, “modern management was born.”
- As a result, railroads became one of the world’s first billion-dollar industries and at their peak accounted for “roughly 60%” of the stock market.
From this history, the essay derives a clear proposition: “Managing AI is harder than managing humans because AI scales dysfunction instantly.” Just as train crashes gave rise to hierarchical management, fleets of agents require similar control mechanisms. And “agent workforces fail in the same ways human workforces do.” Understanding these “7 major parallels” between the two is, the essay argues, the key to unlocking “the next trillion dollars” of value creation through AI.

## Loops and Token Waste — 80% Produces Nothing

The essay is at its sharpest when it attributes token waste to the human side’s inability to give clear instructions. It offers the following observations.

- “The tokenmaxxing discourse cycle completed in less than a month.” But spending itself was never the real issue.
- “Maybe 1 in 100 employees knows how to give AI context.” Very few people can clearly verbalize a process and have the patience to deal with a polluted context window.
- Give the other 99 an agent environment, and you get “loops.” Across every harness — Claude Code/Cowork, Copilot, Karpathy’s Autoresearch — loops are an emergency workaround for the fact that “almost nobody can prompt well.”
- “You are spending tokens on spending tokens.”
The essay maps this directly onto the pathology of human organizations. “The majority of workers are not having meaningful impact on the business” — they are participants in loops, stamping approvals at each layer and hiring more gears for the machine that exists only to keep the machine turning. As evidence, the essay cites the example that “Elon cut 80% of X’s staff and it still worked better.” From that comes the parallel proposition: “Just as 80% of employees do nothing, 80% of tokens today do nothing.”

That said, the essay acknowledges one area where AI truly beats humans: scalability. Human scale demands enormous energy for hiring, onboarding, and attrition, whereas token scale is instantaneous. Which is exactly why bad management is so expensive. The central concept introduced here is “100X tokens.”

- “10X engineers built the last generation of companies. 100X tokens will build the next generation.”
- “Tokens are more expensive than humans on average. But good tokens are cheap at scale” — and management, the author argues, is what converts one into the other.
## Employees Won’t Hand Over the Secret Sauce — The Politics of AI Adoption

The essay argues that even before technical constraints, internal company politics block AI adoption. Its proposition is simple: “Employees do not want to teach AI their secret sauce.”

As evidence, it points to friction happening even inside tech companies. Meta employees — who hold stock and should be strongly incentivized by the company’s AI success — are said to be “outraged” that the company wants to use employee context as training data. The author frames this as “a preview of what is about to happen in every industry.”

- Tribal knowledge has been job security for centuries. Medieval guilds kept production methods secret.
- “AI is the first technology that asks workers to hand all of it over at once.”
- “Nobody trains their replacement for free.”
The conclusion is that the more a person controls 100X tokens, the less incentive they have to give that up. “Emotionally, structurally, and politically, companies are wired to reject the most important technology for their own future.” Therefore, the best way to manage a token workforce is the same as managing human workers: “define what good looks like.”

## Eval as the Most Important Asset — and the Rise of “AI Transformation Companies”

The solution the essay prescribes is Eval (evaluation). Again, it begins from concrete observation.

- The only breakout case that escaped politics was coding. It expanded the pie and made engineers better.
- “The mechanism was Eval. 99% of AI revenue today is coding because coding has Eval built in. Code either works or it doesn’t.”
- Cross-functional AI adoption only takes off once someone builds the needed Eval. The essence of management, the author argues, is encoding ambiguous human processes and “expressing qualitative as quantitative.”
From there, the author moves to an investment conclusion. “A company’s Eval suite will become its most valuable asset.” Just as OKRs are the key to driving a human workforce toward optimal output, Eval will be the key to directing a token workforce that scales infinitely. And because “no two companies will have the same Eval set,” Eval becomes a source of competitive advantage — organizations running generic Eval or generic agents will have no edge.

The essay closes by drawing a map of the business opportunity. The starting premise is the hard reality that “Nobody has AI working reliably yet.”

- Silicon Valley is funding “Neofirms/AI Native Services,” betting against incumbents and targeting “\$21 trillion” in service spend across the knowledge economy. The bet assumes existing companies will be unable to transition because they are trapped by their own politics and processes.
- But the author takes the opposite side. “‘AI transformation companies’ will be 10x bigger than any neofirm.” The reason is Jevons paradox: every use case a company adopts generates 10 more use cases, so the demand for transformation keeps growing as AI adoption spreads.
- The example offered is Palantir. It looks, on the surface, like “the company most vulnerable to Claude,” hand-building custom enterprise apps at half-a-trillion-dollar scale, yet its stock remains strong. In the author’s words, “Palantir was not selling software. It was selling transformation.”
And then the final proposition: “Encoding each company’s unique nuance into agents may become the single largest economic task of the next decade.” In the gold rush, people said to sell picks and shovels, so we built infrastructure. Then came Service-as-a-Software, so we built neofirms. We have enough infrastructure and enough services now. “The job now is to make the trains run on time.” Even now that humans are cheaper than software, “someone still has to decide what each should do.”
