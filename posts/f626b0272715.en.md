The era of asking “which model is the smartest” is quietly drawing to a close. Three facts that seem unrelated at first glance illuminate that shift from the same angle. First, Anthropic has rewritten the economics of code migration—the Bun Zig→Rust migration, covering 1 million lines, was completed in under 2 weeks at an API-price equivalent of about $165,000, and a 165,000-line Python→TypeScript migration was finished over a weekend (L2 “Code migration goes from ‘multi-year project’ to weeks”). Second, on July 8, 2026, The Kobeissi Letter declared that agentic AI had moved from “chatbots that answer” to a “software layer that connects to enterprise data and applications,” evolving into “digital employees,” and estimated the market at more than $155 billion by 2030 (L2 “Agentic AI becomes ‘digital employees’”). Third, Databricks measured on real tasks from its own multi-million-line codebase that the open model GLM 5.2 was statistically tied with Opus 4.8 on quality, while also costing less per task at $1.28 versus $1.94 (L2 “Databricks measures coding agents on its own multi-million-line codebase”). In fact, all three can be explained by the same principle—the locus of value, and the scale used to measure it, has moved from the model itself to outside the model.

## Don’t fix the model—fix the loop that runs the model

The core lesson of Anthropic’s migration method is that the breakthrough did not come from humans manually cleaning up generated code, but from fixing the “process that produces code” itself—the loop (L2 “Code migration goes from ‘multi-year project’ to weeks”). The six-step method laid out in that article is not about improving the quality of a single generation; it is about redesigning the generation→validation→correction loop. That is precisely why, even at the scale of 1 million lines, outcomes depend less on the model’s one-shot intelligence than on how many times the loop can be run.

What is decisive here is the collapse in decision-making cost. The article says the downside of a migration has fallen as far as “the worst case is deleting a branch.” If it doesn’t work, you throw it away—in that world, you no longer need the kind of “existential reason” that used to justify starting in the first place, the sense that “this system has to be migrated or it will die.” The reason language migrations that once took multiple years can now shrink to a matter of weeks is not so much that models became smarter, but that the cost of cleaning up after mistakes became cheaper. The value that moved was not inside the model; it moved in the structure of decision-making around the model.

## Vendors’ headline numbers price the model and miss the process

The Kobeissi post casts the same shift in reverse (L2 “Agentic AI becomes ‘digital employees’”). What matters here is not so much the substance of the claim as the absence of support behind it. The post calls agentic AI “digital employees” and estimates a market of more than $155 billion by 2030, yet the post itself discloses that it is a “Paid partnership,” and the number circulates without any cited source or visible assumptions behind the estimate. In other words, this is not an independent forecast, but a claim made in a promotional context.

What should not be overlooked is that headline numbers of this kind almost always share the same structure: they put a price on the model—or the category—and skip over the process. The story of moving “from chatbots to digital employees” narrates a leap in capability as if it were an intrinsic property of the model. But what the same post also admits is that the focus has shifted to a “software layer that connects to enterprise data and applications”—that is, that the locus of value now sits not in the model but in the connection layer outside it. TAM, as a proxy metric, crushes that outside layer into one giant rounded number.

## Real cost can only be measured on your own real tasks

Databricks’ internal benchmark provided the sharpest demonstration of this “proxy metric trap” (L2 “Databricks measures coding agents on its own multi-million-line codebase”). First, the open model GLM 5.2 was statistically tied with Opus 4.8 on quality, yet cheaper per task at $1.28 versus $1.94. Second, and more importantly, it found that per-token pricing is an inadequate metric for the real cost of a task: even with the same model, differences in the harness—the execution environment that runs the model—caused per-task cost to vary by more than 2x.

Together, those two findings mean that the vendor-supplied proxy metric of “per-token price” can mislead practical decision-making. Cheap tokens do not guarantee cheap tasks—because real cost is determined by the countless generations, retries, and validation passes in between, in other words by the design of the harness. And this true value became visible only because Databricks measured on real tasks drawn from its own multi-million-line codebase. Not through vendor benchmarks, and not through public leaderboards, but through first-party measurement alone. The company’s conclusion that “model selection alone does not complete the efficiency story” is the most concrete testimony yet that the scale of value has shifted outside the model.

## The common principle—models have become inputs, while process has become the differentiator

Put the three facts side by side, and a single principle comes into focus. **The subject of decision-making has shifted from “the performance and price of the standalone model” to “the process that runs the model (the loop, harness, and connection layer), and the measured results of that process on a company’s own real tasks.”** These facts suggest the following: models are rapidly becoming commoditized inputs, and what separates outcomes is the design and measurement that happen outside the model.

The economics of migration (loop design and decision cost), the hollowness of the market narrative (TAM obscuring the movement of value toward the connection layer), and the direct measurement of coding agents (the harness doubling unit cost)—all three contain the same asymmetry. Proxy metrics that put a price on the model fail to capture reality, while direct measurement of the process does capture it. The fact that an open model reached a tie with a top-tier model at Databricks symbolizes that conclusion. The flatter the hierarchy of models becomes, the more the remaining differences can only reside outside the model—in how you run the loop, how you design the harness, and how you connect it to specific tasks. The headline numbers vendors present—the barrier to starting without an “existential reason,” the $155 billion TAM, per-token pricing—are all proxy metrics that overlook that outside layer.

## Forecast and implications—the organizations that can measure will separate themselves from those that cannot

If this principle is correct, the coming divide is likely to be determined not by “which model you choose” but by whether you have the instrumentation to measure on your own real tasks. Just as Databricks used first-party measurement to expose the deceptiveness of per-token pricing, organizations with real measurement will discount vendor headline numbers and redirect investment toward harness design. Organizations without measurement, by contrast, will take proxy metrics such as TAM, per-token pricing, and leaderboard rank at face value, spend their energy switching models, and continue missing 2x swings in real cost. The basis for that probability is that three independent contexts—migration, market rhetoric, and internal benchmarking—all point in the same direction.

The practical implications are clear. First, shift time away from model-selection debates and toward the design of loops and harnesses, along with direct measurement on your own tasks. “Model selection alone does not complete the efficiency story” (Databricks) can be read as a guide to investment allocation. Second, whenever you see vendor-originated numbers—TAM, per-token pricing, capability labels like “digital employees”—ask what exactly they are pricing, and what they are skipping over. In many cases, what gets skipped is the process, and that skipped-over layer is exactly where the real cost and value reside. Third, build for lower decision-making cost first—a structure equivalent to “the worst case is deleting a branch,” where mistakes can be discarded cheaply. Only organizations that have that structure will be able to experiment quickly and learn quickly in a world where models have become commoditized. Model intelligence is now a given. The contest will be decided between those who can measure what lies outside the model and those who cannot.
