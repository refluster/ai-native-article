## Executive Summary

The paper "The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex" (arXiv:2606.26959v1 [econ.GN], June 25, 2026), released by a research team including OpenAI, compares real-world Codex usage logs across three groups—individual users, organizations, and OpenAI employees—and shows at scale that the spread of agentic AI is not just about which tool people use, but about how work itself is structured. The shift is rapid but highly uneven: as of June 11, 2026, Codex accounted for 99.8% of output tokens inside OpenAI, 63.3% for organizational accounts, and 16.5% for individual accounts—an order-of-magnitude gap across groups. The authors conclude that conventional adoption metrics such as active user counts are becoming less informative in the era of agentic AI.

## Study Design — Comparing Three Groups

The paper is authored by six researchers: Drew Johnston, David Holtz, Alex Martin Richmond, Christopher Ong, Prasanna Tambe, and Aaron Chatterji. Their affiliations are OpenAI, Columbia Business School, the Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania, and Duke University’s Fuqua School of Business. The study uses a pipeline that extracts aggregated, anonymized metrics through automated classifiers, without researchers reading individual messages.

- **Individual users** (Free / Go / Plus / Pro plans) — a broad but shallow early-adopter layer.
- **Organizational users** (Business / Enterprise plans) — a group whose adoption is shaped by internal workflows, permissions, and security requirements.
- **OpenAI employees** — an “environment with minimal adoption friction,” with no usage limits and active knowledge sharing. The authors emphasize that this is not meant to represent a typical organization, but rather “a view of the future if friction disappears.”
The distinction between conversational and agentic usage is itself not absolute. In the week immediately preceding June 11, 2026, at least one external tool was called in 60.3% of Codex turns and 21.9% of ChatGPT turns.

## The Shift Is Rapid but Uneven — The Sharpest Gap Appears in Output Tokens

In the first half of 2026, Codex weekly active users grew more than 5x, with the steepest growth occurring outside software developers, the group originally assumed to be its core users. But the depth of adoption varies sharply across groups.

- Share of output tokens attributable to Codex (as of June 11, 2026): OpenAI **99.8%**, organizations **63.3%**, individuals **16.5%**.
- Inside OpenAI, Codex has effectively replaced ChatGPT for work use.
- From November 1, 2025, to June 11, 2026, the median worker’s output tokens increased by at least 10x in **every job category** inside OpenAI.
The implication is that even the way adoption is measured has to change. What looks, by user-count metrics, like “a technology beginning to be used in some places” can already be, when measured by delegated work volume, “the primary means of production in certain environments.”

## What Is Being Delegated Is Software Production — But Not Just Code Generation

Looking at task composition, software-related work accounts for the largest share in every group. But that does not mean code implementation alone: the mix extends across the full software-production lifecycle, including code understanding, change verification, environment setup, repository management, and documentation generation.

The authors describe this not as “consultation” but as “delegated production,” contrasting it with prior research on conversational AI usage patterns, where “asking” accounted for nearly half of all prompts. Task complexity is also rising: the share of individual users who submitted at least one task estimated to take an experienced human more than 8 hours has increased roughly 10x since the start of the year.

Inside OpenAI, where adoption is deepest, usage has spread further into non-engineering domains including research, planning, communication, data analysis, product work, recruiting, and sales.

## Intensive Users Are Restructuring Work Around Parallelism, Long Duration, and Reusability

What the paper emphasizes most is not the quantity of use, but its **structure**. It highlights three observable indicators.

- **Parallel execution** (week immediately preceding June 11, 2026) — 67.4% of organizational users and 63.9% of individual users did not use a parallel turn even once. Inside OpenAI, by contrast, only 10.7% used a single workflow only, while **28.6% managed 5 or more simultaneous agents**. Across the full user base, more than 10% handled 3 or more concurrent agents at some point during the week.
- **Runtime** — On June 11, 2026, the median OpenAI employee had Codex turns running for 2.5 hours. By contrast, employees at the 99th percentile were running the equivalent of about **71 hours** of agent runtime per average day, an increase of about 88% since April 7, 2026. At that same 99th percentile, the increase was about 25% for organizational users and about 50% for individual users.
- **Systematization through skills** (the 7 days ending June 11, 2026) — At least one skill was invoked by 25.7% of individuals, 30.4% of organizations, and **96.2%** inside OpenAI. Across all users, that figure rose from 5.4% on March 1, 2026, to 26.6% on June 11. Inside OpenAI, 50.9% of collaboration conversations involved skills.
Growth is being driven by plugins and custom skills. The authors describe the latter as tools that “embed local procedural context not fully covered by standardized plugins, such as team-specific writing conventions and recurring reports.” The large gap in custom-skill adoption suggests that **the more saturated an environment is with organization-specific context, the greater the value of systematizing workflows**.

## A Comparison With Electrification — But With Radically Lower Redesign Costs

The paper draws a comparison to the shift from steam power to electricity. In the early phase of electrification, many factories simply replaced the central steam engine with a central electric motor, while keeping factory layouts and work patterns intact. The large productivity gains only appeared after factories were redesigned around distributed power from small motors—reworking plant layout, task sequencing, and the production floor itself—and that transition took decades.

From this, the authors derive two implications. First, **when organizations have not yet discovered, adopted, and scaled new production processes, the short-term effects of agentic AI may underestimate its long-term potential**. Second, because digital production processes do not require rebuilding physical equipment, experimentation costs are low, which could make adoption faster than past transitions involving general-purpose technologies.

In the conclusion, the authors argue that as delegation advances, organizations will need to rethink how work is allocated, reviewed, and coordinated. They suggest that the center of gravity in many roles may shift from “doing each task yourself” to directing, monitoring, and integrating agent output. In that framing, judgment, supervision, coordination, and review become more important, while the importance of routine execution tasks may decline.
