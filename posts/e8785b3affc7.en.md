What now determines competition in frontier AI is not the intelligence of the models themselves. The three developments that surfaced this week may, at first glance, look like unrelated stories about the environment, politics, and personnel. Google and Amazon’s carbon emissions rose as AI expanded. The U.S. administration asked OpenAI for a limited release of its new model GPT-5.6. And Nobel Prize-winning researcher John Jumper is leaving Google DeepMind for Anthropic. But in fact, all three can be explained by the same principle. As intelligence becomes relatively abundant, what constrains the frontier is no longer intelligence itself, but the scarce complementary resources around it—physical infrastructure, political permission, and talent.

## Physical Infrastructure as the Bottleneck: Emissions Are the Shadow of Data Center Construction

What “The ‘Real Cost’ of AI” shows is just how dependent advances in intelligence are on the physical world. In sustainability reports released this week by Google and Amazon, Google’s total emissions rose 25% year over year, while Amazon’s rose 16%. What matters is the breakdown. Most of the increase came from Scope 3 emissions not directly controlled by the companies themselves—especially data center construction and the procurement of GPUs and memory.

That is deeply revealing. Rising emissions are not just about the electricity used to run models in inference. As the article points out, the real weight lies in “construction” itself: pouring concrete, installing servers, and stacking semiconductors. The key point is that the increase is centered in Scope 3—not in the companies’ own operating power use, but in emissions embedded in construction materials and chip procurement. In other words, the cost is already locked in before the model is ever “run,” at the stage where a place to put it is built. No matter how refined the algorithm becomes, it cannot conjure a new data center building or an extra GPU allocation out of nothing. Even if inference efficiency doubles, emissions will keep rising as long as demand drives construction even faster. The fact that both companies’ net-zero pledges have become harder to achieve is nothing less than the visible shadow of intelligence expansion being bottlenecked by physical supply chains. Intelligence can be scaled up. Steel, concrete, and electronics cannot—at least not at the same speed.

## Politics as the Gate: The Power to Decide Release Has Moved to the State

“The White House asks OpenAI for a limited release of the new model GPT-5.6” illuminates another scarce resource: permission to pass. According to reporting from The Information, OpenAI plans not to make its newest model, GPT-5.6, generally available, but to distribute it first only to a limited set of partners. The move reportedly comes at the request of the Trump administration, with the government set to “approve access on a customer-by-customer basis” during the preview period; the Office of the National Cyber Director and the Office of Science and Technology Policy are said to have requested the limited release.

What is at stake here is not whether the model is intelligent. It is the judgment of “who may be allowed access, and when.” A regime in which access is “approved on a customer-by-customer basis” introduces tiers and hierarchy into access to the same model. The capability is one and the same, but the state chooses which actors may touch it—creating a form of scarcity independent of intelligence itself. The article frames this as a turning point: an administration that initially championed a “hands-off” approach has, following the June 2 executive order, begun intervening in release decisions for frontier models. It places this alongside the earlier precedent of Anthropic’s Project Glasswing (Claude Mythos). If it were a one-off event at a single company, it could be dismissed as coincidence. But once a second point is added—an executive order and Glasswing—it becomes a line. What is increasingly being centralized under the state as a single gatekeeper is not capability itself, but permission to bring that capability into the world.

## Talent as Scarcity: The Dissolving Boundary Between Roles

“What John Jumper’s move to Anthropic says about the dissolving boundaries between research, product, and strategy in AI labs” concerns a third scarce resource: people. Reuters reported on June 19, 2026, that John Jumper, the researcher awarded the Nobel Prize for AlphaFold, is expected to leave Google DeepMind and join Anthropic. What the article reads in this is not merely a personnel change. It is the intensity of the competition for top researchers, and the structure in which the boundaries between research, product, and strategy—once distinct roles—are beginning to dissolve.

The fact that the movement of a single researcher can redraw the competitive map of frontier labs itself tells us how scarce and irreplaceable talent has become. Model weights can be copied; the judgment required to design them cannot. The boundaries are dissolving because in a world where intelligence has become abundant, what is scarce is not “hands that can implement,” but “minds that can decide what should be implemented.” The fusion of roles is simply another name for the process by which organizations are being reorganized around those scarce minds.

## A Shared Principle: When One Factor Becomes Abundant, Value Moves to Scarce Complements

The three developments—physical infrastructure (emissions), politics (release restrictions), and talent (a high-profile move)—appear unrelated on the surface. But they are different cross-sections of the same shift. Together, they suggest that the bottleneck stage in frontier AI has moved from “can you build a smarter model?” to “who controls the scarce complementary resources needed to make intelligence usable?”

The logic is straightforward. When one production factor becomes abundant, economic value and control shift to the scarce factors that complement it. This is a classic dynamic of complementary goods, and AI is no exception. Marginal intelligence in models is rapidly becoming commoditized, at least at the edges, through the spread of open weights and imitation by competing firms. By contrast, the complements required to make that intelligence usable—power, data centers, and GPUs (physical infrastructure); permission to release (politics); and the small number of minds capable of making design judgments (talent)—are not becoming abundant at the same pace. Competition, and leverage, therefore concentrate there.

This claim is falsifiable. If intelligence itself were still the bottleneck, the frontier would be contested mainly on benchmark performance, and firms would compete primarily on model quality alone. But what we actually observe is maneuvering over power purchase agreements, negotiations with governments over release conditions, and bidding wars over individual researchers. The main battlefield has moved outside intelligence itself—and that is precisely the signature of a shifted bottleneck.

## Forecast and Implications: Measure Position Not by Intelligence, but by Access to Complements

If this principle is correct, several consequences follow, each with a corresponding probability.

First, with high probability, compute resources and power will become explicit strategic axes. Carbon disclosure will shift from a matter of moral presentation to a competitive liability. The basis for this is the rise in emissions and the retreat from net-zero already shown in “The ‘Real Cost’ of AI,” and this is not temporary noise but structural pressure that will accumulate as long as construction continues.

Second, with medium-to-high probability, “customer-by-customer, phase-by-phase” release control for frontier models will become normal. The gap between “a released model” and “a model generally available to the public” will widen. The basis for this is the chain of two precedents seen in “the request for a limited release of GPT-5.6”: the executive order and Glasswing. One point may be coincidence; two points imply direction.

Third, with medium probability, talent concentration will accelerate, and the dissolution of role boundaries will continue. The organizational design of labs itself will become a moat that is hard to imitate. The basis for this is the reality made visible by “John Jumper’s move”: a single mind can alter the structure of competition.

The practical implication for readers is clear. If you are building anything on top of frontier AI, it is better to treat model intelligence as a commodity you will eventually rent and use. Do not bet your differentiation on differences in intelligence alone. Instead, measure your position by which scarce complementary resources you can access, and how much: power and compute, regulatory permission to pass through the gate, and the talent capable of making design judgments. Intelligence will become abundant. The real question is what around it will remain scarce.
