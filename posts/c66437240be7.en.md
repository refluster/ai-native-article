As long as you keep measuring the step that just got cheap, the thing you actually lack stays invisible. Clinical trial failure rates, data-center equipment backlogs, the share of output tokens produced by a coding agent — the three numbers reported this summer share no field, no time horizon, and no audience. Yet all three record the same structural event. These facts can be explained by a single principle: when the cost of one step collapses, the binding constraint moves to another step, but the metric built to watch the old step does not move with it.

## Only the side that can be mass-produced gets counted

"There Are No Magic Wands in Drug Discovery — Daphne Koller on AI Drug Discovery's Lock-and-Key Problem" opens with the anatomy of failure. More than 90% of drugs that enter clinical trials fail, and most of those failures trace not to the design of the molecule but to a wrongly chosen mechanism. In the metaphor used by Daphne Koller, insitro's founder and CEO, the industry has become excellent at mass-producing keys — and the keys are usually cut for the wrong lock.

What matters is that the two steps differ completely in how measurable they are. Design throughput, number of candidate compounds generated, structure-prediction accuracy — the key side can be counted. Which mechanism is the right target — the lock side — is not settled until a trial reads out. So AI investment concentrates on the measurable side, and the 90% failure rate does not move. When Koller argues that the premise "build a powerful enough AI and disease will be cured" is itself wrong, she is not asking for faster design. She is asking for "biological measurement data orders of magnitude beyond what is collected today" — that is, for the ability to measure the lock.

## The metric that was supposed to show a slowdown was not measuring demand

"AI Infrastructure Demand Is Not Slowing — a16z's Data on a Six-Month Backlog, 40% Higher H100 Rents, and Flat 5% Entry-Level Hiring" tests three received claims and finds the same confusion in three different costumes. The backlog for data-center machinery sits stable at just under six months. That reflects unresolved supply constraints, not softening demand. Twelve-month H100 contract rents are up roughly 40% against the prior November — pointing the opposite way from a slowdown. And the tech new-graduate hiring share, widely blamed on AI eliminating entry-level roles, has held at about 5% since 2019: a structure that predates AI and is not even a function of it.

In all three cases the numbers themselves are measured correctly. What is wrong is the assumption about what they are a function of. Backlog was read as a demand indicator when it is a supply-capacity indicator. Here too, what is being counted is the relatively abundant side — the willingness to place orders — rather than the binding one: the capacity to build and install the machines.

### Metrics do not lie; readers mistake the variable

What makes this case distinct among the three is that no new data was required at all. The public series from the U.S. Census Bureau, ADP, Silicon Data, and YipitData already existed. What was missing was not measurement but agreement about what was being measured.

## What became visible the moment we stopped counting people

"Agentic AI Moves from Consultation to Delegation — What Codex Usage Data Shows About the First Half of 2026" is the one case among the three in which the metric was actually replaced. The paper by OpenAI and coauthors, "The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex," compares Codex's share of output tokens across three populations. As of 11 June 2026: 99.8% inside OpenAI, 63.3% for organization accounts, 16.5% for individual accounts — an order-of-magnitude spread.

That unevenness is invisible in active-user counts, because the number of people using the tool is rising in all three populations. It became visible only because the metric changed from headcount to the composition of the work. The authors' conclusion that conventional adoption metrics are losing their information content for agentic AI follows directly from this comparison. And the moment the metric was updated, "adoption" turned out not to be a single rate but three distinct regimes: what would have looked like one smooth curve measured in people is a discontinuity between 99.8% and 16.5% measured in composition.

## The shared principle — measurement stays behind at the old bottleneck

These facts suggest the following. When the cost of a step collapses, the binding constraint moves elsewhere, but the metric does not, because the metric was designed to watch the step that used to bind. The result is that organizations measure their most abundant resource with the greatest precision, and hold no instrument for what they actually lack.

The three cases line up cleanly. In drug discovery the constraint moved from molecular design to disease mechanism, and the metrics stayed on the molecule. In AI infrastructure it moved from demand to supply, and backlog kept being read as a demand series. In Codex it moved from how many people use the tool to how the work is composed, and active-user counts stayed on headcount — except that here, uniquely, the metric caught up.

The principle carries a testable corollary: the length of the lag is set not by a field's maturity or its researchers' diligence, but by the cost of building new measurement infrastructure for the binding side. The three cases sort in exactly that cost order. Codex required only re-aggregating logs that already existed, so its metric turned over within months. AI infrastructure needs only reinterpretation of public statistics but also consensus, so it advances slowly, as an argument. Drug discovery requires new infrastructure — Koller's "orders of magnitude" more biological measurement — and has not been corrected at all.

The claim is falsifiable. Find a low-measurement-cost domain where the lag persists for years, or a domain requiring wholly new measurement infrastructure where the metric turned over quickly, and the principle is wrong.

## Predictions and implications

First, in domains where the binding side can be measured by re-aggregating logs that already exist, the headline metric will be replaced within 2026. Confidence is high: it has already happened with Codex, and the same operation is technically available in internal operations, customer support, and code production. The observable sign is dashboards whose primary tile changes from "users" to "share of work."

Second, in domains where reinterpretation of public statistics suffices, the metric will not be replaced; the disagreement will persist as an argument about how to read the same numbers. What a16z's test showed is not that data is missing but that agreement about the underlying variable is. Consensus is slower than measurement. So "did AI demand slow down?" will be settled by the definition of the variable, not by new data — and the sight of deceleration and acceleration camps both citing the same backlog figure is evidence that this prediction holds, not that the debate is immature.

Third, domains requiring new measurement infrastructure will lag longest, and the gap between AI investment and results will persist there longest. Koller's "orders of magnitude more data" is both a statement of a necessary condition and a prediction: clinical success rates will not move until that investment actually begins. This is sharply falsifiable. If acceleration on the molecular-design side alone produces a significant improvement in clinical success rates, the principle is wrong at least for drug discovery.

The practical consequence is simple. Name the single number your organization measures most precisely, then check whether it happens to measure the step whose cost fell furthest most recently. If they coincide, that metric is tracking surplus rather than results. What deserves measurement is the step that is now most expensive — and if the instrument for it does not yet exist, building that instrument is the real work, not making an already-fast step faster.
