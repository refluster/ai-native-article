## Who Will Win in the Age of AI Agents

## The Reorganization of “Authority × Execution” Happening Outside the UI

At first glance, **speculation about a SpaceX IPO**, **AI-driven changes in software architecture**, and **a training session in which 16 non-engineer executives got an “executive secretary” on their PCs in two and a half hours** may seem like unrelated topics. But in fact, they can all be explained by the same principle: in the AI era, competitive advantage is shifting away from the “visible interface” and toward the underlying infrastructure that controls data, authority, and execution.

## Analysis 1: What attracts excitement is not “explanation” but “the ability to execute”

### What SpaceX IPO speculation tells us

Commentary surrounding a potential SpaceX IPO is often framed with the metaphor that “gravity doesn’t work on Elon Musk.” This is not mere hero worship. What investors are betting on is **the ability to move the real world in ways that exceed ordinary corporate valuation frameworks**.

SpaceX has vertically integrated an exceptionally heavy execution domain: rocket launches, satellite operations, and communications network construction. If an IPO does materialize, what the market will be valuing is not just a “space story,” but an execution platform in which **launch capability, manufacturing capability, operational capability, and regulatory approval capability** are integrated into a single whole. In software terms, this resembles a situation where the winners are not companies with just an app or a UI, but **companies that control APIs, workflows, authentication, payments, and operational infrastructure**.

What matters here is that investors are paying a premium not only for future cash flow, but for “execution infrastructure that is hard for others to imitate.” These facts suggest that **in the AI era, the source of value is shifting from “products that can be explained” to “systems that can act on reality.”**

### What is being valued is not “dreams” but “operations backed by authority”

The same applies to AI agents. A chat UI can be copied, but the ability to **access customer data, move across internal SaaS systems, obtain approvals, and actually complete tasks** cannot be replicated so easily. In other words, the market is beginning to look past flashy surface layers and focus on **who has execution authority, and how far that authority extends**.

## Analysis 2: Software will lose its “head,” and defensibility will move from the UI to the execution layer

### The end of UI advantage

The argument that “software loses its ‘head’ in the agent era” is a sharp one. Traditionally, the center of software was the UI. Users opened a SaaS application, operated the screen, and did their thinking, decision-making, and execution inside that app. But once an AI agent sits in the middle, users no longer engage with individual UIs; instead, they simply **express what they want to do in natural language**.

As a result, the “head” of software is peeled away from the app-specific UI. The starting point for decision-making shifts to the agent side, and each SaaS product is reorganized into a functional module behind the scenes. What becomes important here is not the beauty of the screen, but **whether the functionality has been exposed through APIs, whether authority can be segmented at a fine-grained level, whether audit logs can be captured, and whether rollback is possible when something fails**.

This is not just a design discussion. In domains such as sales support, expense reimbursement, recruiting management, and customer support, agents will process tasks across multiple systems, so the experiential value of being confined to a single UI will rapidly fade. These facts suggest that **defensibility is shifting from “high-frequency screens” to “execution infrastructure that agents can use safely.”**

### The new barriers to entry are “authentication, connectivity, and execution guarantees”

Traditional SaaS moats were supported by training costs, UI habituation, and the inertia of departmental adoption. But the moat in the agent era is different.

- What operational data can it connect to?
- How far can execution authority be delegated?
- How can execution errors be controlled?
- Can the results be audited and explained?
These infrastructural requirements themselves become the competitive advantage. In other words, software companies are being forced to evolve from “companies that make convenient screens” into “companies that provide trusted infrastructure for operational execution.”

## Analysis 3: What the training of 16 non-engineers means—a revolution on the supply side

### What it means to get an “executive secretary” on your PC in two and a half hours

The case in which “16 non-engineer executives got an ‘executive secretary’ on their PCs in two and a half hours” points not to a change on the demand side, but **a change on the supply side**. Until now, introducing business software or automation usually required going through an information systems department, outsourced development, SaaS selection, and requirements definition. But with AI, non-engineers on the front lines can now build their own dedicated support environments in a short period of time.

The numbers that matter here are the specificity of **16 people, two and a half hours, executive secretary**. The important fact is not that a few exceptional geniuses achieved something special, but that a group of non-engineers was able to acquire a support mechanism useful for real work in less than half a day. This was not just an “AI trial session.” It means that support functions for knowledge work can now be distributed to the front lines without specialized development.

### The division of labor inside product organizations begins to collapse

This change will also ripple into development structures. The division-of-labor model in which planners write specifications, designers create screens, and engineers implement them will be reorganized with AI support. As the cycle from requirements to prototype to improvement becomes dramatically shorter, value shifts away from “who writes the code” and toward “who controls the business context, the data, and the authority design.”

These facts suggest that **while AI is democratizing software production, it is actually increasing the importance of authority design and connection infrastructure required for real-world operations**. Building things is becoming easier; entrusting things is becoming harder. That is where the new bottleneck lies.

## A Shared Principle

## In the AI Era, Value Lies Not in “Intelligence” Alone, but in “Execution Capability Granted Authority”

Looking across these three articles, the common thread is not that “AI or expectations create value,” but that **value surges when AI is embedded in a position where it can actually make something happen**. SpaceX is valued highly not only because of its grand vision, but because it can handle manufacturing, launch, and operations itself. The reason software defensibility in the agent era moves away from the UI is that the entry point for decision-making becomes standardized, and differentiation shifts to back-end connectivity, authority, and execution guarantees. What the training example with non-engineer executives shows is that as the cost of using intelligence drops sharply and everyone gains access to “thinking support,” what becomes scarce is **intelligence connected to executable business workflows**.

Put differently, competitive advantage in the AI era will shift away from “the company with the smartest model” and toward **the company that can safely connect models to real-world systems of authority, data, and operations, and carry them through to results**. That is the underlying principle.

Seen through this lens, the “Why” is clear. Intelligence itself is being commoditized. High-performance models, conversational UIs, and functions such as summarization, search, and generation are spreading rapidly, and the differences among them are narrowing. That is why value is concentrating in where and how that intelligence can be embedded: into which systems, with which authority, and under what division of responsibility.

And the “So What” is even more important. The moat companies should be building now is not a UI or a temporary model advantage, but an **execution infrastructure** that includes **authority design, data connectivity, business execution, auditability, and human approval flows**.

## Forecasts and Implications

## What will happen over the next three years

First, what is highly likely to happen is **a change in how SaaS is valued**. Over the next 1〜3 years, single-function, UI-centric SaaS products will be increasingly vulnerable to pricing pressure. By contrast, products with API openness, workflow connectivity, fine-grained authority controls, audit logs, and execution safety are likely to increase in value as the “underlayer” for agents. The probability is high. Why? Because the more agent adoption progresses, the less often users will open the UI each time.

Second, **enterprise IT investment will shift from “generation” to “delegation.”** In 2023〜2024, the focus was on “generation support” such as summarization, search, meeting minutes, and draft creation. In the next wave, the focus will move to “work delegation” such as filing, updating, sending, approval requests, and executing analysis. But because the cost of malfunction is higher here, staged authority assignment, human intervention points, and audit trails will become essential. In other words, the gap between companies that stop at PoC and companies that move into production implementation will be determined not by model performance, but by governance design.

Third, **the role of non-engineers will expand, but the risks of rogue AI will also increase**. In a world where 16 people can get a secretary in two and a half hours, front-line-led improvement will explode. At the same time, problems such as leaking confidential data, inappropriate automatic sending, and erroneous approvals will also increase. Therefore, management must avoid the false binary of either “banning front-line use” or “opening it up without limits,” and instead **build a safe common platform and provide freedom on top of it**.

In practical terms, there are four actions readers should take right now.

1. Break down your company’s operations into the three layers of “viewing,” “judgment,” and “execution” 1. Inventory the authority and approval points required for execution 1. Before UI requirements, check the readiness of APIs, logs, and authentication integration 1. Measure AI adoption KPIs not by “number of uses” but by “number of completed tasks, time reduced, and delegation rate”

In the end, the primary battleground of the AI agent era is not the place that returns clever text. It is **who holds the authority to act on reality, and on what infrastructure that authority can be executed safely**. Only the companies that secure that layer will capture the next era of dominance after the age of the UI.
