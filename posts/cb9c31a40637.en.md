A federal antitrust suit against McDonald's in Chicago, OpenAI's always-on agent "Dots", and record results from Samsung Electronics and TSMC on the same day. Three events, three industries, three unrelated-looking stories. They turn out to share one explanation. Once what AI can do stops being the contested question, the contest moves to attribution: whose judgment does the output count as?

## McDonald's: where a recommendation becomes a decision

The explainer "McDonald's sued: is its AI pricing tool 'price coordination between franchises'?" describes a complaint that never asks how accurate the algorithm is. The system uses machine learning on transaction data from roughly 14,000 US stores and produces item-level price recommendations. The plaintiff's theory is that once pricing guidance is distributed through a central system fed by non-public data, franchisees who believe they set their own prices are steered to a common level. The alleged coordination dates back to 2019.

The company's defence sits on the same axis. Franchisees set their own prices independently, they are under no obligation to accept a recommendation, and data analytics and pricing software are widely used across business. Both sides are arguing not about whether the system is smart, but about whether an output that stops at a recommendation can be called an independent pricing decision.

Note how the price-gap evidence is read. In Fresno, California, two company-owned stores about two miles apart sold the Big Mac at $5.69 and $6.89. As the explainer points out, Reuters could not confirm that pricing technology caused the gap, and the gap shows dispersion, not convergence. The number alone does not settle attribution. What settles it is the operating arrangement: who was obliged to follow the number.

## Dots: a unit made of a name, credentials and permissions

The explainer "OpenAI unveils 'Dots', selling always-on agents as named colleagues you raise" records an announcement that is not about performance. Its source, TechCrunch, sees most of the functionality as already possible on existing agent infrastructure such as Codex. What is new is the packaging. Users name their first "primary dot", and OpenAI envisions a "team of Dots" working together. "Specialist Dots" with specific responsibilities are planned, each assignable a distinct identity, credentials and tools through existing systems, and OpenAI is reportedly working with Microsoft to integrate Agent 365 security controls.

The features did not change; what is being sold did. It is not a capability but a unit: a named entity with permissions that keeps pursuing a goal. Because the entry point is Slack or Teams, requests sit in the same conversational space as requests to human colleagues. Who asked for what, who executed under which authority, and how that record is kept in place: this has the same shape as the question McDonald's court is asking. Between the recommendation and the action, whose responsibility runs through?

## Samsung and TSMC: the level is settled, durability is the question

The third case fits the principle least well, and we say so. According to "Samsung operating profit 107.4 trillion won, TSMC quarterly revenue NT$1.49 trillion: 8 October, when AI-chip demand set records together", Samsung's third-quarter 2026 operating profit was estimated at 107.4 trillion won, up 782.5% year on year, on revenue of 195 trillion won and an operating margin of about 55%. The same day TSMC reported September revenue up 54.6% year on year to NT$511.86 billion, and third-quarter revenue computed from the monthly figures reached a record NT$1.49 trillion.

The market's reaction is the notable part. Samsung shares closed 2.42% lower at 262,000 won and the KOSPI fell 2.62%, as investors took profits and questioned how long the memory boom would last. Record demand is no longer the priced-in question. What carries a price is a view on who sustains that level, and for how long. The explainer lines up dates, ASML on 14 October, TSMC on 15 October, Samsung's detailed results on 29 October and Nvidia in November, and those are checkpoints for durability, not level. The thing everything is attributed to here is neither a person nor a company but an assumption nobody has promised: that demand continues.

## The shared principle

Set side by side, the three events show one structure.

First, the question of level is closed in each. McDonald's system can analyse 14,000 stores. Dots can run continuously and pursue goals. Samsung's and TSMC's numbers are records. Second, the dispute is not over. It has moved: to "whose decision is the recommendation" at McDonald's, "who owns the name and the permissions" with Dots, and "who underwrites the persistence of this level" at Samsung.

These facts suggest that as the volume of AI output becomes abundant, what grows scarce is not capability but the party willing to accept attribution. Capability is copied, prices fall, levels get overtaken. What cannot be copied is the position that signs an output as its own judgment and answers for the result. Courts draw the line between recommendation and decision, products build subjects out of names and permissions, and markets put a price on durability. The tools differ; the work is the same: drawing a line of attribution between output and responsibility.

The claim can be wrong. If performance metrics themselves return to the centre of dispute, for instance if the pricing-tool litigation is decided on predictive accuracy or the legality of the output rather than attribution, the principle fails.

## Predictions and implications

Three predictions follow, with unequal confidence.

First, disputes over algorithmic pricing will proceed as arguments about operating design, not accuracy. Since McDonald's keeps stressing that no one is obliged to accept a recommendation, the hearing is likely to centre on how binding the recommendation actually is, that is, what happens to a store that does not follow it. The allegations are unproven, and filing a suit does not mean a violation occurred. The outcome awaits the court.

Second, the criteria for evaluating agent deployments will shift from comparing capability to designing identity and authority. With Dots shown carrying its own credentials and tools and riding on Agent 365 controls, an organisation adopting agents has to answer first which Dot may do what, and where the record lives. Confidence is moderate; the observable is whether competitors package a similar unit.

Third, the assessment of AI infrastructure will move from level to durability. If Samsung's detailed results on 29 October show whether HBM or the price rise in general-purpose memory drove profit, the durability view gains concrete grounds. If they do not, it stays conjecture. Nvidia's results in November are the next checkpoint.

The practical takeaway for a reader is plain. If you are on the user side of AI, before reading a tool's performance sheet, decide who holds the position that owns the output as their judgment, with what authority and what record. Performance gaps narrow; an attribution design cannot be bought later.
