Data-center construction wages carry a premium of up to 64%. The top 10% of companies by token output are producing 32.5 times the industry average. Five stocks account for nearly 30% of the S&P 500's five-year +84% return. Job postings for "Forward Deployed Engineer" are proliferating even as almost nobody enforces what the title actually means. These four facts look unrelated, but they share one underlying mechanism: **the more a surface label or an aggregate average thins out, the more the real value and execution power behind it concentrates into fewer hands.**

## The 32.5x Divide Hiding Behind the Aggregate Numbers

Moses Sternstein's "Charts of the Week" post at a16z, dated August 21, 2026 (covered in the L2 explanation "データセンター賃金45ドルとエージェントのトークン消費32倍が示す2026年AI経済の実相"), reads at first like a single story: the AI boom is spilling into the real economy. A wage premium of up to 64% for data-center jobs suggests the entire labor market is sharing in the AI windfall.

But the same piece contains a second number that cuts against that impression: the top 10% of information-industry companies by token output produce 32.5 times the average. The story of "the AI economy is growing" turns out, underneath the aggregate, to be a distribution in which a small set of firms monopolizes the execution — token throughput — while the other 90% drag the average down. A further detail — legacy automation tools losing traffic to challengers like Gumloop — confirms that "using an automation tool" no longer tells you where the execution power actually sits. The aggregate wage figure rises; the set of actors actually earning it keeps narrowing. Both "data-center wage premium" and "automation tooling" survive as category names, but the number of firms genuinely operating inside those categories keeps shrinking — the first appearance of the mechanism this piece traces.

## Five Stocks Wearing the Index's Face — the Limits of "the S&P 500 Rose"

The Kobeissi Letter's analysis (covered in "NVIDIAが牽引するS&P500の5年リターン、上位5銘柄で寄与度の3割に集中") sharpens the same structure. A single headline figure — the S&P 500's five-year total return of +84% — reads as if 500 companies broadly lifted the index together. In fact, NVIDIA alone accounts for roughly 12 percentage points of that gain, and adding Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom, and Alphabet brings the top five to about 25 points — close to 30% of the entire five-year return.

The name "S&P 500" is a label meant to represent 500 constituents equally, but the substance of its movement is being carried by 1% of them (5 of 500). The post's 177,800-plus views and its divided reactions are themselves evidence of how many investors had not registered this gap. Once again, the range a label claims to cover and the range where the real returns are actually generated do not match.

## The Case That Names the Dilution Itself — the Truth Behind "FDE" Listings

Product-development specialist Shin's analysis (covered in "エセFDE求人の見抜き方——OpenAIとPalantirの定義に学ぶ『仕事のループ』という判定軸") shows this same mechanism playing out directly at the level of job titles. "Forward Deployed Engineer" is neither a licensed profession nor a role with an industry-wide, rigorously enforced definition, so nothing stops a company from relabeling ordinary contract development or implementation support as "FDE." The label spreads faster every quarter while its content thins with each new posting that borrows it.

Shin's diagnostic axis — not whether the role is client-embedded, but whether the person actually owns the full loop of "find the problem → design → implement → deploy to production → drive adoption and measure outcomes → generalize → improve the product" — is itself a method for detecting the split between label and substance. Anyone can claim the title; only a small number of practitioners actually hold the loop. Unlike the other two cases, this piece supplies the diagnostic tool for the very mechanism it documents — both evidence for the principle and a prescription against it.

## The Common Principle — Why Labels Thin While Substance Concentrates

Together, these three facts suggest that during the AI transition, the gap between "the category displayed on the surface" and "where the substance actually resides" is widening structurally. The reason: AI has drastically lowered the cost of *imitating the surface* — an aggregate statistic, a job title, a category name — while the cost of *owning and executing the full loop*, from finding the problem to measuring the outcome, has barely fallen at all.

Calling yourself an "AI-adopting company," staying included in the S&P 500, or calling yourself an "FDE" are all low-cost surface acts of imitation. Producing 32.5 times the execution power in token consumption, single-handedly generating 12% of an index's gain, or actually owning the full loop from problem discovery to production adoption all require an accumulation of execution capacity that cannot be imitated. As the cost of imitating the surface falls, more actors adopt the label, and the substance each instance of that label represents thins out. But because the cost of producing real execution capacity has not fallen, the actual value keeps concentrating into a relatively smaller set of holders.

This is also a rebuttal to the optimistic story that "AI levels the playing field." AI lowers the apparent barrier to entry by cutting the cost of acquiring a label, but in the same motion it degrades that label's informational value, making it harder to see the gap between the substantive few and the substance-less many. To an observer who only looks at aggregates and titles, the world appears to be flattening. Look at the underlying distribution, and concentration has not loosened at all — if anything, it is tightening.

## Forecast and Implications — Read the Distribution, Not the Label

If this principle holds, three developments should become observable at high probability over the next one to two years. First, the inflation of labels and category names — "AI-native," "AI-agent specialist" — will accelerate, and the informational value carried by labels in job postings and press releases will thin further, with the pattern seen in FDE listings spreading to other job titles. Second, the concentration ratios in token consumption and market returns (currently 32.5x for the top 10%, roughly 30% for the top five stocks) are likely to widen further, since the aggregate average itself hides the true shape of the underlying distribution — this is the logical consequence of treating dilution and concentration as two faces of the same coin, and it is a concrete, quarter-by-quarter testable prediction. Third, as the Gumloop case shows, challengers who notice a label's decay and move to redefine it will keep taking market share in other domains as well.

The practical takeaway for readers is concrete. For investment decisions, check each constituent's contribution rather than the index's headline return. For hiring decisions, check whether the candidate actually owns the loop from problem discovery to outcome measurement, not the title on the posting. For tool selection, check actual processing volume and traffic share, not the "AI-ready" marketing claim. What unites all three is a single instruction: look at the distribution, the substance, not the label, the surface.

This principle is also falsifiable. If future data show concentration in token consumption or market returns actually easing and spreading to a broader set of actors, the hypothesis of "simultaneous dilution and concentration" advanced here would be wrong. Conversely, if the next quarter's a16z-style data update shows the top 10%'s token-output ratio widening beyond 32.5x while the absolute number of companies and postings claiming "AI-native" status grows even faster, that would be concrete evidence supporting this piece's central claim. Readers should stop counting labels and start counting the distribution hidden inside them.
