Enterprise AI rollouts, code-review design, and the withdrawal of an AI penetration-testing tool look like three unrelated stories. They are explained by one principle: how much you can hand to an agent is set not by how capable it is, but by where the last point of reversal sits for what it does.

From more than 100 enterprise customers and more than 80 production agents, Atlassian concludes that enterprise AI stalls because of what surrounds the model, not the model itself. The Claude Code team, working where Claude writes about 90% of the code, did not abolish review; it redesigned it. ARTEX, an AI penetration-testing agent said to have been used in breaches of South Korean banks, was pulled after more than two months of public availability, and the pull does nothing to copies already distributed. Below, the three are read along one axis: reversibility.

## Reversibility, not capability, caps autonomy

### The staging is a staging of reversibility

"Atlassian's FDE Playbook: Four Reasons Enterprise AI Stalls at Pilot, and the 12-Week Path to Production" (the Japanese title is used as published) gives three stages under its governance lesson: Read (search, retrieve, summarise), Draft (the AI proposes, a human approves), and Act (automate narrow, well-understood actions once the organisation is comfortable with the controls). It reads like a story about accumulating trust. Reordered, it is something else: Read changes nothing, Draft lets a human stop the change before it happens, and only Act changes the world. The three stages are ordered by how reversible the action is.

The same source reports a commerce agent with human review and a full Jira audit trail that reached 95% accuracy before human review and is expected to save about 11,800 hours a year. What does the work here is less the accuracy figure than the split between drafting and approving: because a person still reviews, the 5% of errors can be undone before they become orders. When a bank rolled out Rovo chat and search to about 35,000 people, it also started with read-oriented AI and built auditability and security evidence before moving to custom agents. Autonomy widens from the zone where things can be undone. These figures, however, are Atlassian's own reports: customer names are withheld and the source gives no measurement method. What can be taken from them is the order of the design, not the accuracy of the numbers.

### Human time is priced as the cost of undoing

"Moving review to machines to protect human time: the Claude Code team's code-review design" makes the axis explicit. In Anthropic's monorepo, changes landing per day rose from about 2,500 in May to more than 30,000 in September, and line-by-line manual review became, in the authors' words, extremely hard or impossible. The team treats "did a human read it" as an inaccurate proxy for "what could this change break, and which check fits", and sets the risk tier from the change itself, not the author.

The tiers work as follows. Low-risk changes (documentation, wording, internal-only features, additive changes behind a flag) need no human approval if automated review and the security check pass. Medium-risk changes need one person. The highest-risk code, and paths that always require two, need two. What the low tier has in common is that it is easy to put back: a change behind a flag can be switched off without a deploy, the merge queue stops two individually passing PRs from breaking main together, and new features usually ship behind a flag to Anthropic employees first, with the latest channel ahead of stable.

The design also measures failure of reversal. Human time includes both the time spent reviewing and the time spent fixing what bad code broke, so revert rate and incident rate are treated as guardrails: if they rise, human time was moved, not saved. Merged PRs more than doubled from June to September while the share reverted fell by more than 40%, the team says. Their claim is that autonomy widened while failed reversals fell.

### Only what cannot be undone must be stopped at release

"ARTEX developer closes public repository: what 'code that cannot be recalled' leaves behind after the breach of nine Korean lenders" shows the other side of the axis. ARTEX was published on 26 July under AGPL-3.0 and withdrawn on 8 October. Downloadable for more than two months, forks, mirrors and local copies may already exist. In the source's phrasing, the withdrawal is like closing the garage door after the car has left, and open-source code has no recall function.

What matters is the source's account of ARTEX as unremarkable: it chains LLM calls to scan networks, find vulnerabilities and sequence attacks, automating work that security teams do by hand. CrowdStrike said an individual combined ARTEX with Claude Code and several other LLMs to automate reconnaissance and intrusion against South Korean financial institutions. According to the source, the largest known leaks are about 40,000 customer records at Yegaram Savings Bank and about 25,000 at Shinhan Bank, roughly 65,000 in total. The number of affected institutions is not settled, though: the article body says at least nine, while a related-article summary lists seven.

## The shared principle: put control before the point of no return

The three facts are explained by one proposition. **An agent's autonomy can safely be extended only up to the last point at which its action can still be undone; for actions that cannot be undone, control has to sit before the action or in the recipient's defences.**

These facts suggest the following inference. First, Atlassian's three stages limit the scope of Act by placing the point of reversal before the action, in the human approval at Draft. Second, the Claude Code tiers reduce human approval exactly where the means of reversal (flags, merge queue, rollback) are in place; reversibility determines how human time is allocated. Third, ARTEX is a case with no means of reversal at all: once published, the act is complete and the developer has nothing left to pull back. The review by South Korea's Financial Services Commission, which the source considers more important than ARTEX's state on GitHub, reads as an attempt to move the centre of control to the receiving side's defences.

The reason to use reversibility, not capability, as the axis is that in all three cases capability is already a given. Atlassian says enterprise AI stalls not because AI cannot do the work but because the surroundings are not ready. The Claude Code team designs on the premise that agents write most of the code. In ARTEX, the attacker only combined an existing tool with existing models. Once capability is a given, what makes the difference is whether the consequences of using it can be taken back.

The proposition can be falsified. If several organisations were to widen autonomy substantially with weak means of reversal and run for a long period without more reverts or incidents, the principle fails. If instead organisations with stronger reversal show the widest autonomy, it holds. Note also that the Claude Code team itself has no circuit breaker yet that tightens approval when the revert and incident dashboards move, and wants one before it increases zero-human merges. Automating reversal is the unfinished part of this design.

A caution is due. Of the three sources, the two with outcome figures (Atlassian and the Claude Code team) are self-reports by the parties involved. The ARTEX piece rests on a single source article, with Reuters and others cited only indirectly. This principle is a hypothesis drawn from that evidence, not a demonstration. Read the predictions below as claims built on thin evidence.

## What follows

### Prediction 1: expanding autonomy is subordinate to building the means of reversal (confidence: medium to high)

The pace at which firms add production agents is likely to be limited less by model improvement than by how fast they build audit trails, permissions, flags and rollback. In Atlassian's cases, a travel company built more than 120 agents without adoption growing, while an agent embedded in repetitive ticket triage became the most widely used. Atlassian itself lists as an open question that, as implementation speeds up, the constraint moves to planning, review, cross-team dependencies, governance and operations. The grounds are that self-observation, and the Claude Code team's report of more than doubling merged PRs from June to September with revert and incident rates held as guardrails.

### Prediction 2: "release" will be treated as a new approval gate (confidence: medium)

After ARTEX, dual-use AI agents that serve both attack and defence are likely to be asked, before release, what they make irreversible. The source says Metasploit faced the same debate 20 years ago and survived by becoming indispensable to defenders faster than to criminals. ARTEX had no such time: within three months of release it was linked to breaches and went closed-source before defenders caught up. The source, however, gives neither the change in misuse after the withdrawal nor the real number of forks. There is no basis in the sources to say the withdrawal was pointless; what can be said is that it does not touch copies already distributed.

### A practical takeaway

Whoever deploys agents can put one question at the start of the plan: where is the last point at which this action can be undone? If it can be undone, human approval can be reduced, as the Claude Code team does. If it cannot, separate drafting from approval and put the human approval before the action, as Atlassian does. In domains with no means of reversal at all (publishing, outbound sends, billing), postpone widening autonomy and design the means of reversal first. And put a measure of failed reversals, such as revert and incident rates, on the same screen as the time saved. Whether human time was saved or merely moved can be told apart only by that measure.
