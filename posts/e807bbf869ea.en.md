The source of value is shifting from a model’s “ability to produce” to its “ability to determine whether its output can be trusted.” In Tokyo, Sakana AI has introduced its agent-oriented model “Fugu” into the gap opened by export controls; Codez lays out 14 steps for moving “from prompter to loop designer”; and Movez runs 300 agents in parallel on the low-cost Kimi K2.6 while assigning only verification to Opus 4.8. At first glance, these look like three different stories—geopolitics, methodology, and implementation. But in fact they can all be explained by a single principle: generation is becoming commoditized, and verification is what remains scarce. That is where value is moving.

## Generation Can Scale to 300 Agents, but Verification Narrows to One

The starkest illustration of this pattern comes from Movez (@0xMovez), as introduced in L2’s “A self-improving loop that runs 300 agents on Kimi K2.6 and assigns only verification to Opus 4.8.” In that setup, the generation side is expanded in parallel to as many as 300 instances using the open-source model Kimi K2.6. If the count can be increased that far, it means generation has become cheap, replaceable, and abundantly scalable. And yet, when it comes to deciding whether that flood of output is actually acceptable, the role is deliberately narrowed and handed off exclusively to Anthropic’s Opus 4.8, a model in a class of its own.

What Movez explicitly identifies as the source of competitiveness is not the intelligence of any single model, but three things: low-cost parallel execution, a trustworthy verification gate, and the accumulating asset of skill files and constraint files built up with each run. The crucial point is that two of those three lie “outside generation.” Value comes not from one smart model, but from the structure itself: cheap mass generation filtered through a trustworthy gate. Even the remaining factor—“asset accumulation”—is only possible because what gets written into the constraint files is limited to results that have passed verification. In other words, even that is ultimately a byproduct of the verification gate. Put simply, in this procedure, effectively two and a half of the three value-creating elements sit on the verification side. The fact that this article drew more than 2 million views is likely evidence that many practitioners were already feeling this same shift firsthand.

## Whether a Loop “Pays Off” Is Also Determined by Verification

The same principle is reinforced from the standpoint of cost-effectiveness by L2’s “Loop engineering: 14 steps for moving from prompter to loop designer.” The core of the article, published by Codez (@0xCodez) on X on June 9, 2026, is not hype but restraint. Codez repeatedly argues that loops are worth the cost only when all four conditions are met: “the task repeats, verification is automated, the token budget can absorb waste, and the agent has the tools of a senior engineer.” If even one of those is missing, the loop will take more than it gives back.

Of those four conditions, the true dividing line is “automation of verification.” Once the fulcrum shifts from generation itself—writing prompts—to designing systems that automate generation, the question of whether that automation can run without breaking down depends on whether output can be verified mechanically. The moment verification falls back to manual work, the value of running 300 agents disappears. Movez’s decision to pin the “human filtering gate” to a top-tier model, and Codez’s insistence that “automated verification” is a precondition for profitability, are saying the same thing in different words. The freedom to scale up generation is always constrained by how reliably and automatically verification can be provided.

## Regulation Is Also Aimed at Access to Trusted Top-Tier Roles

At first glance, L2’s “The gap opened by Anthropic’s export controls: Sakana AI’s ‘Fugu’ and 360’s cyber AI reveal the geopolitics of model access” seems unrelated. But it is another expression of the same principle. What the Trump administration barred for non-U.S. persons was Anthropic’s cybersecurity-focused model “Mythos,” along with the more tightly restricted “Fable 5.” The blade of regulation was aimed not at general-purpose mass generation, but at top-tier models entrusted with high-risk judgment under conditions of high trust.

As that article makes clear, the issue is no longer “bigger models,” but “continuity of access to top models.” That is precisely why efforts to fill the resulting gaps are emerging around the world. In Tokyo, Sakana AI announced “Fugu” for agents. In China, 360 unveiled the vulnerability-discovery AI “飛龍鋒” and the defense-automation AI “倚天針.” In all of these cases, the reason for the replacement investment is the same: every camp understands that what would be most damaging to lose is access to the top-tier roles that can be trusted with the highest-level tasks. The resource states seek to control and the role engineers pin to elite models are, in fact, the same thing.

## The Shared Principle: What Is Scarce Is Not Generative Capacity but Trustworthy Verification

Put these three facts side by side, and a single line connects them. Movez scales generation up to 300 agents while reserving verification for a model in a class of its own. Codez makes automated verification the precondition for loop economics. Export controls attempt to sever access to the trusted top tier. Together, these facts suggest that the center of gravity for value and scarcity in the AI stack has shifted from the ability to “produce” output to the ability to determine whether that output can be “trusted.”

The logic is straightforward. Generative capacity is rapidly moving toward oversupply as models become cheaper and easier to run in parallel. Once a resource becomes abundant, it stops being a differentiator in itself. By contrast, the function that guarantees the quality of the vast volume of output—the verification gate—does not become less valuable as quantity increases. If anything, its value rises as generation scales. Scarcity emerges from the “asymmetry in ease of scaling” between generation and verification. Generation can be multiplied 300-fold, but verification that guarantees trust—whether through a top-tier model or an automated gate—is not nearly so easy to scale. That is why the strategic priority shifts away from securing cheap generation and toward controlling trustworthy verification. The fact that regulation targets top-tier models that perform verification-like roles is itself proof that the regulating side also understands that what is scarce is not generation, but verification.

## Forecast and Implications

If this principle is correct, then over the next year the axis of competition will shift decisively from “how much can you generate” to “how much trustworthy verification can you control.” First, the advantage of an organization or product is likely to lie not in possessing the single smartest model, but in designing the verification gate that filters cheap mass generation. Of Movez’s three elements, what is hardest to imitate is not cheap generation, but the trustworthy gate and the accumulated constraint assets that build up with each execution. Second, the geopolitical fault line will also run along the verification layer. If export controls are trying not to stop mass generation but to cut off trusted top-tier roles, then countries and companies will diverge based on how they secure “their own verification capability”—and the moves by Sakana AI and 360 can be read as an early phase of that process.

The practical implication is clear. If you are building agent loops, determine first how far verification can be automated and fixed in place before competing on the number of generating agents. As Codez’s four conditions make clear, a loop whose verification cannot run automatically becomes more costly the more you scale it. Treat generation as an abundant assumption, and place your budget and design weight on verification, which is the truly scarce resource.

This claim is falsifiable. If, going forward, verification itself can be sufficiently automated by low-cost models and no longer requires either a top-tier model or a human gate—in other words, if verification becomes commoditized at the same speed as generation—then the center of scarcity will move somewhere else, and this article’s conclusion that “verification is scarce” will no longer hold. Even in trying to see where the center of gravity may move next, the first thing to watch is this: who controls verification, how cheaply they control it, and how reliably they can provide it.
