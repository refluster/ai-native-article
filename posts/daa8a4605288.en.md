## Introduction

Three observations look unrelated: people feel just as exhausted after a day of AI-assisted work even though task time drops; five stocks account for nearly a third of the S&P 500's five-year return; and most "FDE" job postings fail to meet any real industry definition. They share one mechanism. Automation doesn't shrink total work evenly — it re-concentrates judgment, capital, and legitimacy onto whoever actually closes the loop, from problem discovery to measured, generalized results.

## Concentrated oversight: what "AI fatigue" reveals about judgment

Product-development specialist Shin (@shin_sasaki19, posting on X, August 27, 2026) analyzed a paradox: AI shortens task time, yet a strong end-of-day fatigue remains. His conclusion is that AI automates *execution* — the doing — but not the five burdens that stay with the human: oversight, decision-making, verification, handling the incomplete, and updating. What matters is that these five burdens don't spread evenly across a team. As long as one person remains ultimately accountable for the outcome, no amount of AI-driven task automation reduces that person's oversight and judgment load — if anything, it stands out more sharply as everything around it gets mechanized. A team's total workload may fall, yet the frequency of "handling the incomplete" and "updating" that lands on the accountable person doesn't fall, and can even rise. AI isn't compressing the total amount of work; it is splitting work into a "mechanizable outside" and a "responsibility-bearing inside," and leaving only the inside in human hands.

## Concentrated capital: the five-year return NVIDIA is pulling

The same structure shows up in market numbers. According to an analysis The Kobeissi Letter posted on X, of the S&P 500's roughly +84% total return over the past five years, NVIDIA alone contributed over 10 points — about 12% of the index's entire gain. Add Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom, and Alphabet, and the top five stocks' combined contribution reaches roughly 25 points — nearly a third of the index's five-year return. This isn't market inefficiency. Thousands of companies participate in the AI boom, splitting up the associated work — contract development, tooling, data preparation. But the payoff doesn't spread evenly across all of them; it flows to whoever actually, self-sufficiently closes the underlying technical loop — chip design, manufacturing, and supply. A number that looks like a "collective" index return is, in substance, the earnings of a handful of loop owners.

## Concentrated legitimacy: the loop test for spotting a fake "FDE"

The third facet concerns the legitimacy of a job title itself. Postings for "FDE" (Forward Deployed Engineer) are surging, but as Shin's analysis on X points out, FDE is neither a licensed role nor one with a strict, industry-wide definition. Even so, there is a real test: not whether the role is client-site, but whether the person owns the entire "work loop" — problem discovery → design → implementation → production rollout → adoption and outcome measurement → generalization → product improvement — start to finish. A posting that covers only one segment of that loop (implementation alone, or rollout support alone) and merely borrows the FDE label is not describing a real FDE, Shin argues. Here too, the test is neither "how much work" nor "what title," but "does this role close the loop." Anyone can own a fragment of the work; only someone accountable for the whole loop earns the legitimacy to claim the title. Phrases like "on-site with the client" or "technical support" don't actually specify which segment of the loop is being staffed — which is why they read as informationally incomplete.

## The unifying principle: re-concentration onto whoever closes the loop

One principle runs through all three facts. Automation is not a technology for reducing total work. It is a technology for splitting work into an "outside the loop" (mechanizable, divisible) and an "inside the loop" (owns problem discovery through measured, generalized outcomes, and bears responsibility for the result). In the AI-fatigue case, AI takes over execution while oversight, verification, and decision-making — the loop-closing function — stay with humans, surfacing as fatigue. In the NVIDIA case, within the vast AI-infrastructure supply chain, only a handful of companies actually, self-sufficiently close the technical loop of design, manufacturing, and supply, and market returns flow disproportionately to that spot. In the fake-FDE case, amid a proliferation of the "FDE" label, whether someone actually runs the full loop from problem discovery through generalization is the one test that separates a legitimate claim to the title from an illegitimate one.

These facts suggest that what AI automates is "work," not "the function that closes the loop and bears responsibility for the outcome." So as AI adoption spreads, value, returns, and legitimacy re-concentrate onto the small set of actors — people, roles, or companies — standing inside the loop, while everything outside the loop is leveled into a commodity anyone can supply. A reduction in work and a reduction in responsibility are not the same phenomenon at all.

## Forecast and implications: the question is loop ownership, not workload

If this principle holds, three observable consequences should follow. First, at the individual level, more people will likely report "AI fatigue," and the explanation will increasingly shift from workload to concentrated oversight and decision burden — the kind of analysis in this window is an early signal of that shift. The practical implication: the AI usage worth pursuing isn't "less work" but a design that makes visible who closes which part of the loop, so oversight cost doesn't concentrate on one person by default.

Second, in capital markets, whether the top five stocks' contribution keeps rising is the falsification point for this principle. If their combined contribution pushes past 30% toward 40% within a year, the hypothesis that wealth is re-concentrating onto the companies actually closing the AI-infrastructure loop is reinforced; if contribution broadens instead, this principle is at least refuted on the capital-markets facet.

Third, in labor markets, the "FDE"-style title inflation is likely to spread to other emerging roles — AI engineer, prompt engineer, and similar titles. As it does, a selection axis built on "which segment of the loop, start to finish, does this role own" — rather than "which tasks does this role perform" — should become a standard interview criterion for both employers and candidates within the next one to two years.

The practical takeaway converges to one question for individuals and companies alike: in the AI era, the metric that matters isn't how much work you've cut, but which part of the loop — from problem discovery through measured, generalized outcomes — you actually close. Without closing the loop, compressing work with AI buys neither the weight of judgment, nor the return on capital, nor the legitimacy of a title. Conversely, someone who owns only a fragment of the loop and uses AI to work faster is more likely to end up with a lingering anxiety about the responsibility they still don't hold than with any real sense of relief — which is exactly the imbalance these three observations, read together, expose.
