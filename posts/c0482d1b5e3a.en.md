## Executive Summary

Sustainability reports released this week by Google and Amazon make clear that both companies’ carbon emissions are rising as AI expands. Google’s total emissions rose 25% year over year, and Amazon’s rose 16%, with most of the increase coming from “Scope 3” emissions outside their direct control—especially data center construction and the procurement of GPUs and memory—making their net-zero pledges even harder to achieve.

## The Numbers: How Much Emissions Increased

The increases shown in the reports are not small. Both companies have pledged to eliminate carbon emissions in the near future, but AI is clearly pushing that trajectory backward.

- **Google’s total emissions rose 25% year over year**, while **Amazon’s rose 16%**.
- Both companies acknowledge that energy consumption has increased sharply alongside the growing use of AI.
- Both companies emphasize “carbon intensity”—the amount of pollution per dollar of revenue. This is the same metric China has used in climate negotiations even as its total emissions have surged.
- Both reports spend several pages arguing that AI can contribute positively to the environment, which the article characterizes as “protesting too much.”
At the same time, emissions from electricity purchases have been relatively contained. That is largely because years of renewable energy procurement are paying off. However, tech companies including Google have begun making major investments in natural gas power plants to keep up with AI’s electricity demand, and that picture could soon change.

## What’s Driving the Emissions: Scope 3, Data Centers, and Semiconductors

The main driver of the increase is not electricity but **Scope 3 emissions**—indirect emissions outside a company’s direct control, including pollution associated with the goods and services it buys and the products it sells.

- **Google** discloses Scope 3 under the combined categories of “capital goods” and “use of sold products.” The company itself acknowledges that the latter is too small to be significant, effectively suggesting that the real driver is data centers. Last year, its Scope 3 emissions **increased by 2.1 million tons** and **doubled compared with 2019**, its base year.
- **Amazon** reports Scope 3 primarily in capital goods and fuel- and energy-related categories, including data centers and warehouses. The report states that “to meet strong customer demand, we added more data center capacity globally in 2025 than any other company, including more than **1.2 gigawatts (GW)** in the fourth quarter alone.”
## Why Decarbonization Is Hitting a “Wall”

The biggest source of emissions used to be electricity for offices and mid-sized data centers, which could be offset through renewable energy purchases. AI has overturned that assumption.

- Data centers can still, in principle, be powered by renewables plus batteries, but in practice a return to fossil fuels has already begun, making net-zero pledges harder to meet.
- More deeply rooted are the emissions from building and outfitting the data centers themselves. Steel and cement are both heavy-emitting industries, and startups working on low-carbon and decarbonized production technologies have not yet reached the scale required by tech companies.
- GPUs and memory chips cannot be ignored either. Semiconductor manufacturing is highly energy-intensive, and many of the most advanced fabs are located in Asia, where power grids still rely heavily on fossil fuels. In addition, many of the chemicals used in chipmaking are potent greenhouse gases with warming effects thousands of times greater than the same amount of CO2.
## Conclusion: Not Impossible to Solve, But Not Easy

In the article’s view, none of these problems is impossible to solve. But to fulfill their net-zero pledges, both companies will have to make several major investments at the same time.

- A further expansion of renewable energy purchases.
- Large-scale investment in advanced steel and cement production.
- The purchase of carbon removal credits on the scale of millions of tons.
It may still be achievable, but the broad adoption of AI has not made that path any easier—that is the key takeaway from these two reports.
