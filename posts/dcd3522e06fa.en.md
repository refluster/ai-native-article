## Executive Summary

Writing for a16z, Seema Amble uses Salesforce's August partnership with Anthropic — "Claudeforce," which lets users operate the Salesforce CRM from inside Claude — as the jumping-off point for a three-way map of power between incumbent systems of record, general-purpose agents like Claude, and vertical AI-native startups. Her thesis: a customer's actual job is always bigger than the record an incumbent owns, and that gap is exactly where vertical AI companies can still compete.

## Can Incumbents Escape the Record?

Docusign's Iris reviews contracts, Atlassian's Rovo aims to resolve and route requests, and Klaviyo's Composer builds campaigns and assesses marketing performance. Amble argues these products mark a shift for incumbents beyond a "slap on a chatbot" strategy and into actually taking action.

Claudeforce, though, shows a different dynamic: "Claude can become the front door to the job while Salesforce still controls the underlying CRM data and actions." Amble describes this as the interface and the system of record "unbundling," creating an opening for AI-native startups to "rebundle around the job."

## Four Rungs of Agent Autonomy

The piece organizes application-level agents into four types by how much autonomy and judgment each requires:

- **Retrieval assistants** find information, summarize documents, answer questions, and draft responses.
- **Process agents** carry out rule-based work like updating records or routing approvals, using limited judgment to interpret the task and act.
- **Policy agents** apply the rules in an organization's playbooks, precedents, and thresholds to ambiguous cases.
- **Principal agents** make judgment calls about what the organization should do, often with open-ended tradeoffs around strategy, risk, and resources.
A year ago, Amble writes, most incumbents' AI products were "still at the retrieval stage, if that." Now more can take action based on the workflows, permissions, and rules already embedded in their products, and a few — like Docusign's Iris applying a legal team's playbook — are beginning to apply narrow, human-defined policy. But she is explicit about the ceiling: "that judgment is still largely bounded by the record the incumbent owns." That line is the article's hinge, echoed in its own headline: **"The job is bigger than the record."**

## Where the Record Ends and the Job Continues

"The contract is not the legal matter, the ticket is not the customer's resolution, and the opportunity is not the sale." With these three pairs, Amble shows how a customer's actual work crosses applications, teams, and sometimes companies, while an incumbent is limited to building what she calls a "local system of work." The larger opportunity, she argues, is managing the job across those boundaries — all the parties, documents, decisions, revisions, and the final result.

A general-purpose agent like Claude or Codex can reach across systems via MCP, but Amble names concrete limits: latency and inconsistency from each application describing the same customer, contract, or transaction differently, and the fact that even full internal access doesn't automatically extend to information held by external parties, such as between buyers and suppliers. Even so, she grants that Claude can sit above systems of record — Claudeforce again being the example — and keep gaining context "through deeper integrations blessed by the incumbents."

## Remembering Is Not Learning

Owning more of the job matters, Amble writes, because it lets a startup see "the decisions and corrections that produced the final result." A model only performs well on a specific job through a vertical harness with the right context, tools, workflows, and evals — and the learning loop is what improves that system, model plus harness, after each job.

Completed records alone, she notes, don't suffice as a training curriculum: "A signed contract shows what the parties agreed to, but not every alternative considered or every reason for an exception. A closed ticket shows the resolution, but not every hypothesis the support team tested."

She draws a sharper distinction still: as labs build general-purpose memory, preserving context alone stops being a durable advantage. Memory can help an agent recall a customer's preferences or a decision from last week, "but it doesn't tell the agent whether the work was good or why an expert changed it or what to change next time." Focus, in her framing, is what lets a company see more examples of the same job, learn faster, and measure improvement.

Amble splits this learning into two kinds: learning the **profession** — how a good professional does the work, teachable from expert-made assignments, realistic examples, and standards for a good result — and learning the **institution** — how a particular firm or customer does the work, including its templates, precedents, risk thresholds, and escalation rules, which begins with the customer's own materials and improves through corrections and exceptions.

## How Harvey Manufactured a Curriculum

Amble points to legal AI company Harvey's "Tenet" project as a concrete case. Harvey post-trained an open-weight model using synthetic data, public legal data, and human-expert-created data — without starting from customer data — and built roughly **1,750 legal-task environments**, each simulating a partner-assigned matter complete with documents, tools, and an expert rubric for a high-quality result. The average assignment carried about **50 specific criteria**. Rather than waiting years to accumulate enough customer history, Amble writes, "Harvey manufactured a curriculum it could use to train and evaluate its model."

## Four Tests for a Good Vertical AI Market

Drawing on the Harvey example, Amble lays out four questions for evaluating a potential vertical AI market:

1. Can an expert quickly tell what the AI got right or wrong, and explain how to improve it? 2. Is the work hard enough that judgment matters, versus simple rules? 3. Does the work happen often enough for the product to learn? 4. Can the startup begin with one assignment and grow into doing an entire job?

Many workflows in legal, tax, and accounting satisfy all four, she writes, because the work repeats, requires judgment, and already gets reviewed by experts. The same pattern, she adds, shows up in less obvious markets like manufacturing: when a defect appears, a quality engineer must gather test results, supplier documents, equipment logs, and plant rules from across several systems and decide what's acceptable — and "no single product owns the full investigation," leaving room for a startup to start with drafting the investigation report and eventually own the end-to-end resolution.

Amble's closing view is that some jobs will be fully served by a system of record plus a general-purpose agent, but meaningful opportunity remains for vertical AI-native startups wherever work happens often, expert judgment matters, and the learning loop is strong — because "the incumbent may own the record and the lab may control the front door, but the vertical AI company can still win by becoming the best at doing the job itself."
