## Corporate AI Strategy × Rebuilding the Development Stack

## The Winners Will Be the Companies That Capture the “Point of Inflow,” Not Just “Gross Margin” — The Common Principle Running Through Services-Led Growth, GitLab Act 2, and AI-Native Development Organizations

## Introduction

At first glance, an AI company’s service strategy centered on the Forward Deployed Engineer, GitLab’s redesign under “Act 2,” and topics as varied as Meta’s Muse, pharmaceutical AI partnerships, and fragmented state-by-state regulation may look like separate stories. But in fact, they can all be explained by the same principle. **In the AI era, competitive advantage is determined not by the model itself, but by who controls the point where data flows in, the business context, and the implementation surface.**

## Analysis 1: AI Companies Are Prioritizing “Ways Into the Front Line” Before “High-Gross-Margin Software”

The most important point made in the first article is straightforward. AI startups are not simply aiming, as traditional SaaS companies did, to “reduce the service ratio and raise product gross margins.” Instead, they are prioritizing the use of **services as a strategic wedge to embed themselves deeply into customer workflows and capture the inflow points for data and problem discovery**.

At the center of this approach is the **Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)**. Famously associated with Palantir, this role is not just an implementation support function. It involves entering the customer’s operating environment and connecting AI in a way that is usable in the real world, even when requirements are ambiguous, workflows are immature, and data is scattered. In essence, the article argues that startups are reevaluating services not as “temporary inefficiency,” but as an **exploration mechanism for discovering repeatable product opportunities**.

What does this suggest? It suggests that **AI’s value does not emerge from a general-purpose model alone, but only when it is connected to real data, real workflows, and real operations**. That is why even low-margin services can have more long-term strategic value than the product itself, so long as they allow a company to penetrate deeply into the customer’s actual business.

Even more importantly, in this structure, what matters is not “where you make money first,” but “where you gain access first.” In AI adoption, customers are not buying a finished product so much as seeking a co-design process around still-undefined problems. That makes the high-touch model mediated by FDEs more than simple contract work: it functions as a **mechanism for transforming tacit customer knowledge into productizable knowledge**.

## Analysis 2: GitLab’s “Act 2” Sees AI Not as Code Generation, but as a Redefinition of the “Software Generation Platform”

GitLab’s “Act 2” reveals the same structure from another angle. According to the article, GitLab is positioning AI not as a mere coding-assistance feature, but as the **platform for the agent era that will reorganize DevSecOps as a whole**. This is not just a feature expansion. It is a shift in product philosophy.

What GitLab sees is the reality that AI is moving the center of value away from “writing code” and toward “connecting requirements, generation, review, security, and operations in one continuous flow.” In other words, competitive advantage is moving away from standalone generation performance and toward **who controls the key points across the entire software lifecycle**. Code generation alone can be replaced. But an integrated environment spanning Issues, MRs, CI/CD, security scanning, and audit trails is much harder to substitute.

What deserves particular attention here is the framing of a shift from “human-centered DevSecOps” to a “software generation platform for the agentic era.” This means moving away from the assumption that human developers directly perform all tasks, and toward an operating model in which AI agents handle much of the work while humans focus on decision-making, governance, and exception handling. In that world, what matters most is not the smartest model, but the **system boundaries, permission design, auditability, and workflow integration that allow AI agents to work safely**.

These facts suggest that **in the AI era, platform value is shifting away from “generation capability” and toward “orchestration capability.”** What GitLab is redesigning is not just its product UI. It is the governance layer required for enterprises to use AI organizationally. That is why this strategy is not merely participation in a race over AI features; it is a positioning move around the reorganization of how enterprises produce software.

## Analysis 3: AI-Native Organizations, Pharmaceutical Partnerships, and Regulatory Fragmentation — Value Is Moving from the “Model” to the “Ability to Embed”

The topics covered in DeepLearning.AI’s The Batch reinforce the same principle. Research and development efforts like Meta’s Muse, the rise of AI-native development organizations, AI partnerships with pharmaceutical companies, and fragmented state-level regulation may appear unrelated. But viewed across the board, what they share is that the central issue is no longer AI as an isolated technical component, but **how AI gets embedded into the operating systems of existing industries**.

Take pharmaceutical AI partnerships. They are not competing on model accuracy alone. In drug discovery, outcomes depend on data access, validation processes, regulatory compliance, clinical knowledge, and experimental design with partners. In other words, the value of an AI company lies not in “having a model,” but in its ability to **connect to a pharmaceutical company’s experimental systems, data systems, and decision systems**. This is structurally identical to the FDE-style approach.

The issue of fragmented state-level regulation is also critical. In an environment where regulation is not harmonized, improving the performance of a single model is not enough to build a durable advantage. What is needed instead is a platform that can adjust data handling, auditing, accountability, and operational workflows in response to local constraints. Here again, value resides less in model capability and more in **the implementation capacity to adapt and govern according to operational context**.

The rise of AI-native development organizations points in the same direction. The focus there is not simply on headcount reduction, but on small teams increasing development speed with AI as a given, and reorganizing the rhythm from specification through implementation, testing, and deployment. These facts suggest that **the winners and losers in AI adoption will be determined not by how much technology they possess, but by how quickly they can update their organizational structures and work design**.

## Presenting the Common Principle

Taken together inductively, the most plausible underlying principle can be formulated as follows:

### Common Principle: In the AI Era, Competitive Advantage Comes Not from “Intelligence Itself,” but from Control over the “Entry Points” Where Intelligence Flows In and the “Implementation Surface” Where It Is Fixed into Work

Why can we say this? First, Services-Led Growth centered on the FDE shows that AI’s value does not arise autonomously from a standalone product, but **emerges only when it is deeply connected to the customer’s actual operating environment**. Second, GitLab Act 2 shows that the value of AI agents is not maximized through isolated code generation, but **within the governance, audit, and connectivity structure of DevSecOps as a whole**. Third, pharmaceutical partnerships and regulatory fragmentation show that AI competition cannot be reduced to model competition, but depends on **whether AI can be embedded into the institutional and operational contexts of each industry and geography**.

These facts suggest that the AI market is even more “context-dependent” than the traditional software market. Models can commoditize rapidly, but the entry points into operational data, execution permissions, audit workflows, front-line know-how, and compliance-oriented operational design are much harder to commoditize. That is why companies will not win by selling “the smartest AI,” but by securing **the deployment surface for AI that is most deeply embedded in the real world**.

The So What is clear. The strongest companies in the future AI market will not necessarily be the model developers. More likely, the winners will be the companies that control customer workflows, development platforms, regulatory operations, and data touchpoints—and can implement multiple models and agents there in a swappable way. Put differently, the winners may be not “the companies that build AI,” but **the companies that design the places where AI works**.

## Forecasts and Implications

This principle leads to four future predictions.

### 1. AI Companies with High Service Ratios Are Likely to Be Revalued

The probability is **high (around 70%)**. The reason is that generative AI adoption is still in an exploratory phase, and customer problems have not yet become standardized. The high-touch model that traditional SaaS disliked becomes, in AI, a source of learning velocity. Companies that employ FDE-type talent in particular may be disadvantaged on short-term gross margin, but are better positioned to accumulate material for long-term productization.

### 2. DevSecOps Platforms Are Likely to Evolve into “AI Agent Governance Platforms”

The probability is **high (around 75%)**. As GitLab’s Act 2 makes clear, future differentiation will not come only from the accuracy of completions inside the IDE. What matters is what to delegate to agents, where to review, how to audit, and where to stop them. The value of platforms that unify CI/CD, security, access control, and audit logs will rise.

### 3. Vertical AI Will Win Through “Operational Specialization,” Not “Model Specialization”

The probability is **very high (around 80%)**. In heavily regulated industries such as pharmaceuticals, finance, and the public sector, adoption is shaped less by model intelligence than by validation processes, accountability, and data handling. Accordingly, future vertical AI companies will likely differentiate themselves less through proprietary models than through workflows, templates, audit design, and implementation support.

### 4. Regulatory Fragmentation Favors Large Companies, but Can Become a Tailwind for Startups with Adaptive Infrastructure

The probability is **above moderate (around 60%)**. Differences across states and countries make simple scale strategies harder, but they also act as barriers to entry in favor of companies with flexible configuration management, data separation, and audit design. In other words, regulation is both a growth constraint and a moat for companies with strong implementation capability.

The practical implications are clear. First, when evaluating an AI strategy, one should look not at “what model it uses,” but at **which business entry points it controls**. Second, product organizations should redesign service divisions not as cost centers, but as **market-learning engines**. Third, companies that own development platforms should prioritize not additional AI features, but the **governance layer that allows agents to work safely**. Fourth, executives need to understand AI adoption not merely as an automation investment, but as a **reconfiguration of data inflow points and the operating system of work**.

Ultimately, the real issue in the AI era is not “which model is the smartest.” It is **which company can control the interface where intelligence is actually converted into value**. The Forward Deployed Engineer, GitLab Act 2, and AI-native organizations all converge on that single point.
