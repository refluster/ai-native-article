Being able to observe and being able to stop are different capabilities, and the current argument about AI spending confuses the two. An agent loop that burned $2.84 of a $4.24 budget without producing a single point of improvement; a data-centre machinery backlog that has held steady just under six months; AI-related capital expenditure reaching a $1.5 trillion annual rate — these look like events at three unrelated scales. In fact one principle explains all three. Each describes a domain where the instruments are abundant and only the condition for stopping was never written.

## A perfectly observed loop threw away 67% of its budget

The figures reported in "When should a loop stop — a16z's four conditions for convergence, and the Lighthouse experiment where 67% bought nothing" are the cleanest laboratory version of this principle. A loop equipped with a crisp instrument — the Lighthouse score — and able to read its current state at every step spent the first $1.40 of a $4.24 total driving the score from 26 to 89, then spent the remaining $2.84 — 67% of the whole — on exactly zero points.

What matters is that the failure was not a lack of observation. The score was legible to the end. The loop still did not stop. Of the four convergence conditions the piece sets out — a goal state, an observable current state, a means of local edit, and a stopping rule — the only one this loop lacked was the fourth. Observability is one condition of four, and on its own it guarantees nothing.

### The verifier's ceiling

The same piece adds a second constraint that is easy to miss: a loop's quality never exceeds the verifier at each step. Whatever Lighthouse cannot measure does not improve, however many dollars are poured in. So the instrument does nothing to halt spending, yet it does set the ceiling on what spending can achieve. The freedom to keep going and the ceiling on the result sit on two sides of the same gauge.

## The demand instruments were measuring something other than demand

Change scale. "AI infrastructure demand is not slowing — a16z's data on a six-month backlog, 40% higher H100 rents, and a 5% entry-level hiring share" tests the three indicators most often cited as evidence of a slowdown and finds that none of them shows demand receding. The data-centre machinery backlog has stabilised just under six months; twelve-month contract rents for H100s are up roughly 40% against the previous November; the share of entry-level tech hiring has sat at about 5% since 2019.

The confusion here runs the opposite way from the previous section. In the loop, the instrument was present and nothing stopped. Here the instrument itself is being misread — a stable backlog reflects a supply constraint rather than saturated demand, and a low graduate-hiring share is a structure that predates AI. On that piece's reading, most of the indicators treated as slowdown signals are measuring something that is not demand.

Using a number as the basis for a decision without first establishing what it measures is a quieter failure than the absence of a stopping rule, and it gets worse the more observation you add: with enough indicators, one of them will always be pointing the way you hoped.

## A $1.5 trillion annual rate arrives with no condition for enough

The third piece, "AI-related private capital expenditure hits a $1.5 trillion annual rate — what is actually inside the claim that it accounts for 25–33% of US GDP growth," handles a number that is overwhelming as a level: up $300 billion (+25%) year over year in Q2 2026, a record $1.5 trillion annualised. Computers and peripherals led, followed by communications equipment, software and data centres. The post attributes 25% to 33% of recent US GDP growth directly to AI investment.

What the piece treats carefully is provenance: this is a post from a market-commentary account, not a statistical agency's release. But set the provenance limit aside and something decisive is still missing. Nothing attached to the number says how much would be enough, or what would count as too much. A share of 25–33% of GDP growth is an attribution, not a rate of return and not a condition of achievement. The precision of a level and the material for judging whether the level is warranted are entirely separate things.

## The unifying principle — a stopping rule comes from outside the instruments

These facts suggest the following: **abundant instrumentation is not a substitute for a stopping rule, and the two must be built independently.**

The first piece supplies the proof in experimental form: with perfect observability but no stopping rule, 67% of the spend can be worthless. The second shows that the instrument side carries its own independent failure mode — the gauge exists, but what it measures is not what you think. The third shows the state where both are missing: the level is reported precisely, and nobody has written the rule that decides whether the level is sufficient.

Why does this happen? Because a stopping rule comes from somewhere other than observation. Observation can be generated inside the system — compute the score, count the backlog months, total the quarter's capital expenditure. But the judgement "stop here" requires an objective function that sits outside the system: what is the spending for, and at what level is the purpose met? It is no accident that the first piece brackets its four conditions with a goal state at one end and a stopping rule at the other. They are two ends of the same object, and neither emerges from inside the loop.

Instrumentation also enjoys a strong tailwind. Gauges accumulate when you build them; the more there are, the more explanatory they look, and the more they resemble material for a decision. A stopping rule has no such tailwind. Writing one removes options, halts spending and ends somebody's plan. So observation grows on one side only, and that asymmetry produces exactly the state we are in: more instruments, and no stop.

The principle is falsifiable. If abundant observation alone produced convergence — if a loop that could read its Lighthouse score had come to rest near $1.40 without any stopping rule — the claim would be false. The experiment ran the other way.

## What follows, and what to do about it

First, a slowdown in AI infrastructure investment is more likely to appear as the introduction of a stopping rule than as a reversal in demand indicators (confidence: medium to high). The grounds are the character of the instruments described in the second piece. Backlogs and H100 rents both reflect supply constraints; neither is built to lead a saturation in demand. So long as we watch the current indicator set, the turning point will only be observable after the fact. If deceleration does begin, it will be because some spender finally wrote down "this level is enough," not because a chart turned down.

Second, in agent engineering, designing the stopping rule becomes work of equal standing with designing the verifier (confidence: high). The first piece's four conditions can be used directly as an implementation checklist: can the goal state be written, can the current state be read, can edits be made locally, and is there a condition for stopping? The 67% figure is worth carrying as one example of what the fourth condition's absence costs.

Third, investment in instruments and investment in stopping rules will increasingly be evaluated separately (confidence: medium). The former raises the ceiling on results; the latter cuts the floor out from under spending. The constraint the first piece names — that a loop's quality never exceeds its verifier — does not mean observation is a poor investment. What is wasteful is counting investment in observation as though it were a stopping rule.

The practical takeaway reduces to one test. For whatever loop you are running — a coding agent, or an internal investment plan — check whether you can write the sentence "we stop when X" in one line. If you cannot, the problem is not insufficient observation. No amount of added observation will produce that sentence.
