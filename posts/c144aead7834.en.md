## Executive Summary

In “How Agents Are Transforming Work,” OpenAI argues that AI agents are changing the unit of knowledge work from “single interactions” to “delegated, long-horizon tasks,” and backs that claim with internal usage data. As of May 2026, 80.6% of individual-user requests amounted to more than 30 minutes of human work, and 25.6% exceeded 8 hours; meanwhile, the coding assistant Codex had spread into non-engineering functions including legal, finance, and recruiting. The data presented shows that agents are lowering the cost of working across domain boundaries, while also suggesting that for those benefits to reach an entire organization, workflows themselves must be redesigned.

## The Difference Between Chatbots and Agents—From “Single Interactions” to “Long-Horizon Autonomous Execution”

OpenAI condenses the article’s central claim into a single sentence: “Agentic AI changes the unit of knowledge work from single interactions to delegated, long-horizon tasks.”

The difference between the two lies in runtime and autonomy. The article notes that “Chatbot interactions are often short and self-contained,” whereas agents “operate independently for minutes or hours while orchestrating tool calls, interacting with environments, and iterating towards solutions.”

- Chatbots: short, self-contained one-off exchanges
- Agents: autonomous execution lasting from minutes to hours, orchestrating multiple tool calls and iterating while interacting with environments
This distinction matters. The usage statistics presented later are all measured along the axis of how long the delegated task would take a human worker to complete, positioning the value of agents not as “faster answers” but as the ability to take over extended stretches of work.

## Delegated Tasks Are Getting Longer—80.6% Exceed 30 Minutes, 25.6% Exceed 8 Hours

The most concrete evidence presented in the article is the distribution of requests by threshold, measured in terms of how long they would take a human to complete. For individual users as of May 2026, it gives the following figures:

- 80.6%: requests equivalent to more than 30 minutes of human work
- 70.2%: requests equivalent to more than 1 hour of human work
- 25.6%: requests equivalent to more than 8 hours of human work
OpenAI summarizes this as: “Nearly a quarter of all Codex requests are for tasks that would take a person more than one hour to complete.”

The measurement is based on four human-work-time thresholds—30 minutes, 1 hour, 4 hours, and 8 hours—and how many users exceeded them. It is worth noting that the article itself does not spell out the estimation methodology in detail, but every one of these figures points in the same direction: usage is shifting away from answering short questions and toward taking over work that would otherwise require sustained human effort over long periods.

## Cross-Functional Penetration—Codex Spreads to Non-Engineers

Agent usage is not confined to technical teams. The article gives concrete growth multiples for adoption and penetration by department.

- Since August 2025, non-developer users increased by 137x among individuals, 189x in organizations, and 12x internally at OpenAI
- Codex accounts for 99.8% of weekly output tokens inside OpenAI
- Usage in the research organization increased by a median of 56x from November 2025 to June 2026
- Usage in customer support increased by 32x over the same period
- As of June 2026, users in the top 1 percentile were generating more than 60 hours of agent work per day
The timeline of departmental transition is also clear. Engineering had shifted to Codex-centered usage by December 2025, followed by legal, finance, and recruiting by April 2026. In those departments, users were generating more than 85% of their output tokens via Codex.

What stands out is the direction of this boundary crossing. According to the article, more than a quarter of Codex work by employees in business functions involves engineering or coding tasks. Workers in non-technical departments are beginning to use agents for “automation, data transformation, tooling, debugging, and structured analysis.”

## Implications—Lowering the “Cost of Crossing Work Boundaries” and Rebuilding Workflow Design

From these figures, OpenAI draws a core conclusion: agents reduce the friction involved in crossing specialized task boundaries. The article says, “Agents can lower the cost of moving across task boundaries,” and links that to the broader assessment that “agents are quickly becoming the most powerful AI tool for work.”

This is where the evidence and the conclusion need to be read separately. What the data establishes is that use of coding-related tasks has increased in non-engineering departments, and that delegated tasks are becoming longer. To move from there to the conclusion that organizational productivity will change requires several intervening conditions—conditions the article itself acknowledges—including redesigning workflow bottlenecks, establishing oversight structures, and rethinking how humans and AI collaborate.

In other words, the value of this article lies not in asking whether agents are useful, but in quantifying with internal data which departments are already using them, and for how long the tasks being delegated actually are. The fact that employees in departments such as legal, finance, and recruiting are generating more than 85% of their output tokens via Codex shows that the debate over agent adoption has moved beyond technical validation and into the realm of workflow design: who gets to work across which boundaries, and how.
