## Executive Summary

In a post published on 15 April 2026, two Uber product managers summarise a year of using AI prototyping across the Product organisation. The core change is sequence, not speed: prototypes arrive earlier, which pulls exploration, alignment and the start of execution forward, and the PRD moves from the place where an idea is defined to the place where intent, tradeoffs and decisions are recorded. Uber's conclusion is not to drop the PRD but to pair it with a prototype.

## What happened (facts from the source)

The authors are Aayush Agrawal and Akanksha Sharma, both Product Managers at Uber. The post opens with a quote from a product manager on the Merchant team: “Two Hours of Prototyping Unblocked Four Weeks of Discussion.”

That manager used AI to customise a prototype to one merchant's catalog as part of product research. The merchant understood it immediately and gave specific, actionable feedback, and internal alignment followed just as quickly.

By “AI prototyping” Uber means using AI-assisted tools such as Lovable, Figma Make, Claude Code and Cursor to generate and iterate on interactive flows quickly, so teams can test assumptions, gather feedback and align before decisions harden. The post says it was not driven by a mandate or a new process; teams reached for prototypes when they needed to move faster or work through ambiguity. One signal it cites: nearly 40% of submissions to the global Uber tech hackathon incorporated a prototyping tool.

## Three patterns

**1. Greater exploration of ideas.** Exploring several directions used to be expensive and coordination-heavy, which pushed teams to converge early. A product manager on the Business Platform team explored six distinct concepts for the same problem in about 20 minutes, work that previously took multiple iteration cycles. The post stresses that the change was not speed for its own sake: exploration moved earlier, so assumptions could be tested before PRDs were locked and before architecture decisions hardened.

**2. Faster alignment.** Alignment is where the post sees the clearest impact. Conversations moved from “what is this?” to “is this the right approach?” One PM on complex cross-functional systems said, “We achieved high-level alignment across product, partners, design, and engineering with much lower turnaround time than a document-only process.” A PM on a platform team said showing a prototype made it easier for senior stakeholders to understand the direction and engage in more substantive discussions.

**3. Unblocked execution.** Execution rarely stalls because teams disagree, the post argues; it stalls because key questions surface too late. Scope, sequencing and what counts as the MVP tend to appear just as engineering is ready to start. Prototyping pulled them forward. As one PM put it, “having a prototype made it much easier to distinguish MVP from post-MVP.” Execution did not get faster because steps were skipped, but because teams entered each step with fewer unknowns.

## The PRD did not die; it became a sidekick

As prototyping spread, the question came up: do we still need PRDs? Uber found the opposite. A prototype is an experiential artifact: it shows what an idea might look like or how it might work, not why a direction was chosen, what tradeoffs were made, or which constraints shaped it. In the post's words, if prototypes help teams experience the future, PRDs help teams align on it.

- A prototype without a PRD can drift away from the problem the team intends to solve.
- A PRD without a prototype can stay abstract and leave room for inconsistent interpretations.
Where legal, ops, policy, marketing, data science and platform teams must review and sign off, a prototype speeds early conversations but the PRD still carries the detail needed for final decisions. Uber compares the pair to Batman and Robin and describes the flow as problem framing → early prototyping → a sharper, more informed PRD → final designs.

The post also gives a rule of thumb for where to start.

- **Prototype-first** if the problem area is ambiguous or unfamiliar, the team needs fast stakeholder alignment, the solution space needs several directions, user-flow complexity is high, the initiative needs leadership buy-in to unlock resources, the product is internal-facing or low-stakes, or the goal is early vision exploration.
- **PRD-first** if the product is mobile-first (AI prototyping tools often underperform there currently), the problem is strategic rather than UI-heavy, the product is mature and changes have ripple effects, the domain is high-risk, compliance-sensitive or safety-critical, dependencies are complex (legal, infra, data), the scope modifies an existing feature with clear constraints, or the product is external-facing with high fidelity expectations.
The post names prototypes being mistaken for decisions as the main pitfall and recommends prototyping with guardrails. It summarises pitfalls and guardrails in Figure 4, whose contents are not reproduced in the text, so this article does not describe them.

## What changed when everyone could prototype

Uber decided early to open AI prototyping beyond product teams. Engineers used prototypes to spin up interactive dashboards and rethink engineering workflows; operational teams used them to simplify processes and explore internal tools. For example, EMEA sales teams built their own opportunity tracker to find relevant leads instead of relying on an off-the-shelf tool. The post argues that when ideas can be shared in a form others can interact with, the best ideas rise regardless of where they come from, and ties this to the Uber value “Great Minds Don’t Think Alike.”

## Three open questions

The post says the question has shifted from whether this works to how the rest of the product system should evolve around it.

1. **The role of the PRD.** If going from idea to prototype is fast and cheap, the PRD can no longer be the primary place ideas are defined. Its value lies in capturing intent, tradeoffs, success metrics and decisions. Uber is exploring where AI can help draft that thinking and where human judgment must stay explicit and durable. 2. **Gathering insights faster.** When prototypes are easy to make, the bottleneck moves from building artifacts to learning from them. Uber is thinking about how AI can synthesise feedback, surface patterns and turn interactions into insights at the same pace. 3. **From prototype to production.** Production requires rigor. Uber is working through how prototypes inform architecture, design systems and engineering work without being mistaken for shippable solutions. The challenge, it says, is not speed alone but knowing when to move from exploration to commitment.

## Reading it: evidence apart from conclusion

**Evidence.** The post gives three numbers: one case of two hours against four weeks, six concepts in about 20 minutes, and nearly 40% of hackathon submissions using a prototyping tool. Most of the effect is reported through PM testimony. The post gives no company-wide measure such as a change in iteration cycles or release speed. Its conclusion says the patterns are already translating into faster iteration cycles, earlier alignment and clearer decisions before teams commit to building, but it does not say how that was measured.

**Implications.** First, what got cheaper is the prototype, not the decision. As prototypes get cheaper, the document that records why a decision was made becomes relatively scarcer. Second, the post places the benefit where coordination cost is the main constraint, as in Uber's multi-region, multi-function structure. Third, on verification and trust, the authors themselves list the guardrails against prototypes being mistaken for decisions, and the criteria for moving to production, as open questions.

For a smaller team, the prototype-first versus PRD-first list is the most portable part. It is a rule of thumb drawn from Uber's own observation, not an externally validated standard.
