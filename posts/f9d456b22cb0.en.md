## AI Economics × Rebuilding the Agent Foundation

## One Principle That Runs Through Token Runaway, Massive Funding, and GitLab’s Redesign

In the AI industry, events that seem unrelated at first glance are unfolding at the same time. One company received a **$500 million-scale Claude bill** because of a missing usage cap, and **Uber had exhausted its 2026 AI coding budget by April**. Meanwhile, Anthropic **raised $3.5 billion**, pushing its valuation to **$61.5 billion**. And GitLab has begun redesigning itself from human-centered DevSecOps into a **software creation platform for the agentic era**. In fact, all of these developments can be explained by the same principle.

## Analysis 1: Why Is Total Spending Exploding Even as Unit Prices Fall?

### The AI paradox: the cheaper it gets, the more people overspend

The first fact shown by the L2 articles is that **declining per-token prices are not leading companies to rein in AI spending**. Quite the opposite. The articles point out that as companies aggressively adopt AI and autonomous agents execute tasks continuously, **token consumption itself is surging**, breaking through budgets.

The most symbolic example is that **Uber had exhausted its 2026 AI coding budget by April**. The fact that an annual budget evaporated in the first few months of the year shows that what determines spending is not unit price but the **nonlinear growth of usage**. In another case, a company generated a **$500 million Claude invoice** because of a **missing usage cap**. This is not merely a management mistake. It is an extreme example showing that once AI usage shifts from “a tool humans call on each time” to “a system that continues running recursively on its own,” the traditional SaaS-style intuition for costs no longer applies.

It is also important that, against this backdrop of confusion, the Linux Foundation has begun standardization efforts around **Toke… (in the context of token measurement and standardization)**. Standardization normally happens when a market matures. This time, however, **unmeasurable cost inflation** arrived before maturity did. These facts suggest that the bottleneck in the AI market is shifting from a race over performance to **consumption governance**.

### Agentification changes the cost structure

With conventional API usage, human clicks and explicit requests effectively imposed a ceiling on consumption. Agents, however, plan, break down tasks, search, retry, call other tools, and retain long contexts when needed. Because the “invisible amount of computation” per task rises sharply, **one cheap call** turns into a **large expensive total**.

These facts suggest that AI costs are no longer governed by the simple formula of “unit price × number of calls,” but by a compound function of **degree of autonomy × number of iterations × context length × number of tool calls**. In other words, companies are not buying a model; they are buying **a probabilistically expanding computational activity**.

## Analysis 2: Why Is So Much Capital Flowing In Now?

### What Anthropic’s major funding round means

The second fact is that even as costs run wild, capital is becoming even more concentrated in frontier model companies. Anthropic announced that it **raised $3.5 billion in Series H**, reaching a **post-money valuation of $61.5 billion**. The company said the funds would be used for **developing next-generation AI systems**, **expanding compute capacity**, and **accelerating research**.

What matters here is that investors do not appear to believe that “AI demand will stall because of cost problems.” If anything, they likely see cost chaos as increasing demand for **foundation models that are more capable, safer, and better able to withstand enterprise deployment**. In other words, capital markets are beginning to value AI not as a luxury good but as **core infrastructure that requires control**.

The valuation of **$61.5 billion** is itself telling. It is difficult to explain that number simply as a multiple of current revenue. What the market seems to be pricing in is not just future growth in model usage, but the possibility that model providers will control a broad stack that includes **inference costs, routing, guardrails, billing design, and the infrastructure for operating agents**. These facts suggest that the enterprise value of AI companies is beginning to depend not just on whether they can build smart models, but on whether they can **institutionalize and monetize expanding usage**.

### Capital is betting not on “intelligence” but on the “supply chain of intelligence”

Huge investment in next-generation AI development is not only about training. Embedding AI into business operations requires the surrounding stack: inference infrastructure, deployment, observability, security, enterprise readiness, SLAs, legal support, audit logs, and more. In the age of agents in particular, what determines adoption is not the quality of a single response so much as the **predictability of budgets at scale**. Anthropic’s funding round reflects intensifying competition to control this entire supply chain.

## Analysis 3: What GitLab’s “Act 2” Says About the Redefinition of the Software Foundation

### From DevSecOps to an “agent operations layer”

The third fact is that not only AI companies but also existing software infrastructure companies are being forced to redesign themselves. GitLab’s “**Act 2**” argues that AI will reshape software development and DevSecOps as a whole, and positions the company’s next phase as a **software creation platform for the agentic era**.

What should not be overlooked here is that GitLab is not treating AI as a mere coding-assistance feature. The problem itself is being redefined. GitLab is trying to move from a tool that supports human developers to a **foundation built on the assumption that AI agents participate in code generation, review, testing, fixing, and operational tasks**.

This shift is directly connected to the cost issue described earlier. Once agents enter the SDLC, the volume of code generated, the number of tests run, the number of pipeline executions, the number of vulnerability scans, and the amount of context loaded all increase. In other words, the more development productivity rises, the more **token consumption and compute consumption are amplified** behind the scenes. GitLab’s redesign is not simply about “adding AI”; it is an answer to the question of **how to govern a development lifecycle in which AI consumption is built into the premise**.

### The new value controlled by platform companies for software creation

GitLab’s position is an interesting one. It is not a model company, but because it controls the flow of development from entrance to exit, it could potentially observe how much AI is used at each stage, where the cost-effectiveness is high, and where waste occurs. That means something beyond simple feature integration. In the future, value will belong not only to the highest-performing model, but to the **layer that can control who should use how much inference, where, and for what work**. These facts suggest that the competitive axis of software infrastructure is shifting from UI and workflow to the **allocation and governance of AI execution**.

## Presenting the Common Principle

### The essence of the AI market is not “the democratization of intelligence” but “the institutionalization of compute consumption”

If we line up the facts so far inductively, a shared pattern comes into view. **Per-token prices are falling, yet total spending is rising**. Extreme accidents occur, such as a **$500 million bill** caused by inadequate usage limits. **Uber’s budget is exhausted at the start of the fiscal year**. In response, the **Linux Foundation moves toward standardization**. At the same time, **$3.5 billion** in capital flows into Anthropic, while GitLab redesigns itself around a **platform for the agentic era**. These facts suggest that the central issue in AI adoption has shifted from “how to improve model performance” to “how to measure, constrain, and convert expanding intelligence consumption into business value.”

Put succinctly, this underlying principle is that **AI is not a software feature but a self-propagating demand for computation**. Traditional software had relatively predictable marginal costs after deployment. But generative AI—especially agents—expands its own computation in proportion to the discretion it is given, decomposes tasks, and demands additional context. So even when companies think they are buying an “AI feature,” what they are actually operating is **the right to consume computational resources that are variable and sometimes explosive**.

That is why the standardization now underway, the massive funding rounds, and the redesign of platforms are not separate stories. Standardization is happening **to measure consumption**. Massive funding is happening **to supply that consumption**. Platform redesign is happening **to connect that consumption to work**. If you push the Why to its limit, the value of AI lies not in intelligence itself but in the ability to **use intelligence reproducibly within budget**. The So What is that the next winners will not be the makers of the highest-performing models alone, but the companies that possess **a stack capable of controlling consumption while maximizing outcomes**.

## Future Outlook and Implications

### Changes that will unfold over the next 3 years

The future implied by this principle is fairly clear. First, **AI cost management is likely to become an independent management domain, not just a peripheral function of FinOps**. The probability is **high (70-80%)**. The reason is that real damage has already appeared in the form of budget overruns and massive bills, and organizations at the scale of the Linux Foundation have already begun moving toward standardization. Companies will likely begin managing KPIs such as token volume, context length, number of tool calls, agent retry rate, and cost per successful task.

Second, the competitive axis for model companies will broaden from performance benchmarks to **budget predictability, governance features, and pricing design for agents**. This, too, is likely to proceed with **high probability (65-75%)**. That is because major funding rounds like Anthropic’s can be interpreted not merely as research capital, but as upfront investment to meet enterprise operational requirements. In the future, there will be more situations where companies choose not “the smartest model,” but the **model/router with the most stable cost per completed business task**.

Third, platform companies in the GitLab mold may evolve from development-support tools into an **AI execution orchestration layer**. The probability is **moderately high (60-70%)**. Competitive advantage will come not just from code generation, but from deciding where to use expensive models, where small models are sufficient, and when to insert human approval into the process.

### Practical implications for readers

For practitioners, the key is not to treat AI adoption as an extension of a PoC. The first requirement is to implement **usage caps, budget guardrails, alerts, and approval workflows**. Next, KPIs should shift away from “number of uses” and toward **cost per outcome**. For example, measure cost per “completed PR review,” per “resolved support case,” or per “generated test.” And rather than using the highest-performing model across every stage, it is better to design around **model tiering** and **task-specific routing** from the outset.

Finally, management needs to understand AI budgets not as a standalone IT expense item, but as **a new form of labor input accompanied by compute consumption**. A world in which labor costs have management accounting but AI agents do not will not last for long. The companies that will be competitive in the future are not the ones that use the most AI. They are the companies that can **govern AI consumption and convert it into reproducible profit**.
