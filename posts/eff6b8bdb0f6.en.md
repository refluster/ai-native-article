## Executive Summary

According to Capital & Main on July 29, 2026, backlash against data center construction in Pennsylvania has emerged as a central issue in the November 2026 governor’s race and state legislative elections. An Emerson College survey found that the share of U.S. voters who said they oppose construction of a data center “in their community or nearby” rose from 42% last December to 63% by late July, while a March survey by Muhlenberg College found that 64% of adults in the state view data centers as a “crisis” or a “problem.” The issue has not broken through via environmental activism, but through electricity bills, and bipartisan resistance is reshaping existing political coalitions.

## Backlash Is Now Showing Up in Voting Behavior

In Archbald in the northeast, adjacent to a former coal-mining site, nurse Kelsey Pazanski Wargo, who lives within 1 mile of the planned site of a 400-acre data center complex, laid out a simple standard in a text message: “I won’t vote for a candidate who isn’t being strong against them.”

In Skippack, 100 miles to the south, healthcare advocate Peg Eitl drew a different line. She voted for Governor Josh Shapiro in 2022, went door to door for him, and has been registered in the past as both a Democrat and a Republican. “I respect and appreciate many of his policies. But unless he pushes a moratorium, I won’t vote for him.” At the same time, she says she will not vote for his Republican opponent, Stacy Garrity, either.

Chris Borick, a political science professor at Lehigh University, points to packed town halls, protests, and polling, and says that “this is the first year data centers have really emerged as a high-salience issue in Pennsylvania.” The issue is not standing alone. According to Borick, it has fused in voters’ minds with the biggest issue of all: the cost of living.

## Two Candidates Converged on the Same Issue from Opposite Directions

In June, State Treasurer Garrity laid out a natural-gas-first policy agenda at a Marcellus Shale Coalition gathering in Hershey. It included removing barriers to drilling, speeding permits for pipelines and power plants, and rolling back renewable energy and energy efficiency mandates. In a statement to Spotlight PA in early July, she made clear that she opposed the “clean energy mandate” included in the state’s data center legislation. “We should use the abundant natural gas resources beneath our feet. They currently account for 60% of the state’s power generation.”

Shapiro’s shift is a story in itself. In June 2025, when Amazon announced it would invest $20 billion to build two data center campuses in Bucks County and Luzerne County, he said: “Thanks to Amazon, I’m relieved that the future of AI runs through this Commonwealth.” He has since changed course. The standards proposal he presented in June would require data centers, in exchange for existing tax incentives, to source at least 32% of their operations from clean energy and to sign community benefits agreements. In his February address to the state legislature, he said, “We have to be selective about the projects that get built here.”

The latest poll from Franklin & Marshall College shows Shapiro leading Garrity 50% to 28%, well beyond the margin of error.

## Actual Regulation Has Been Stuck in a Senate Committee for 4 Months

Even as the politics move, the legislation has not. HB 1834, a bill passed by the House in March, would give the state Public Utility Commission (PUC) authority to manage costs so that data center grid connection costs are not shifted onto ratepayers. Its prime sponsor, Democratic Representative Robert Matzie of Beaver County, calls it “essentially a ratepayer protection bill for data centers.” It also includes provisions aimed at skeptics — data centers could still get 90% of their electricity from fossil fuels, and the initial 10% clean energy requirement could be met with nuclear power. Nuclear already accounts for 40% of the state’s power mix.

Even so, the vote split sharply. It passed 104 to 95, with every no vote coming from Republicans. The bill has remained in a Senate committee since March 31.

A related bill introduced by Representative Paul Friel passed 201 to 1. That one would bar data centers from claiming public utility status — meaning they would no longer be able to override local zoning rules or exercise eminent domain.

Katie Blume, political director of Conservation Voters of PA, sums up the common thread in the polling this way: residents do not want data centers next door, but “if they’re going to come, people want them regulated.” She also points to the experience of fracking, where people remained split between support and opposition and “everybody forgot about really strong regulations that protect workers and communities and the environment.” In her view, “Pennsylvania now has a chance to do what it didn’t do then.” Her read is that Senate Republicans are seeing the same survey data. “The same senators who a year ago were introducing bills to welcome them and strip away regulation are now introducing bill after bill to regulate data centers.”

## The Coalitions Are Being Rewired

The backlash has scrambled existing political alignments. Construction unions, which have historically clashed with tech companies over organizing and allied with Democrats, are “all in” on data centers, and Borick calls it a case of “strange bedfellows.” At the same time, the MAGA base and progressives are showing rare agreement around the argument that wealthy outside interests are profiting while locals bear the burden.

Views on jobs break into two layers. Polling last fall found that about 61% of Pennsylvania adults believe data centers will create new jobs in the state. But according to Borick, there is skepticism about whether those jobs will last. Speaking about the Scranton area where he grew up — an old mining region where data centers are clustering — he said, “People are already skeptical of the energy industry. They’ve been burned too many times. There’s this lingering Pennsylvania skepticism that industries come in, take what they want, leave problems behind, and move on.”

Garrity is trying to flip that script. She points to Homer City, where a former coal-fired power plant is being converted into a power source for data centers, as evidence that blighted land can be turned to local benefit, and at a June community meeting in Montgomery County she told reporters, “The community is actually getting better. There’s more housing and more jobs.” At the same time, she acknowledged that some residents are “not totally pleased.”

## Climate Policy Is Advancing in the Language of Electricity Bills

The state legislature is divided — Republicans control the Senate, Democrats the House, and both chambers are on the ballot in November. On top of that, 4 congressional districts Democrats are targeting in their bid to retake the U.S. House will be voting the same day.

In Borick’s phrasing, climate hangs over all of it, mostly unnamed — policymaking by “stealth means.” The numbers are moving. This year, 44% of Pennsylvania adults said climate change is a “crisis,” up 15 points from 2023 and the highest level on record. Of the heat wave that hit tens of millions of Americans this summer from the Midwest to the East Coast, scientists said it would have been “virtually impossible” without global warming, and Philadelphia hit 103 degrees (Fahrenheit), canceling its Independence Day parade.

For Blume, anger over rising electricity bills is not a detour from climate politics but an entry point into it. “Climate change is hard to think about when it feels so existential. But when your electricity bill goes up, or when a data center is coming that’s going to use all your water, environmental issues become much more tangible for voters.”

Whether AI infrastructure gets built is beginning to be decided in state-level elections through the language of rates, water, and zoning. What is being contested here is not the value of the technology itself, but the design of distribution: who pays the costs, and who gets a say in the decision.
