## Introduction

An observation on X that AI is changing the very way software development is carried out. A playbook for founders that frames building a company in the age of AI not as “adding more people” but as “designing systems.” And the fact that Cofounder 2 has declared it will “run the entire company by coordinating agents across 4 departments.” These may look unrelated at first glance, but they can in fact be explained by the same principle. **The essential value of AI is shifting away from automating individual tasks and toward the orchestration of cross-organizational decision-making and execution.**

## Analysis 1: What’s happening in development is not “coding efficiency” but a redesign of the SDLC

The first item under observation, the X post “shin_sasaki19’s AI and Software Development,” may be a memo built around URL-based confirmation, but it includes an important point: **AI is changing software development workflows and prompting a rethink of parts of the traditional SDLC.** What matters here is that the unit of change is not “individual productivity” but “the connections between stages.”

Conventional thinking about development assumed a division of labor across stages such as requirements definition, design, implementation, testing, and operations. But once AI intervenes in each of those stages, the conversion cost from specification to prototype, code, and test cases drops sharply. As that happens, the source of value shifts from “who writes it” to “how it flows.” For example, if natural-language requirements can be followed by a semi-automated chain that produces a design draft, UI mock, code skeleton, and generated tests, then the bottleneck is no longer implementation speed but **managing consistency across stages**.

That fact suggests that the primary battleground for AI adoption is not localized functions like IDEs or code completion, but **the connective surface from planning through shipment**. In other words, the question development organizations need to confront is no longer “What percentage faster can we write?” but “Which decisions should humans own, which should machines handle, and how should we audit them?” The X post’s suggestion that “the SDLC needs to be revisited” is not merely an impression. It is a sign that software development is **changing from a workflow optimization problem into a multi-agent control problem**.

## Analysis 2: The founders’ playbook is beginning to see the company not as “headcount expansion” but as a “designable system”

Next, “The Founders' Playbook,” as far as can be confirmed from the provided text, is positioned as a practical document about **how to build and scale a company in an AI-driven environment**. The detailed body of the PDF is limited here, but what can be read from the title and description is that its intended audience is not engineers but founders, and that its focus is not tool usage but **managerial design**.

This is extremely significant. If AI’s impact were limited to simple work assistance, then a document for founders would still center on conventional topics such as hiring, sales, talent evaluation, and fundraising. But the reason an AI-era playbook is needed is that the scaling laws of the company itself are changing. Specifically, whereas traditional SaaS companies have increased headcount in development, CS, operations, and marketing roughly in proportion to revenue growth, AI-native companies make it newly realistic to **design systems that produce the same output with fewer people**.

The implication here is clear. The founder’s role is expanding beyond simply “gathering talented people” to becoming **the designer of execution units that include AI agents**. The playbook format itself signals a desire for a reproducible organizational OS. These facts suggest that the target of AI adoption is shifting from individual tools to the company as a whole, and that corporate competitive advantage is moving from “hiring power” to “orchestration capability.”

## Analysis 3: By coordinating “agents across 4 departments,” Cofounder 2 raises AI’s unit of value to the cross-functional level

The third piece of evidence, the article on Cofounder 2, provides the most concrete support for this hypothesis. The key point is straightforward: the company presents its second-generation product as **an orchestration platform for coordinating agents across 4 departments and running the company as a whole**. The three phrases worth focusing on here are “4 departments,” “coordinating,” and “the company as a whole.”

First, the explicit reference to “4 departments” means AI’s scope is not limited to improving efficiency inside a single department. Second, the phrase “coordinating” shows that value does not emerge simply from multiple agents working independently; rather, **managing interdependencies** is the core. Third, the claim that it can “run the company as a whole” indicates that AI is being redefined not as an R&D tool but as infrastructure for managerial execution.

What is more, the description includes expanded automation across **planning, design, development workflows**. This reflects an attempt to treat the continuum of planning → design → implementation as a single control surface. Where conventional development-support AI was skewed toward code generation and review assistance, Cofounder 2 treats upstream decision-making and downstream implementation as one integrated system. This is not a mere feature expansion. It is an attempt to **replace interdepartmental handoffs inside the company with inter-agent protocols**.

That fact suggests that competition in the age of AI agents will shift from “who has the smartest model” to “who can connect departments most smoothly.” The more model intelligence becomes commoditized, the more the differentiator becomes integration design. Cofounder 2’s positioning is an early move into that future.

## Presenting the Shared Principle

When these observations are integrated, the most likely underlying principle can be formulated as follows: **As AI agents spread, the basic unit of the company is shifting from “departments people belong to” to an “orchestration layer” that connects intent, judgment, and execution.**

Why say that? What the X post showed was the fact that AI is reconnecting the stages of the SDLC and driving changes to the design of the process itself. What Founders' Playbook shows is the fact that building a company in the AI era is no longer an extension of hiring and management, but a design problem centered on systems that can run with small teams. What Cofounder 2 showed is that, as a concrete implementation of this, **platforms for coordinating groups of agents across 4 departments** are beginning to appear. These facts suggest that AI’s value resides not in isolated “points,” but in “surfaces,” and further in the “lines” that connect those surfaces.

Put differently, the first wave of generative AI was **local optimization**: text generation, code completion, search assistance, and the like. But in the second wave, what matters is not local performance so much as how consistently multiple units of work can be made to move together under a shared goal. What becomes necessary here is an orchestration layer. This is not merely an automation tool. It is “the driver’s seat of the company”: a system that breaks down goals, assigns them to the appropriate agents, validates outputs, and escalates exceptions to humans.

The so what is clear. Corporate competitiveness going forward will not be determined by model selection or prompt-writing tricks alone. It will be determined by **how well cross-functional work can be translated into machine-readable rules, states, and lines of responsibility**. In other words, AI strategy is not an IT adoption strategy. It is an organizational design strategy.

## Future Outlook and Implications

At least three future changes follow from this principle. First, the unit of AI-agent adoption is likely to shift **from the “individual seat” to the “functional team.”** In probability terms, this looks highly likely—roughly above 70%—because even in the observations here we already see expansion across the full development process, founders’ management design, and integration across 4 departments. Going forward, it will become easier to explain the ROI of “1 team, 1 orchestrator” than “1 person, 1 assistant.”

Second, **the org chart itself will be reorganized**. Traditional departments will remain, but above them there is likely to be a new owner for “AI operations design,” along with a new core function that fuses PM, BizOps, Platform, and Security. The probability is around 60%. The reason is that the more agent utilization advances, the more necessary it becomes to have a command function that oversees authority design, audit logs, knowledge updates, and exception handling.

Third, **the skills valued in the hiring market will change**. Rather than prompt-writing skill alone, organizations will place greater value on the ability to decompose work, formalize decision criteria, design escalation paths, and connect operations to KPIs. This will likely happen with fairly high probability, around 75%. The reason is that agents can appear to function plausibly even when instructions are ambiguous, but in organizational operations, “plausible” is not good enough. People who can build reproducible operations will become scarce and valuable.

The practical implications for readers are also clear. First, do not stop at comparing AI tools; instead, **inventory your own operations as a flow of “goal → judgment → execution → verification.”** Second, rather than letting PoCs proliferate department by department, set use cases that span at least development, planning, and operations. Third, replace success metrics based solely on “time saved” with measures such as “number of handoffs,” “rework rate,” and “lead time from decision to implementation.”

These facts suggest that the real winners in the age of AI agents will not be “the companies that use the most AI,” but **the companies that orchestrate themselves best**. AI is not a substitute for people. It is a new execution OS for redesigning the organization itself.
