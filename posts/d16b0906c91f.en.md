## Executive Summary

David Haber of a16z argues that AI agents have moved past bounded tasks and can now own customer journeys that run for weeks and months. By owning the whole journey, a vendor can be paid for the revenue expansion it creates, not only for cost savings. Red Ventures, which owned an end-to-end sales journey and was paid per closed policy, is the precedent; Haber argues its model is now structurally threatened and sketches an AI-native successor.

## Value Only Appears at the End of the Relay Race

The traditional support call meant phone menus, hold time, explaining the issue to one representative and then again to another, and finally a technician appointment. AI agents can now see the account, check whether other customers in the area are affected, and either resolve simple problems or book a technician. Haber says that "until recently, this was the technological limit." What has changed is that agents can "follow threads and execute tasks over longer horizons."

His worked example is plaintiff personal-injury case preparation. Intake specialists gather facts from phone leads and an attorney decides whether to take the case. After signing, paralegals order police reports, try to reach witnesses, send spoliation letters to defendants to preserve evidence, and update the client. Firms wait for treatment to conclude before drafting a medical chronology and handing information to attorneys to finalize the demand letter.

Haber calls this a "relay race", with a risk of drop-off or value leakage at every handoff:

- Prospective clients call several firms and often sign with whichever gets back first; voicemail or a slow qualification decision loses the case.
- After signing, evidence collection runs many parallel threads, and firms may lack the persistence to call a witness a fifth time after four unanswered attempts.
- Labor constraints keep firms from reviewing every piece of evidence in detail on seemingly routine, low-value claims. A firm may accept the first offer when a detailed read of the doctor's note would have surfaced indicators of a brain injury.
- Cases run for months with information spread across documents, stakeholders and systems, so the full picture is nearly impossible to maintain. Important bills may be missing from the demand letter or treatment gaps unexplained, driving lower settlement value.
## The Long-Running Agent Opportunity

Until recently agents mostly handled bounded tasks ("Send this follow-up email", "Summarize that call"). Customer support was a great early use case because the problem space is well defined, interactions are brief, outcomes are verifiable, and existing support teams remain in place for escalations.

Long-running agents pursue goals across dozens of steps, modalities and longer horizons. In the plaintiff-law example, an agent is assigned from the initial intake call with the goal of maximizing the client's recovery. It saves and retrieves case context, wakes on events or deadlines, dispatches sub-agents to contact witnesses, updates the client, and interacts with attorneys by phone, email or text. It consolidates findings, plans next moves and drafts documents for review. Each case generates data that helps it handle the next one better.

That closes the leaks. The agent is available at any hour and can assess cases nearly instantaneously. It can spin up a sub-agent to place the fifth, tenth or twentieth call. Even routine-looking cases get worked fully and each piece of evidence is reviewed in detail, surfacing the potential brain injury that might otherwise be missed. And it keeps a complete record, from witness call transcripts to police reports and medical records.

## Getting Paid for Outcomes

Owning the journey also changes the vendor's economics. Because impact is observable and attributable to agents, vendors can participate in the upside they create by pricing on work products or outcomes. In plaintiff law a single case is a discrete unit of work and the settlement amount is a clearly identifiable outcome. Vendors may then share in topline expansion, reaching contract values previously unattainable by focusing solely on cost reduction.

Haber admits that "switching to outcome-based pricing is not frictionless":

- Most states prohibit non-lawyers from sharing in legal fees, so pricing would need to align with settlement values without taking a direct share.
- Procurement teams without precedents for outcome-based contracts may object.
- Attribution debates will likely surface at first, even as agents move closer to fully owning outcomes.
Deploying long-running agents is therefore "a commercial challenge as much as a technical one."

## The Precedent: Red Ventures

"Getting paid for outcomes on high-value customer journeys is not a new idea," Haber writes. Red Ventures (RV), founded by Ric Elias and Dan Feldstein, pioneered digital end-to-end outsourced sales operations for long-term consumer contracts. For insurance, it owned sites like homeinsurance.com to collect details on searching customers, reached out, walked them through coverage options and closed the policy, collecting a payment from the insurer for each policy closed. According to S&P Global, RV generated about $370M in EBITDA on about $1.4B in sales in 2022.

Haber sees that model as now structurally under threat. At the top of the funnel, consumers can ask a personal agent such as Meta's Muse or Instinct for recommendations directly instead of relying on Google search or comparison sites, lowering the value of owning exploration sites. Downstream, instead of hundreds of humans running linear funnels, long-running agents can work across far more complex journeys, across products and communication modalities, in parallel, in any language, with infinite persistence. RV's EBITDA decline to an estimated ~$160M in 2025, driven by customer churn and softer ad spend, is offered as an early indication of the model's vulnerability.

## Three Ways to Market

1. **Selling the platform.** Sell the agent as software supporting existing operations. With few comparable contracts anchoring prices there is revenue upside, but new budget lines lengthen sales cycles and outcomes are harder to attribute to an agent that touches several departments. 2. **Selling the service.** Take over an already outsourced service and deliver the outcome more effectively, as Red Ventures did. ROI math is simple and BPO budget lines exist, so cycles are shorter; but pre-AI pricing may not reflect the agent's true value, and many high-value journeys are run in-house today, limiting reach. 3. **Going full-stack.** Vertically integrate and become the AI-native player. Instead of selling an agent to a debt-collection firm, buy debt portfolios and collect them yourself, capturing all the upside and solving attribution, at the cost of owning licensing, compliance and other work outside the journey.

Haber says it is too early to tell which will prevail, but he is "convinced that long-running agents will win the relay race at scale and expand the market addressable by AI from a share of labor budgets to a share of revenue." He expects the same pattern in mortgage origination, insurance claims processing, complex enterprise sales and other high-value journeys.

## Assessment

**Evidence (the source's claims).** Long-running agents can own whole journeys and be paid on outcomes. RV's 2022 EBITDA and its estimated 2025 figure are offered as the contrast. There are three routes to market.

**Implication (our reading).** The thesis is also a claim that the billing unit moves from seats or usage to completed outcomes. Pricing on outcomes needs the agent's actions recorded across the whole journey and auditable enough to settle attribution disputes. Commenters on the post raise similar points, such as a full call log proving the work outcome pricing will need and agent state having to outlive the conversation, the model version and the vendor, but those are reader comments, not the author's argument. The article's figures, including RV's estimated 2025 EBITDA, are secondary numbers the author cites and the article does not verify them.
