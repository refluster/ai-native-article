Elon Musk’s five-step process repeated on the factory floor, the observation that AI is rewriting the software development lifecycle itself, and McKinsey’s argument for moving “from scattered pilots to an assembly line” as a corporate strategy. The speakers, subjects, and scales are all different. But in fact, all of them can be explained by the same underlying principle: **as AI and automation drive the cost of “execution” asymptotically toward zero, the source of value moves from downstream execution to upstream selection**. What got cheaper was “building fast”; what became scarce was “deciding not to build.”

## Why Musk Bet on “Order,” Not “Steps”

The key point made in L2 “Elon Musk’s ‘Algorithm’” lies not in the content of the five steps, but in their **order**. Most people start with optimization, acceleration, or automation. Musk put “question every requirement” and “delete” ahead of them. The reason is distilled in Musk’s own words: “The most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist.”

What makes this line so sharp is that it exposes how optimization, by its nature, silently affirms the premise. The moment you try to make a process faster, you have already accepted the judgment that the process ought to exist. But what is truly effective is rewinding that judgment itself—deletion. Deletion erases the entire downstream cost. Optimization trims downstream cost by some tens of percent. The leverage is on a completely different order of magnitude. That is why Musk fixed “question and delete” at the front of the sequence. The moment you reverse that order, you fall into the trap of carefully polishing something that should never have existed in the first place.

## What McKinsey’s “Assembly Line” Rejected

L2 “The CEO challenge of turning AI adoption into an ‘assembly line’” argues that the reason companies fail to extract productivity from AI is the unchecked proliferation of scattered pilots. Its prescription is to prioritize high-value use cases, build the right operating model, align talent, governance, and technology, and then scale across the company—in other words, to turn the process into an assembly line.

What is being rejected here is the most automation-adjacent instinct of all: “let each department just try some AI for now.” Scattered pilots are equivalent to starting the line before deciding what should run down it. The assembly-line model demands the opposite: decide first what goes on the line and what does not. “Prioritizing high-value use cases” is, turned inside out, nothing other than deleting low-value use cases. What Musk said at the desk of a single engineer—“delete before you optimize”—McKinsey is simply restating at enterprise scale in the language of the operating model. The scale differs, but the principle is the same: before you switch on the amplification machine, decide what is worth amplifying.

## Redesigning the SDLC Is Not About Speed; It Is About “Deleting Steps”

What L2 “shin_sasaki19’s AI and software development” captures is the shift in which AI is reconfiguring the conventional software development lifecycle (SDLC) and changing the role of the engineer itself. What matters is that this is not a story about “speeding up the old SDLC with AI.”

If AI can write code, then the bottleneck moves elsewhere. What remains are the specification of what should be built, the reviews that are actually necessary, and the deletion of unnecessary work. In other words, the essence of AI-assisted development is not acceleration but a re-examination of which steps should exist at all. This is simply Musk’s “algorithm” applied to the SDLC. Before making each step—writing code, testing, deploying—faster, you ask whether that step should still exist in the first place. Precisely because AI has made execution cheaper, the work that remains on the process chart simply by inertia becomes relatively more expensive. Redesigning the SDLC is another name for removing that inertia.

## The Shared Principle: As Execution Gets Cheaper, Scarcity Moves Upstream

Put these three facts side by side, and beneath their surface differences the same structure appears. Musk says at the desk, “put deletion before optimization.” McKinsey says at the level of the enterprise operating model, “put selection before rollout.” Observers of the SDLC say in development, “put re-examination before acceleration.” **Taken together, these facts suggest that when automation and AI push the marginal cost of execution toward zero, the organization’s binding constraint shifts from downstream execution to upstream judgment.**

The logic is straightforward. As long as execution is expensive, the scarce capability—and therefore the source of value—is the ability to build correctly and quickly. But when execution gets cheap, the dominant risk becomes building the wrong thing quickly. Amplification machines do not choose their content. If you automate a process that should not exist, all you do is mass-produce waste at high speed. Therefore, the lower the cost of execution falls, the scarcer upstream judgment becomes—the judgment that determines what not to execute—and value concentrates there. Musk put “deletion” first because he was, in effect, already thinking from the premise that execution—manufacturing, coding—would become cheap.

So what? The scarce capability is no longer “being able to build” or “being able to optimize.” It has shifted to “being able to decide not to build.” In the AI era, what gets protected is not the fast player, but the player who can delete before amplifying.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, several changes are highly likely.

First, roles will polarize. Execution—writing code, making slides, running pilots—will rapidly commoditize, while compensation will concentrate around upstream selection: the judgment that decides what not to build and which steps to eliminate. The fact that McKinsey emphasizes the operating model, and that SDLC observers are capturing a transformation in roles, is evidence that this polarization is not confined to any one company but is unfolding across the board. The probability is high, because falling execution costs are directly tied to AI’s performance curve and are unlikely to reverse.

Second, the symptom “we adopted AI but productivity did not improve” will persist at a meaningful rate. The cause is not the technology but the sequence. Organizations that automate existing processes without first passing them through the deletion path will end up merely accelerating waste. This aligns with McKinsey’s warning about the uncontrolled spread of scattered pilots.

Third, this prediction does have a falsifiable edge. If execution becomes cheap and accurate enough, then even the cost of “building the wrong thing quickly” may become so small as to be negligible, and there may be domains where brute-force trial and error becomes more rational than deletion. If this principle is ever broken, it will be at that boundary.

For the reader, the practical implication can be reduced to one thing. **The moment you decide to automate or accelerate something, first run it through the “deletion path” once.** Should this process exist at all? Is this use case truly high-value? Is this step still necessary after AI? Question it, cut it back, and only then amplify what remains. For the same reason Musk fixed the order, deletion must always precede optimization. Before you spin up cheap execution, put scarce judgment to work first. That is how you protect the one thing that does not scale in the age of amplification.
