## Executive Summary

Even as per-token prices fall, more aggressive AI adoption and the spread of autonomous agents are driving token consumption higher, pushing enterprise AI spending past budget limits. Uber had already exhausted its entire 2026 AI coding budget by April, and one company reportedly ended up with a $500 million Claude bill after forgetting to set employee usage caps. Against this backdrop, the Linux Foundation has announced the “Tokenomics Foundation,” a standards body aimed at bringing the same kind of cost discipline to AI tokens that FinOps brought to cloud spending, and a market for tools and vendors to visualize and optimize spending is rapidly taking shape.

## Spending That Blows Through Budgets — A Shift From “Optimism” to “Guardrails”

The article opens with a series of concrete examples showing that companies across the industry are beginning to recoil at the price of AI. Uber had already spent its entire 2026 AI coding budget by April. Microsoft revoked Claude Code licenses it had enabled for its own developers just a few months later. Employees at Priceline told TechCrunch that standard renewals for their Cursor contracts came back at 4 to 5 times the previous price.

The shift is visible in the tone of the conversations themselves. Alexander Embiricos, OpenAI’s head of enterprise, said at an event in New York City: “6 months ago, every conversation with customers was ‘What can this do, is it good enough.’ There’s none of that now. The conversation now is, ‘We’re spending this much. What visibility is there, what auditability, what token controls are there, how efficient is the model?’”

J.R. Storment, executive director of the FinOps Foundation, offered similar testimony. “In April and May, we started hearing from companies, ‘Oh my god, we’re already more than 3x over our entire 2026 token budget, and it’s only April.’ We started hearing existential-threat conversations, and the whole discussion flipped from tokenmaxxing and ‘move fast’ to ‘we need guardrails, how do we get this under control.’”

## Why Consumption Exploded — Agent Capabilities and Murky Productivity Gains

The trigger for the surge in spending was that CEOs pushed teams to use the best models and move quickly regardless of cost. New models released in November — including Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.5, OpenAI’s GPT-5.1, and Google’s Gemini 3 Pro — significantly improved agentic tools and doubled consumption. One company reportedly ended up facing a $500 million Claude bill after forgetting to set employee usage caps.

Chris Reed, Priceline’s senior director of IT finance, said the company has begun imposing token caps on some groups, adding, “It’s like the spread of crack cocaine. You let people try it, they get addicted, and now you’re tied to it.” Vitaly Gordon, CEO of Faros AI, said one CTO confessed to him: “One of our engineers spent $40,000 on tokens last month. I genuinely don’t know whether I should stop him, or tell everyone else to be more like him.”

The productivity returns are not as clear-cut as the spending suggests. In a two-year study of 20,000 developers published by Faros in April, output increased, but so did bugs and rewrites. Engineering management platform Jellyfish similarly found that the engineers using the most tokens were about twice as productive as engineers with lower AI usage, but they spent 10 times as many tokens to get there. Nicholas Arcolano, Jellyfish’s head of research, said by email that per-developer consumption had risen by about 18.6x over nine months, and that most of the spending explosion was driven by agent capabilities. “Whether extreme spend pays off depends on the ultimate business value of the shipped code, like revenue, but most companies can’t measure that yet,” Arcolano said.

## The New Challenge of Tracking, and a Market Taking Shape

Part of the measurement problem comes from the sheer scale at which AI is being used today. Storment put the difference this way: “Tracking cloud costs is a hundreds-of-millions-of-rows-per-month data problem. Tracking token costs is a trillions-of-rows-per-month data problem. You can’t throw that into a spreadsheet or basic tooling. You have to fundamentally rethink the tools, the specifications, and the accounting systems.” Priceline’s Reed said he is already seeing discrepancies between vendor-reported usage and internal data: “I started my career in telecom expense management, and from telecom to cloud to AI, I see the same pattern. Every time you introduce something new, it creates billing errors and opportunities for audit and optimization.”

A market is beginning to form around the problem. There are pure-play companies such as Pay-i, which tracks, measures, and optimizes the cost and performance of GenAI investments, and Paid, which makes it possible to charge based on actual value rather than subscription fees. Jellyfish, Waydev, and Faros AI offer AI agent monitoring and aim to prove the ROI of developer tools. Companies with existing distribution channels are also adding new features: Ramp has moved into AI spend management, while Datadog and New Relic have added services such as cloud cost management, token-level observability, and GPU monitoring. At next week’s FinOps X conference, AWS is expected to announce new financial management features for enterprise AI spending.

Tiffany Luck, a partner at NEA, believes token efficiency and observability are most likely to be built into the “harness or application layer,” pointing to Factory, an enterprise AI agent startup that launched a model router this week to automatically choose the right model for each task. Gordon expects frontier labs and model providers to adopt OpenRouter-style optimization that routes queries to the cheapest model. “The financial report of how much you spent on Anthropic — even if you called the Opus model, some of that spend will actually be Sonnet or Haiku. They’re smart enough to do that.”

## The Absence of a Common Language and the Tokenomics Foundation

All of these tools are being built without a common language or shared definitions for token cost, what tokens produce, or how spending should be compared across vendors. That is where the Tokenomics Foundation is trying to prove its usefulness. The group is developing a standard definition and framework for “tokenomics,” open standards, specifications, and metrics for AI token usage and billing, and new AI economy metrics such as cost-per-intelligence and tokens-per-watt. It also plans to define metrics that cut across the effectiveness and consumption efficiency of token factories. The official launch is scheduled for July, and the group plans to announce additional members at next week’s FinOps X conference.

In a statement, Salesforce chief availability officer Nishant Gupta said: “The token economy is fundamentally more abstract and opaque than anything we’ve managed at this scale. It requires a different operational muscle than what the industry built for cloud.” Even so, Goldman Sachs forecasts that global token usage will increase 24x by 2030. Companies already over budget need solutions now, but the group’s first deliverables are still months away. “We may have built the steam engine, but we haven’t found the assembly line yet,” Gordon said. According to Arcolano, the smart move is broad, moderate adoption: “The best ROI comes not from pushing heavy users even harder, but from moving the broad middle from low usage to moderate usage.”
