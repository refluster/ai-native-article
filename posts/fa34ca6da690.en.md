In the age of AI, value no longer resides in model performance itself. It has shifted to the "design of implementation"—how those models are embedded into enterprise operations. And that shift is now becoming visible simultaneously from multiple independent vantage points.

At first glance, there are three unrelated facts. Alex Karp of Palantir criticized frontier AI labs, saying they "don’t understand how much they are hated" ("Frontier AI Labs Are Hated"). McKinsey argued that AI adoption should not be handled through sporadic pilots, but turned into an "assembly line" ("The CEO Challenge of Turning AI Adoption into an Assembly Line"). And Palantir’s "blueprint of a company’s operations" was presented as evidence of a shift in the competitive axis in the AI era ("What Palantir’s Blueprint of a Company’s Operations Shows About the Shift in the Competitive Axis"). Critique, management prescription, product philosophy—the language differs, but in fact all three can be explained by the same principle.

## Dissatisfaction with the Labs Points to the "Absence of Implementation"

The core of Karp’s criticism is not emotional rhetoric, but a structural diagnosis. According to "Frontier AI Labs Are Hated," what he was really pointing to was a fault line: while the labs are focused on competing over model performance, they are neglecting the layer of "implementation" where enterprises actually derive value.

That fault line shows up in the numbers. Citing Gartner estimates, the article notes that only 28% of AI use cases fully met ROI expectations. The remaining more than 70% did not fail because the models were weak. They failed because they were never designed into business workflows in a way that could convert capability into value.

The decisive point is that the labs themselves are beginning to acknowledge this. "Frontier AI Labs Are Hated" points to OpenAI’s acquisition of the consulting firm Tomoro and its formation of an implementation support team. Those who build the models went out of their way to buy those who can carry implementation into organizations. That is effectively an admission in action, from the actors closest to the models themselves, of where the center of value now lies.

## An "Assembly Line" Means Productizing Implementation

McKinsey’s prescription illuminates the same point from the other side of that fault line. "The CEO Challenge of Turning AI Adoption into an Assembly Line" argues that companies only achieve major productivity gains when they industrialize AI adoption as an "assembly line," rather than relying on scattered pilots.

What is being demanded here is not a better model. The strategic actions the article calls on CEOs to take are prioritizing high-value use cases, building the right operating model, and aligning talent, governance, and technology. All of these are questions not of "what to use," but of "how to integrate it."

The reason pilots fail to turn into company-wide deployment is the same. A pilot is one-off handwork; an assembly line is a repeatable design. When McKinsey says companies must move "from scattered pilots to enterprise-wide deployment," it is pointing to a shift from the stage of "testing models" to the stage of "designing work." The 28% wall Karp lamented is nothing other than the absence of that design.

## The "Blueprint of Work" Becomes the Competitive Axis

The third observation confirms this shift from the product side. "What Palantir’s Blueprint of a Company’s Operations Shows About the Shift in the Competitive Axis" argues that AI is reshaping work, roles, and the way products are developed, while blurring the boundaries of responsibility between teams (role blurring). In that argument, what is explicitly named as the object of competition is not the model, but the "blueprint of a company’s operations" itself.

Calling the blueprint the competitive axis carries major implications. Models can be bought, but the blueprint for how a company’s work actually flows cannot simply be purchased from outside. It becomes an asset specific to each firm, and one that is difficult to imitate. Here again, the issue moves down from relative differences in performance to the question of "how work is designed."

The "blurring of responsibility boundaries (role blurring)" the article highlights explains from the other side why that blueprint is so hard to copy. Once AI enters the picture, the seams in work that were previously divided by people and roles get redrawn. Who is responsible for what, and where humans and AI hand work off to one another—the way those lines are drawn is the substance of the blueprint itself, and it reflects each organization’s own history and constraints. That is why other companies may be able to buy the same model, but they cannot draw the same blueprint. While differences in model performance can be erased in a matter of months, differences in operational design endure because they are rooted in organizational structure. The shift in competitive advantage from the former to the latter is therefore a natural consequence of that difference in durability.

## The Shared Principle: The Bottleneck Has Shifted from Models to Design

Set these three facts side by side, and a single principle emerges. What now governs the speed of AI value creation is no longer model performance, but the "design of implementation" that embeds the model into work.

This inference is strengthened by the fact that the three parties are independent of one another. The critic (Karp) attacks from the outside, saying the labs neglect implementation. The consultant (McKinsey) prescribes from within, saying implementation must be industrialized. And the product-side argument (Palantir’s blueprint) points ahead, saying the blueprint itself is the axis of competition. Different positions, different intentions—yet all three point to the same layer: the implementation design layer that sits between the model and the business workflow. Taken together, these facts suggest that the center of value and competitive advantage has shifted away from the model layer, which is becoming commoditized, toward the harder-to-copy layer of implementation design.

Why is that happening? Once model performance exceeds a certain threshold, and multiple labs are roughly at parity, the model stops being a differentiator. In a world where everyone can access strong models, the deciding factor becomes only this: how did you reorganize your own operations around that model? The 28% ROI achievement rate is not evidence that the technology is still insufficient; it is evidence that the design is still insufficient. That is why OpenAI bought Tomoro, why McKinsey is preaching the assembly line, and why Palantir calls the blueprint the competitive axis.

## Forecast and Implications: Those Who Control the Blueprint Will Gain the MOAT

If this principle is correct, the structural changes of the next few years can be predicted with fairly high confidence.

First, value capture by frontier labs will continue to be compressed. As models commoditize, profits will flow to the implementation layer—deployment support, operational design, and operating models. OpenAI’s acquisition of Tomoro can be read as an early sign that the labs are not resisting this flow, but moving downstream to capture the implementation layer themselves. The probability is high, because the actors are already moving.

Second, the core question behind enterprise investment decisions will change. "Which model should we adopt?" will quickly become a stale question, replaced by "Which workflows should we turn into an assembly line, in what order, and how?" McKinsey’s "operating model" and Palantir’s "blueprint of operations" are pointing to the same thing here. The sooner a company draws a blueprint that is hard to imitate, the wider the gap it can open over latecomers.

The practical lesson is simple. The next time you are tempted to ask, "Should we switch to the latest model?" ask instead: "What blueprint of work have we still failed to design with the model already in our hands?" Most of the failures in that 70% outside the 28% are not the result of choosing the wrong model, but of the absence of design.

Finally, this principle should be stated in a falsifiable form. If the next leap in model performance were to drive ROI sharply higher on its own, without changing implementation design—if simply swapping in a better model turned that 70% of failures into successes—then the claim of this essay, that "value has shifted to the implementation layer," would be wrong. But the fact that three independent observations all point to the same layer suggests that such a refutation is unlikely in the near term. The competitive axis has already moved down from models to the implementation layer. The real question now is no longer which model, but who controls the blueprint of work.
