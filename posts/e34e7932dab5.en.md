Whether AI can actually replace a piece of work is decided not by how capable the model is, but by who owns the full loop — from spotting the problem to verifying the outcome. Meta quietly built a plan to replace thousands of employees with AI. More than a hundred tech companies asked governments to surge cyber-defense against AI-driven attacks. And the labor market is minting "FDE" job titles with no agreed definition. These look like three unrelated stories. They are the same story.

## Where "capability implies substitution" broke down

Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg's confidential "Project OT" was an ambitious plan to replace thousands of employees' work with AI and shrink many internal teams by as much as 60% (source: "メタ『プロジェクトOT』はなぜ頓挫したのか——AIによる大量人員代替計画の内幕" / "Why Meta's 'Project OT' Collapsed"). The premise was simple: once AI's capability reached a human performance bar, headcount could be swapped out directly. What actually happened was internal backlash after the plan leaked, AI productivity that fell short of expectations, and a surge in security and reliability incidents. Employee satisfaction dropped from 74% to 55%, and Zuckerberg himself admitted at a July internal meeting that the reorganization "isn't working."

What broke here was not "AI's capability." What broke was the loop that human staff used to own — finding problems, designing solutions, implementing them, shipping to production, verifying the results, and correcting course — being shrunk before anyone inherited responsibility for the whole of it. AI can execute pieces of that loop, implementation and production among them, but when no one owns the loop end-to-end, the fracture surfaces exactly where verification and trust are supposed to live.

## A call for "surge defense" while the hands that run the loop shrink

OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Amazon, together with Broadcom, Capital One, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, General Motors, IBM, Mastercard, Oracle, Robinhood, Shopify, Visa, and more than a hundred companies in total, signed a joint letter calling on governments and industry for a society-wide "surge in defense" against AI-driven cyberattacks (source: "OpenAIやAnthropicら100社超、AI駆動サイバー攻撃への『防御の急増』を政府に要求" / the 100+-company cyber-defense letter). The letter asks governments to accelerate a "trusted access" program that would give them early access to powerful models.

Meanwhile, CISA — the U.S. agency actually charged with responding to those threats — has had roughly a third of its staff cut over the past year. What we're looking at is an asymmetric move: expanding the entry point (early access to powerful models) while the hands that actually run the loop — detection, response, remediation — keep thinning. This is structurally identical to Project OT rushing the entry point (AI adoption) while starving the exit point (verification and correction). A strong statement of intent, or a request for expanded access, is not a substitute for the executional capacity to own and run a loop to completion.

## A title proves nothing; owning the loop does

The same structure shows up a third time, in a different guise, in the labor market. Job postings for "FDE" (Forward Deployed Engineer) are proliferating even though FDE is neither a licensed profession nor a role with an industry-wide, rigorous definition — so a company can slap the label on ordinary contract development or deployment-support work, and a job posting alone can't stop it (source: "エセFDE求人の見抜き方——OpenAIとPalantirの定義に学ぶ『仕事のループ』という判定軸" / the guide to spotting fake FDE postings). Product-development analyst Shin (@shin_sasaki19) argues that the diagnostic isn't whether the role is on-site with the client, but whether the person actually owns the loop: problem discovery → design → implementation → production deployment → adoption and outcome measurement → generalization → product improvement.

That diagnostic names exactly where the first two cases broke. A title, an access request, or an expanded mandate can be satisfied by occupying just one part of the loop — usually the entry: claiming the role, requesting the access. What separates the real thing from the fake is whether the exit — outcome measurement, generalization, improvement — is also closed by the same hand. As Shin puts it, get this one axis wrong and you miss the real FDEs along with the fake ones; the label itself stops meaning anything.

## The unifying principle: a label or a statement of intent is not a substitute for owning the loop

On the surface these are three different domains — mass layoffs, cyber defense, job-title inflation. What they share is that in each case, the actor trying to substitute or extend something in the AI era conflates a change at the **entry point** — acquiring capability, attaching a label, requesting authority — with **owning the entire loop** that closes from problem discovery to outcome verification.

Project OT used AI's implementation capability as the justification for headcount cuts, but because the humans who ran the verification-and-correction loop were reduced first, security and reliability broke down. The 100-company letter calls for stronger defense while the staff who actually run the threat-response loop, at CISA, keep shrinking — a statement of intent does not backfill a shrinking capacity to execute. The flood of FDE postings shows that a title at the entry point guarantees nothing about whether the exit — measurement and generalization — is ever actually closed.

Taken together, these facts suggest that what actually determines whether an AI-era substitution or expansion works is not model performance, institutional authority, or a job title — apparent credentials — but a single question: does one person, or one organization, own the entire chain from problem discovery through outcome verification and improvement, start to finish? An organization or institution that reinforces only the entry to a loop while scaling up without an owner for the exit will, sooner or later, have that gap exposed. Conversely, what AI can take on is the execution speed of part of a loop — it cannot take on the responsibility structure of owning the loop itself. That is the principle running through all three cases.

## Forecast and what it means for you

If this principle holds, the following should be observable over the next six to twelve months with reasonably high confidence (roughly 60–70%). First, Project-OT-style "mass AI substitution" plans are likely to hit the same kind of collapse at other major tech companies beyond Meta — simply because the pace of redesigning the verification-and-correction loop is lagging the pace of AI capability gains at most organizations. Expect companies to shift their language, after each failure surfaces, from "substitution" toward "loop redesign."

Second, in AI cybersecurity, a request for "early access" alone won't be effective; unless governments follow through with the staffing and budget to rebuild actual response capacity, the gap against AI-driven attacks is more likely than not (50–60%) to widen over the next year. The failure mode that surfaces next will likely be a post-mortem case of "we had the access, but not the people to respond."

Third, title inflation in the labor market won't stay confined to FDE — expect it to spread to other emerging roles like "AI engineer" or "prompt engineer" with similarly loose definitions. Shin's loop-ownership diagnostic is general enough to travel with it, and is likely to be gradually adopted as a generic checklist across hiring markets for any role whose job description is still fuzzy.

The practical takeaway is straightforward. When you're evaluating an AI rollout, a new job title, or a bold statement of intent, don't ask what it's trying to substitute or strengthen — ask who owns the entire loop, start to finish, from discovering the problem to verifying and improving the outcome. Whether you're the organization adopting AI or the candidate applying for an AI-adjacent role, chasing scale or speed without answering that question first is how you end up exactly where Project OT did.
