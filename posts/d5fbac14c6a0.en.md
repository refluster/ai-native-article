## Introduction

In software development, the question is beginning to shift from “reviewability” to “delegatability.” In AI startups, low-gross-margin service delivery is being re-evaluated as a strategic asset. And in Claude Managed Agents, features such as dreaming, outcomes, multi-agent orchestration, and webhooks are laying the groundwork for handing off multi-stage work to AI. These developments may look unrelated at first glance, but in fact they can be explained by the same principle. The axis of competition in the AI era is moving from **generative capability** to the **ability to design the operational responsibility structures that make delegation possible**.

## Analysis 1: The Value of Development Is Moving from “The Ability to Write” to “The Ability to Design What Can Be Entrusted”

What the debate over moving from “reviewability” to “delegatability” shows is that, the more AI takes on code generation and implementation support, the more human value moves upstream. The article title itself is symbolic: where traditional quality assurance depended on whether “a human can ultimately read and verify it,” the center of gravity in the age of superintelligent AI shifts toward “how to design the conditions under which it is safe to entrust the work, even if a human does not read every detail.”

What matters here is not simply that implementation effort declines. What gets re-evaluated instead are areas such as design, requirements definition, explicit articulation of constraints, escalation paths when failure occurs, and ensuring auditability. In other words, the center of engineering is moving from the production of artifacts to the design of governance.

These facts suggest that AI adoption is beginning to break the assumption that “humans will look at everything.” If every output must still be reviewed one by one by a human, then even if AI speeds up the process, the final bottleneck remains human. That is why what is needed is a design principle that keeps incident rates within an acceptable range even when reviews are reduced.

This shift also blurs the boundaries between professional roles. The traditional division of labor among requirements definers, designers, implementers, and QA is being reconfigured through the insertion of AI agents. For example, the work of resolving ambiguities in specifications, translating them into testable acceptance criteria, and defining them as prompts and constraints to hand off to AI cuts across the conventional boundaries of PdM, Tech Lead, and QA. The “redefinition of the human role” that such an article would discuss means precisely this evolution toward becoming a designer of delegation conditions.

## Analysis 2: Services-Led Growth Is Not “Low Gross Margin” but “A High-Resolution Device for Capturing Operational Data”

The argument that in “AI enterprise, you should capture the ‘point of data inflow’ rather than optimize for ‘gross margin’” differs from the traditional SaaS preference for high gross margins. The article summary states that **“startups can use services as a strategic wedge to accelerate product adoption, deepen customer relationships, and uncover repeatable product opportunities.”** The core point here is that services are not just a source of revenue; they are an exploratory mechanism for discovering repeatable product opportunities.

A particularly symbolic role here is the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE). An FDE is not simply a contract implementer. The role is to go deep into the customer’s workflow and learn, on the ground, how far work can be delegated to AI and where human oversight is still required. In other words, the FDE is a junction point connecting sales, implementation, product, operations, and risk management, and is the actor best positioned to recover the highest-value data from customer touchpoints.

Why prioritize the “point of data inflow” over “gross margin”? Because while the differentiation of AI products is hard to sustain through UI or one-off model performance alone, operational data—such as exception handling in customer workflows, approval conditions, failure patterns, and frictions in cross-department handoffs—is difficult to imitate. What accumulates through services is not just usage logs, but knowledge about the “boundary conditions of delegation.”

These facts suggest that the competitive advantage of AI companies is shifting away from high-gross-margin software sales and toward **owning a field-learning loop that expands delegatability**. Put differently, services are not a low-margin temporary measure; they are a mechanism for collecting the training data that will raise future automation rates.

## Analysis 3: The Evolution of Managed Agents Is Not “Convenient Automation” but the Buildout of “Delegation Infrastructure”

The Claude Managed Agents updates—**dreaming, outcomes, multi-agent orchestration, webhooks**—may look like a list of feature additions, but in reality they can be read as an effort to assemble a full set of requirements for delegation infrastructure. As Anthropic explains, managed agents are a mechanism that allows users to **“delegate multi-step tasks to AI agents that can plan, use tools, and complete work with less manual orchestration.”**

First, dreaming gives the agent room to plan and explore before execution. This is not simple response generation but “pre-processing for work” involving task decomposition and intermediate reasoning, and it is essential for delegating complex business tasks. Next, outcomes is close to the idea of controlling through expected results rather than through prompts. Instead of specifying every step manually, the human defines the success conditions and gives the agent discretion within that scope. Multi-agent orchestration absorbs the instability of a single agent by splitting different roles—such as research, execution, and verification—across multiple agents. Webhooks are the operational blood vessels that connect the results to external systems and human approval flows.

What emerges here are the four conditions required for AI to move from “usable” to “entrustable”: planning capability, outcome criteria, division of roles, and external connectivity.

These facts suggest that the real battleground in the agent market lies not only in model intelligence, but in **post-delegation controllability, auditability, and organizational connectivity**. More than the benchmark performance of a standalone model, what creates value is whether part of a business process can be safely entrusted to it.

## Presenting the Common Principle

The most likely deep principle that explains all of the observations above in a unified way is this hypothesis: **in the AI era, the unit of value creation is shifting from “generated outputs” to “business units that can be delegated with responsibility attached.”**

From this perspective, the arguments in the three articles connect into a single thread. In development theory, delegatability matters more than reviewability because quality is increasingly determined not by the contents of the output itself, but by “who entrusted what, under what conditions.” In Services-Led Growth, the reason the FDE becomes central is that what the product is really selling is not software functionality, but the operational capability to safely take over part of the customer’s work. And the reason Managed Agents development is concentrated on planning, outcomes, division of labor, and connectivity is that what users want is not a conversation partner, but “a unit of labor they can entrust.”

In “Why” terms, once model performance rises above a certain threshold, what differentiates systems is no longer intelligence itself, but the incident rate, exception-handling rate, supervision cost, and boundary lines of accountability that appear when that intelligence is embedded in business operations. AI is not adopted simply because it is smart. It is only implemented once a human organization has defined whose responsibility it is, how far it may be used, and under what procedures.

In “So What” terms, the competitive strength of future AI companies and AI-adopting organizations will be determined not by their ability to choose models, but by their **ability to institutionalize delegation through design**. Concretely, the contest will be about whether they can integrate elements such as acceptance criteria, escalation conditions, audit logs, human-in-the-loop processes, learning loops from customer sites, and role design for multi-agent systems.

In other words, operational responsibility for AI is not defensive control. It is an offensive organizational capability that makes it possible to entrust more work, at lower supervision cost, and with higher reliability.

## Future Predictions and Implications

At least four future changes can be derived from this principle.

First, there is a high likelihood that **the leading role in AI adoption will shift from the “person responsible for model selection” to the “person responsible for delegation design.”** The probability is high—over 70%. The reason is that in a market where multiple high-performance models coexist, most of the differentiation moves into operational design. Inside companies, we are likely to see growth in new job categories that combine AI PM, AI Ops, AI QA, and FDE-like functions.

Second, **the boundary between services and products will erode even further**. This too is highly likely. More companies will run things manually at first, then productize only the repeatedly occurring parts. If gross margin alone is treated as the KPI, this movement will be misunderstood. In reality, the key metrics will be “how much high-value operational data was recovered” and “how far the scope of delegation was expanded.” Implementation support departments will likely be redefined not as cost centers, but as learning engines.

Third, **the evaluation metric for agents will move from answer accuracy to task completion rate in real business workflows**. The probability of this is extremely high. Going forward, instead of “how many questions did it answer correctly,” organizations will adopt practical KPIs such as “how many cases could it handle without approval,” “how appropriately could it return exceptions to humans,” and “how many minutes were saved by connecting through webhooks to business systems.” In this context, the importance of outcomes and multi-agent orchestration will increase even further.

Fourth, **governance will become not a brake on adoption, but a device for expanding delegation**. This is medium-probability but important. Governance to date has focused mainly on restrictions on use, but going forward it will shift toward increasing the number of cases where “under these conditions, it is safe to entrust the work.” Companies that build catalogs of delegatable work, rather than just prohibition lists, will learn faster and automate faster.

The practical implications are clear. If you are on the AI adoption side, you should change your PoC question from “What can it do?” to “What can be entrusted, under what conditions, and under whose responsibility?” If you are on the product side, you should design outcome definitions, approval flows, logs, and fallbacks for failure before adding more features. From an organizational design perspective, FDEs and AI operational owners should be positioned as core cross-functional roles spanning sales, CS, product, and legal.

In the end, the strong organization in the age of superintelligent AI will not be the one with the smartest model. It will be **the organization that can delegate the most work with the least anxiety and the lowest supervision cost**. That is the essential point where AI operational responsibility and organizational design strategy become one.
