## Executive Summary

a16z’s “Charts of the Week” argues that the decline in public software company share prices reflects not worsening business results but “market doubts about future sustainability,” and that it is not a sector-wide indiscriminate selloff but a clearly selective move. At the same time, it presents three sets of “counter-pessimistic” data: Jevons-style demand dynamics in which falling intelligence costs lift demand, resilient employment data for AI-related roles, and a counterintuitive relationship between data centers and electricity prices. This article follows the figures in those charts to distinguish what is actually evidenced from what remains open to interpretation.

## Selective Selling: Prices Are Being Set Not by Current Performance but by “Long-Term Durability”

The recent weakness in software stocks does not reflect current business performance. According to a16z, multiples on next-12-month free cash flow are “below 2014 levels,” meaning investors are assigning the cash-generating power of software only the lowest premium in more than 10 years. What the market is questioning is not “the current 12 months” but “the 12 months after that,” which the firm frames as a narrative driven by fears of AI disruption.

But the decline is not indiscriminate.

- Over the past 30 trading days, the top quartile and median names in IGV (the software ETF) have outperformed the ETF as a whole, and what has dragged down the overall index is the bottom quartile, including some of the largest companies.
- The spread between quartiles began widening around “the turn of the calendar year,” and there is now roughly a 50-percentage-point gap between the top and bottom groups.
- What separates winners from losers is not revenue growth. Companies posting high growth rates (high-teens to low-twenties) are also concentrated in the bottom quartile, and there is almost no correlation between growth and recent performance.
**The conclusion is that what the market is pricing in is whether a company has “defensibility” against AI.** The top and second quartiles are dominated by cyber/observability and vertical SaaS, while the bottom two quartiles skew toward horizontal SaaS, cloud/infrastructure, and “other” categories including marketplaces and ad tech. In cyber, the market appears to view incumbent vendors as benefiting from a “trust premium,” while in vertical SaaS it sees barriers to entry in workflow, data, and customer relationships. Horizontal platforms, by contrast, are viewed as having little of that protection. Accumulated ARR and broad feature sets are “yesterday’s news”; software by itself is no longer a moat. That, a16z says, is the market’s message.

## The Sharp Fall in the Cost of Intelligence and Jevons-Style Demand

“From token-maxxing to token-optimizing” — the idea that marginal gains from frontier models no longer justify their added cost, and that customers are shifting to cheaper models, including open-weight ones, has been taken as evidence that frontier development itself may not be sustainable. But a16z presents evidence that lower intelligence costs are instead pushing demand higher in classic Jevons-style fashion.

- Compared with the PC revolution, AI has achieved in about 3 years the kind of price declines it took PCs about 20 years to deliver.
- On the consumer side, PNC Bank data show that paid household penetration remains small at just 2.2%, but monthly spending has risen by about 25% since the start of the year, climbing at a steeper angle. For reference, in 1997 PC ownership among 35- to 54-year-olds was only about 45%, while household computer ownership today is about 90% (about 97% including smartphones), showing that mass adoption takes time.
- In YipitData’s analysis of OpenRouter data, the token share of “Asia-based providers,” used as a proxy for open-weight models, has tripled since the start of the year to about 60%. At the same time, frontier token usage has continued to rise.
- On OpenRouter, both tokens per user and spending per user are increasing, but the former is rising far faster than the latter.
Cheap sub-frontier tokens are gaining share, while total spending continues to grow exponentially. **a16z concludes that this pattern — where efficiency increases demand rather than suppressing it — looks much closer to the bullish case than to the bearish argument that the market is engaged in “cannibalization.”**

## Employment Data Do Not Support “AI Job Destruction”

Against the contradictory story that AI is “not useful enough to generate ROI, yet useful enough to eliminate all jobs,” a16z argues that there is very little evidence of job losses. In particular, even entry-level hiring, long viewed as the labor market’s weak spot, appears to be seeing tailwinds.

- Revelio data show that summer internships for 2026 are running well above the levels seen in the prior year ('25) and the year before that ('24), though still below '23.
- ADP data show that wage growth for younger workers (ages 16–24) has rebounded from its 2025 low, which can be interpreted as a demand signal.
- In an analysis of Revelio job-posting data and Ramp spending data, “high-intensity AI adoption” corresponds to an increase of about 6 percentage points in entry-level headcount two years after adoption, while “low-intensity AI adoption” corresponds to only about a 0.50-percentage-point decline.
- According to Indeed Hiring Lab, since May 2025, jobs with higher AI exposure have seen stronger recoveries. Measured from the release of Claude Code, software engineer job postings are up about 15%, versus an overall decline of about 7%.
**a16z stresses that this data leaves little room for the claim that “AI is killing entry-level jobs.”** At the same time, it also notes that the metric of “AI exposure” itself comes with caveats: the higher the exposure of a job category, the larger the mismatch in estimation tends to be, so the interpretation still requires caution.

## The Paradox of Data Centers and Electricity Prices

This week, New York Gov. Kathy Hochul announced a 1-year moratorium on hyperscale data center development, citing protection for ratepayers. But a16z points to evidence that data centers may actually be lowering costs for ratepayers, calling the decision “strange.”

- Based on research by Berkeley National Laboratory and The Brattle Group, there is a negative correlation at the state level between rising electricity prices and increasing demand.
- Over the past 6 years, Texas and Virginia, where load growth has been strongest and data center development has been heavy, have seen almost no price increases. By contrast, California and New York, where price increases have been steepest and new development has been limited, have actually seen load growth decline. California reduced electricity demand by about 5% while still raising prices by about 33% more than the next-highest state.
- EIA data show that, with Ireland as the exception, EU countries also saw the largest price increases in the countries where electricity demand fell the most.
The reason is economies of scale in the grid. As demand rises, fixed infrastructure costs are spread more widely, lowering the price per ratepayer. **That means Gov. Hochul’s moratorium, rather than “protecting ratepayers,” could instead push electricity prices higher by removing demand that would otherwise help absorb fixed costs.** a16z also argues that this should not be overlooked because tech-related investment is now effectively the only investment category still growing, meaning the moratorium also carries the side effect of cutting off one of the most important drivers of investment growth.
