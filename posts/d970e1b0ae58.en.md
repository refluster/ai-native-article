## Executive Summary

George Sivulka’s a16z essay, “You Just Hired a Million Bad Employees” (July 14, 2026), flips the assumption that AI will replace labor. “AI was supposed to replace human labor. Instead, the opposite happened”: for the first time in history, humans have become cheaper than software, even as wasteful agent use simply reenacts the failures of human organizations. The core issue, he argues, is not technology but management—and what determines the outcome is neither tokens nor models, but the people who tell both what to do.

## The Reversed Premise: Humans Have Become Cheaper Than Software

Sivulka begins from a counterintuitive premise: “For the first time in history, humans are cheaper than software.” Rather than replacing people, generative AI has pushed token consumption to a point where its cost can be directly compared with human labor.

But he does not present this as a simple story of human superiority.

- “Humans are cheaper than tokens on average, but good tokens are cheaper at scale”
- In other words, the key is not average cost, but how effectively work can be concentrated into “good tokens.”
That asymmetry pulls agent operations back into the realm of personnel management. Hiring huge numbers of mediocre agents is structurally the same as hiring huge numbers of mediocre employees—that is the implication behind the essay’s title, “You Just Hired a Million Bad Employees.”

## Seven Parallels: Agent Workforces Reenact the Failures of Human Organizations

The backbone of the essay is a one-to-one mapping between inefficiencies in agent operations and pathologies familiar from human organizations.

1. **Tokenmaxxing is the new “throwing bodies at the problem”** — dumping in massive amounts of tokens without understanding how to use them effectively. It is driven not by necessity, but by ignorance. 2. **Loops are “meetings for the sake of meetings”** — Sivulka describes agents repeatedly calling themselves to patch weak initial outputs as a form of brute force that compensates for unclear instructions. 3. **Wasted tokens are the new headcount bloat** — just as organizations accumulate unnecessary layers, 80% of token consumption produces no business value. 4. **100X tokens are the new 10X engineer** — even if humans are cheaper on average, optimized tokens outperform human labor at scale. Management means transforming abundant but inefficient token use into concentrated, high-impact resources. 5. **Context hoarding is the latest job-protection strategy** — employees, afraid of being replaced, withhold knowledge from AI. “Nobody trains their replacement for free.” 6. **Evals are the new OKRs** — coding became AI’s breakout domain because evaluation mechanisms are built into it. Bringing AI into broader areas of work requires turning qualitative processes into measurable evaluation frameworks. 7. **The next trillion-dollar opportunity is the “transformation company”** — rather than upstart “neofirm” companies simply disrupting incumbents, the bigger opportunity lies in transformation services that continuously optimize the agent workforces of asset-rich existing enterprises, creating more value than any single application.

As an example of the redundancy that can be cut, under parallel 3 he cites Elon’s reduction of X’s headcount by 80% while improving performance. The scale of the next transformation market is estimated in terms of the “$21 trillion of services spend” that exists across the knowledge economy.

## The “Railroad of 1841” and the Next Management Revolution

Sivulka likens the current disorder to the period just before the birth of modern management.

- In the 1830s, railroad track mileage “120Xed” over the course of a decade, and railroads at their peak accounted for “roughly 60%” of the stock market.
- On October 5, 1841, two trains collided on the Western Railroad in Massachusetts. The cause was not technological failure, but failure of coordination.
- That accident pushed railroad companies to hire managers, formally define roles, and establish clear hierarchies—effectively inventing “modern management.”
The implication is straightforward. Whenever rapidly cheaper and more powerful agents of execution create disorder—railroads then, agents now—the solution has come not from a new model, but from new organizational design: role definition, hierarchy, and clarity.

## Conclusion: Evals and Management Will Decide the Next Trillion Dollars

The bottleneck Sivulka identifies is neither model performance nor token pricing, but managerial capacity: the ability to translate business requirements into executable specifications. The essay names concrete tools—Claude Code / Cowork, Copilot, Palantir, and Karpathy’s Autoresearch—but Sivulka’s argument does not reduce to the strengths or weaknesses of any particular product.

The closing line captures the point: “Humans just became cheaper than software. Someone still has to tell them both what to do.” In a world where the cost of “hiring” agents has collapsed, evaluation design through evals—and management that can turn instructions into specifications—becomes the truly scarce resource. That is the bet at the heart of the a16z essay.
