## Executive Summary

Maor Shlomo, who spent 7 years building a VC-backed company of more than 100 employees, created the vibe coding platform “Base44” in just 4 months to test what it would mean to build with zero employees. After launching in February 2025, it generated roughly $1.5 million in subscription revenue within 1 month, and in June that same year it was sold to Wix for $80 million. AI agents and coding tools are increasingly concentrating work once distributed across product managers, QA engineers, and developers into a single person, but compute costs that can reach hundreds of thousands of dollars per month, the absence of specialized judgment, and the physical burden of operating alone 24 hours a day still constrain the range within which this model can scale.

## How These Cases Define the Current Limits of “Solo Founding”

Two founders interviewed by Fortune (Beatrice Nolan, May 18, 2026) put concrete numbers on what AI-enabled solo entrepreneurship looks like in practice.

- **Maor Shlomo / Base44**: A “vibe coding” platform that lets non-technical users build software simply by describing what they want to a chatbot. It launched in February 2025, generated roughly $1.5 million in subscription revenue within 1 month, and was acquired by Wix in June for $80 million. Before that, Shlomo had spent 7 years building a VC-backed data business into a company of more than 100 people.
- **Dana Snyder / Positive Equation**: Founder of a consulting business for nonprofits. With no technical background, she used Replit’s AI coding tools to build a platform in 6 months. It provides step-by-step support for designing monthly giving programs and targets the roughly 93% of U.S. nonprofits that cannot afford to hire a human consultant. She remains the company’s only full-time employee.
Both are positioned as part of a wave of “AI-enabled solopreneurs” building and expanding businesses without the headcount that would once have been required.

## The Macro Data: Solo Entrepreneurship Is Not an Exception but a Trend

Solo founding is not an anecdotal boom but part of a longer-term trend.

- According to data published by the U.S. Census Bureau last May, nonemployer businesses number 29.8 million, generate about $1.7 trillion in revenue, and account for roughly 6.8% of total GDP. New business applications are running at more than 440,000 per month, a pace more than 90% faster than before the pandemic (though those figures are as of 2022).
- More recent estimates suggest that the number of solo entrepreneurs in the U.S. now exceeds 41 million.
- According to NYU Stern entrepreneurship professor J.P. Eggers, the average number of employees at companies less than 1 year old has been declining for 20 years. “15 to 20 years ago, it was 7 to 8 to 9 people. Even 2 to 3 years ago, it had fallen to 3 to 4.”
That contraction has been driven over many years by cloud computing, e-commerce infrastructure, and freelance platforms. AI is now adding a further compression of “time, cost, and expertise,” and beginning to redraw the economics of entrepreneurship. Small teams achieving outsized exits is not new, however: Instagram had about 13 employees when it was acquired in 2012, WhatsApp had about 55 in 2014 when it sold for up to $19 billion, and Minecraft developer Mojang had about 40 employees when Microsoft acquired it that same year for $2.5 billion.

## What AI Is Replacing Is the “Function” Itself

The founders say they are using AI agents and coding tools to automate work that once required dedicated hires, replacing not just individual labor but the specialized functions those roles embodied.

- In the first few months, Shlomo precisely tracked how he was spending his time, then built automation to win that time back. He deployed an agent to sift through user feedback and extract product ideas, an agent to patrol the platform and detect UX issues, and agents to build and run quality assurance tests—single-handedly covering work that would normally be split across product managers, QA engineers, and developers. In addition, he ran a daily app that monitored shipped code and automatically turned it into marketing content, including feature-update posts and charts drawn from sales data.
- He did shut down his customer support bot after 2 weeks. Handling support tickets himself was necessary to maintain a grounded feel for what was happening in the product.
- Snyder has done something similar: when a nonprofit answers a series of questions about its mission and donor base, an AI agent trained on her methodology generates 3 candidate names for a giving program, along with the rationale behind each one. That is work that previously took hours of consulting time. For pre-meeting outreach, if she provides a speaker’s LinkedIn URL and a connection message, the agent handles the sending.
Snyder describes this automation as something that “creates room to use your brain for the ideating that humans should really be doing.”

## The Three Limits of This Model

Behind the glamour of these case studies, Fortune makes clear the constraints that limit how far this approach can expand.

- **Lack of domain expertise**: In an experiment Eggers ran with Microsoft in which NYU Stern MBA students were asked to try building businesses with AI agents, the AI performed well at executing discrete tasks and accelerating brainstorming, but it could not replace the judgment that comes from having experts in the room. “You’re just trusting that what the AI gives you is pretty good. No one actually has the deep expertise needed across all these different domains.”
- **The non-obvious nature of cost**: Monthly AI bills at lean startups can reach hundreds of thousands of dollars, especially when always-on agents are running. That can rival the payroll costs they were supposed to replace. At the same time, compute costs scale more flexibly than headcount and do not come with expectations of equity, so founders who build this way tend to retain a relatively larger ownership stake in their businesses.
- **Wealth concentration and physical burden**: Even if many companies can be created with very small teams, that does not necessarily mean the number of successful companies will increase. There is a limit to how many winners the market can support, and the more AI takes on the work of teams, the more the wealth from success may flow to a smaller number of people. On top of that, the everyday burden of operating solo is heavy. In Base44’s early days, with no one monitoring things overnight, Shlomo set alarms every 2 to 3 hours to check that servers were still running, and even on nights when outages hit because of sudden traffic spikes, he could detect them within 10 minutes—not 6 hours later.
## Conclusion: The Boundary Is Expanding, but It Has Not Disappeared

Shlomo ultimately chose to sell because he recognized that building a truly global business would require expertise he did not have—specifically, the consumer marketing capabilities Wix had developed over many years. “I’m a product person. But to really scale, you need help.” Snyder, meanwhile, is already building her next set of AI agents, including ones for podcast production and keynote generation.

As AI systems take on more of product development, operations, and business development, the boundary of what one person can technically run is expanding again. But the question—“How big can you get before one person can no longer keep up?”—remains unresolved. Solo founding may be able to compress functions, but it still cannot erase the constraints of expert judgment, cost, and human endurance.
