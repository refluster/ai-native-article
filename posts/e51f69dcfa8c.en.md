## The Deep Principle by Which AI Is Simultaneously Reshaping Employment and Knowledge Creation

## Read It Not as “Will It Take Jobs?” but as “How Will It Change the Supply Curve of Problem-Solving?”

Around generative AI, several seemingly unrelated developments are unfolding at once: “Sam Altman denied a near-term jobs apocalypse,” “companies are restructuring SIer businesses and slimming down internal IT departments,” and “OpenAI’s model contributed to disproving a conjecture in discrete geometry.” In fact, all of these can be explained by the same principle. AI is not simply replacing people; **it is drastically lowering the cost of knowledge generation, exploration, and implementation, and in doing so, it is reorganizing both the composition of work and where value resides.**

## Analysis 1: What’s Happening Is Not the “Disappearance” of Employment, but a Change in the Granularity of Work and Its Redistribution

### What Altman’s Remarks Point To Is Structural Change, Not a Change in Aggregate Volume

The core of Sam Altman’s remarks is clear. AI will change “many kinds of work,” but a near-term “jobs apocalypse” is unlikely. The implication matters. The question is no longer whether employment will shrink in a zero-sum way, but **which tasks will be standardized, and which roles will be redefined**.

Two concrete facts stand out here. First, while Altman acknowledges that technology will change work, he also emphasizes that “people tend to adapt and new forms of employment emerge.” This suggests that the first-order effect of AI adoption is not the immediate destruction of total employment, but the reorganization of tasks. Second, in another article cited by the user, companies are said to be advancing “operational efficiency” and “decision support” through generative AI. That indicates that what is being replaced first is not entire occupations wholesale, but **partial processes within knowledge work**: drafting reports, organizing requirements, handling inquiries, code completion, and comparing design options.

These facts suggest that AI’s impact on the labor market will show up first not at the level of occupations, but at the level of workflow steps. In SIer firms and internal IT departments, for example, some of the work that was traditionally accumulated on a person-month basis is now being shortened through automated generation, summarization, test support, and knowledge search. As a result, headcount does not immediately go to zero; instead, more domains become manageable with smaller teams, and the surplus labor hours are reallocated to upstream design, governance, data platforms, and AI utilization strategy. The expectation that a jobs apocalypse is unlikely rests on this mechanism of redistribution.

## Analysis 2: What SIer Restructuring Shows Is That AI Is Moving the Profit Pool from Downstream Execution to Upstream Design

### As Implementation Is Automated, the Value of Consulting, Transformation, and Integration Design Rises

What can be read from the article summary, “AI disruption drives SIer business restructuring and the slimming of internal IT departments,” is that generative AI is not merely a tool for cutting IT costs. It is functioning as **a mechanism for rewiring the value chain inside the enterprise**. The article lines up elements such as “operational efficiency,” “decision support,” “productivity improvement across a wide range of industries,” “consulting and business process reform,” and “new value creation and competitive advantage.” These are not random items placed side by side.

To begin with, the phrase “slimming of internal IT departments” means that the marginal cost of repetitive knowledge work—maintenance and operations, routine development, help desks, internal inquiry handling—is falling. Complexity that used to be absorbed by adding people is beginning to be absorbed by AI assistants, code generation, and operational knowledge search. Next, “SIer business restructuring” indicates that while the contract-development model of selling engineering hours is coming under pressure, higher-value domains such as business design, transformation planning, data integration, AI governance, and support for in-house development are becoming relatively more important.

What matters here is that AI is not reducing **IT demand itself**. If anything, demand is increasing. But the composition of that demand is changing. In the past, revenue was generated in the phase after requirements definition, when large-scale implementation took place. But as generative AI speeds up portions of implementation, documentation, and testing, the source of profit shifts toward “what should be built,” “how should the business change,” and “how should the boundary of responsibility between humans and AI be designed.” These facts suggest that the more AI advances IT automation, the more it paradoxically **increases the scarcity value of people who can work across management, operations, and technology**.

## Analysis 3: The Discrete Geometry Counterexample Shows That AI’s Core Value Is Not Efficiency but the Expansion of Search Space

### In Research, It Matters Less as a “Machine That Gives Answers” Than as an “Amplifier for Hypothesis Search”

At first glance, the case in which OpenAI’s model helped disprove a conjecture in discrete geometry may seem far removed from discussions of employment and IT department restructuring. But this is where the common principle appears most clearly. According to the article summary, the model played a role in “advanced mathematical research” and contributed to “disprove a conjecture.” This is not simple clerical efficiency. It is a qualitative shift in which **AI is beginning to enter the process of discovering previously unknown knowledge**.

More concretely, in mathematical research, value does not come only from summarizing known information. What matters is how efficiently one can traverse a vast possibility space: searching for counterexamples, trying constructions, enumerating candidate lemmas, and comparing proof strategies. The fact that a model contributed to disproving a conjecture shows that AI is not merely a “compressor of existing knowledge,” but can also function as **a proposer of search order**.

This point also connects directly to business practice. In business transformation and system renewal, the essence is not to implement a single correct answer, but to compare multiple options in a short time and identify a workable one. Searching for counterexamples in mathematics, designing business processes in companies, and redesigning roles in the labor market all depend on the same two factors: the breadth of the search space and the number of iterations one can attempt. These facts suggest that the value of AI lies not in acting in place of humans per se, but in **lowering the cost of search and increasing the density of experimentation in knowledge creation**.

## Presenting the Common Principle

### AI Lowers the “Marginal Cost of Knowledge Creation” and Rewires Employment and Industrial Structure

Taken together inductively, the most plausible deep principle is this hypothesis: **the essence of AI lies in dramatically lowering the marginal cost of knowledge generation, exploration, and implementation, thereby shifting the entire supply curve of problem-solving to the right across society.** As a result, employment is not so much erased as reorganized; in industry, downstream work sees price erosion, while upstream design, integration, judgment, and accountability become more value-rich. And in research settings, access opens up to hypothesis spaces that were difficult for humans alone to explore, accelerating new knowledge creation.

Why does this hypothesis explain all three articles in a unified way? First, Altman’s statement that a “near-term jobs apocalypse” is unlikely is consistent with the idea that AI does not immediately eliminate total demand for work; rather, by making it possible to solve more problems at lower cost, it **creates new demand at the same time**. Second, SIer restructuring and the slimming of internal IT departments can be understood as the result of lower marginal costs for parts of knowledge work, shifting sources of profit from implementation to planning and transformation. Third, the discrete geometry counterexample shows that falling costs do not simply improve operational efficiency; they also make previously uneconomic forms of exploration and experimentation possible, expanding knowledge creation itself.

In other words, the “Why” is not that AI replaces people. It is that AI lowers the cost of **trying, comparing, combining, and implementing**. The “So What” is straightforward: competitive advantage going forward will be determined not by whether you use AI, but by where you reinvest human time once AI has made intellectual work cheaper.

## Forecasts and Implications

### What Will Happen in 2026-2030 Is Not Mass Unemployment but “Price Destruction in Middle Processes” and “Competition for Upstream Capabilities”

The future implied by this principle is fairly clear. First, what is most likely to happen over the next 2〜5 years is not “total employment collapse,” but **price destruction in middle processes**. The probability is high. The reason is that the set of articles already shows all three phases at once: early-stage adoption in the form of anxiety about jobs, the organizational-application phase in the slimming of IT departments, and the emergence phase in expansion into research use. The unit prices of requirements organization, document preparation, initial analysis, coding, testing, and inquiry response will continue to fall.

Second, on the other side of that shift, demand is likely to increase for **context-integrative work** such as business transformation, product design, data strategy, AI control, mathematical and research applications, and customer negotiation. Companies will not simply reduce headcount; they will become much more selective about what human time should be spent on. SIer firms will move away from selling person-months and toward responsibility-for-outcomes and transformation-partner models. Internal IT will be redefined from a maintenance function into one that leads business transformation and the AI operating model.

Third, in R&D and the professions, the real divide will not be between people who can use AI and people who cannot. It will widen between **those who can give AI questions, verify its outputs, and connect them to institutions and real-world settings**, and those who cannot. As the mathematical counterexample illustrates, AI is not a machine for correct answers but an amplifier of search. Accordingly, practitioners should cultivate not prompt technique in itself, but hypothesis setting, evaluation-metric design, falsification processes, and the securing of auditability.

The implications for readers can be narrowed to three points. First, inventory the work in your department not by “job type” but by “process step,” and identify the parts whose marginal cost AI can reduce. Second, reinvest the labor hours saved into upstream design, customer understanding, data preparation, and quality assurance. Third, do not measure AI utilization only through efficiency KPIs; track it with knowledge-creation KPIs such as **number of trials, decision-making speed, and number of new hypotheses**.

These facts suggest that the winners in the age of AI will not be “the companies that automated the most,” but **the companies that learned the most and redesigned the fastest**.
