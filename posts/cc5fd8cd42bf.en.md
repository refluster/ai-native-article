## Executive Summary

Nvidia (NVDA) alone contributed more than 10 percentage points — roughly 12% — of the S&P 500's +84% total return over the past five years, according to an analysis posted on X by the financial commentary account The Kobeissi Letter. Together with Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom, and Alphabet, the top five contributors account for roughly 25 percentage points, or about 30% of the index's five-year return. The post drew over 177,800 views and a mix of reactions, from warnings about concentration risk to outright skepticism of Nvidia's valuation.

## What the numbers show

According to the post published by The Kobeissi Letter on X on August 15, 2026, the S&P 500's total return over the last five years is +84%. Of that, Nvidia's contribution is "over 10 percentage points," described in the post as "more than any other stock by a wide margin."

- Nvidia's contribution alone represents roughly 12% of the S&P 500's entire five-year gain ("This represents ~12% of the S&P 500's entire 5-year gain").
- That is more than double Apple's contribution ("more than double Apple's... +5 percentage point contribution").
It is worth being precise about what "percentage-point contribution" means here: it measures how much a single stock's move pushed the market-cap-weighted index's overall return, not the stock's own price return. Nvidia's own five-year share-price return is almost certainly far higher than the index's +84% — the post does not state that number directly. What it states is only how much of the *index's* movement this one stock produced.

## The rest of the top five

The post goes on to list the next-largest contributors after Nvidia:

- Microsoft ($MSFT): roughly +3 percentage points
- Broadcom ($AVGO): roughly +3 percentage points
- Alphabet ($GOOGL): roughly +2 percentage points
Summed together, these five stocks — Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom, and Alphabet — contribute roughly 25 percentage points, or about 30% of the S&P 500's +84% five-year total return. The post closes with the line: "A handful of mega-cap stocks are driving the entire market." Every company on that list is tied, directly or indirectly, to AI infrastructure — semiconductors, cloud compute, or search/advertising platforms that fund AI buildout — meaning the index's apparent diversification is, in practice, increasingly concentrated exposure to a single theme.

Adding the five individual figures as stated (Nvidia "over 10," Apple 5, Microsoft 3, Broadcom 3, Alphabet 2) sums to roughly 23 percentage points, slightly under the post's stated combined figure of "~25." That gap is consistent with Nvidia's contribution being expressed as an open-ended "over 10" and with every other figure carrying an explicit "~" (approximately) — the post itself presents these as rounded estimates, not exact figures.

## The reaction: concentration worry versus valuation skepticism

The post attracted a range of replies. One user (@I_Just_Johnny) wrote: "strip nvidia out and suddenly the other 499 look a lot more ordinary" — a pointed way of noting how much of the index's apparent strength rests on one stock. Another (@RedRoccck) drew a more skeptical comparison: "Enron did the same. $nvda is cyclical, watchout for vicious crash once ai mania over" — questioning whether Nvidia's valuation, and the broader AI growth narrative behind it, is sustainable. A third reply was shorter and blunter: "Take profits fam $NVDA." Taken together, the replies split roughly evenly between concern about concentration and skepticism about the durability of the AI trade itself.

## Implication: index investors are carrying concentrated exposure they may not have chosen

What these numbers make visible is that investors who believe they hold a broadly diversified S&P 500 position are, in practice, carrying concentrated exposure to a handful of mega-cap companies built around semiconductors, cloud, and AI infrastructure. Nvidia alone driving over a tenth of the index's gain, and the AI-linked top five driving nearly a third of it, means that because the index is market-cap-weighted, a setback in growth expectations for any one of these companies could move the entire index disproportionately.

The replies to the post capture the two live readings of that fact. The "strip Nvidia out" comment is a warning that the index's headline return is propped up by a small number of names. The Enron comparison is a separate claim — skepticism about whether the AI growth story underlying those names is durable at all. Neither reply disputes the numbers themselves; both are arguments about how to interpret the concentration they describe. Passive investing's appeal has long rested on the promise of diversified returns; a return this concentrated in a small number of stocks tied to one theme is a reason to watch AI capital-expenditure cycles and demand trends not only as a story about individual companies, but as a question of index-level risk management.
