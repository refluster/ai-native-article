## Executive Summary

Anthropic's blog post "The AI-Native SDLC playbook" (published August 21, 2026, by Louis Claxton) argues that as coding speeds up, the bottleneck shifts to the stages that still run at human speed — plan, design, review, and deploy. Drawing on its Applied AI team's work with customers, the piece lays out concrete plays and governance mechanisms for embedding Claude Code across all six SDLC stages: Plan, Design, Build, Test, Deploy, and Maintain.

## Faster Code, a Governance Problem

The article's starting point is that the traditional SDLC was optimized for an era when writing and implementing code was the most time-consuming and expensive stage — that is no longer true. Once the build phase collapses to a matter of hours under agentic coding, three things happen at once, according to the piece:

- The bottleneck moves to the steps to the left and right of build — mainly plan, review/test, and deploy, which still run at human speed.
- Controls stop matching reality: reviewing each line by hand made sense when a person wrote it, but can't keep up once agents write most of the diff.
- Governance costs rise because exceptions still route through meetings and committees that meet weekly or monthly.
Using security review as the worked example, the piece notes that review teams are sized for human output, so once agents multiply code output, a regulated organization is forced to choose between a growing review queue or under-reviewed code shipping — neither of which it can accept.

## One Thread Through Six Stages: The Committed Artifact

The core mechanism of the AI-native SDLC is that each stage ends by committing a version-controlled artifact — `intent.md`, `spec.md`, `plan.md`, the diff and its tests, the PR with its review findings, and the incident record — and the next stage begins by reading it. That chain of commits doubles as the audit trail: who asked for what, what the agent produced, and who approved it.

Selected plays by stage:

- **Plan**: instead of routing an idea through backlog entries, story points, and refinement meetings, the originator brainstorms directly with Claude and commits the result as `intent.md`. Measured by the elapsed time from first conversation to a committed `intent.md`, and by the "survival rate" — the share of `intent.md` files the product owner accepts into Design rather than closes.
- **Build**: engineers start in Claude Code's plan mode and get an accepted `plan.md` before any code is written. Governance runs on three layers: `CLAUDE.md` (conventions, commands, architecture, and mistakes the team keeps making), `skills` that encode policy as version-controlled instructions, and `hooks` that block, allow, or ask before an action — plus parallel sessions in separate git worktrees and single-purpose subagents for recurring checks.
- **Test**: every session gets a way to check its own work — tests, a build, or a screenshot diff — before a human sees it. On top of that, the agent's own configuration (`CLAUDE.md`, skills, hooks) is regression-tested: the platform engineer collects 20 to 50 real tasks with expected outcomes into a continuous eval suite that runs in CI on every configuration change, gating merges on the pass rate.
## Deploy and Maintain: The Agent Acts Up to the Production Gate, Never Past It

Deploy rests on two mechanisms. The first is Claude in the PR review loop: a `REVIEW.md` at the repo root defines three passes — bugs, security, and compliance against `spec.md`/`plan.md` — and draws the line between "Important" and "Nit," with `@claude` mentions triggering fixes pushed back to the same PR. The second is hooks as approval gates; the article's worked example for a regulated enterprise shows a full managed-settings JSON — `permissions.deny` blocking secrets and arbitrary network egress, a `sandbox` block enforcing OS-level domain allowlisting, and admin-only controls like `allowManagedHooksOnly` and `requiredMinimumVersion: "2.1.193"`.

Maintain introduces a `bands.yaml`-style config that tiers a monitored metric by standard deviation: at 1σ the system only logs; at 2σ it invokes Claude read-only to diagnose; only at 3σ can Claude act, and even then only by opening a PR into the review gate or triggering a pre-approved runbook such as `runbook:rollback-deploy`. The article gives three worked examples: a CI test-failure-rate breach past 3σ has the agent quarantine the flaky test or open a revert PR for the review gate to decide; a post-deploy 5xx spike coinciding with a recent deployment triggers the existing rollback pipeline; and a PR-cycle-time drift produces a report for engineering leadership — showing the same harness applies to process metrics, not just production ones. Critically, no person sits in the invocation path: the agent's diagnosis is written back out as a Stage 1 `intent.md`, feeding the loop from the top. The piece also points to Claude Tag (public beta) making Claude a named member of Slack incident channels, with the channel itself serving as the audit trail.

Every play in the piece carries the same four-part shape — prerequisites and infrastructure, execution steps, governance considerations, and a leading/lagging measurement pair — which the article frames as a set of independently adoptable, dependency-labeled modules rather than an all-or-nothing rollout.
