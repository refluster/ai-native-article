## Executive Summary

Financial newsletter The Kobeissi Letter says U.S. data center power demand will rise 253% from 2026 levels, reaching a record 194 gigawatts by 2035. That would put data centers at roughly 20% of U.S. electricity consumption, with the burden concentrated in a handful of grid regions including PJM. Its conclusion is straightforward: the next constraint on AI is not model intelligence, but electricity.

## The Shape of the Numbers — A 253% Increase and 194 Gigawatts

The Kobeissi Letter’s central forecast is that U.S. data center power demand will rise 253% from 2026 levels, reaching a record 194 gigawatts in 2035.

To make that scale more intuitive, the newsletter converts 1 gigawatt into something “roughly equivalent to the generating capacity of one traditional nuclear reactor.” Taken at face value, that would mean the demand projected for 2035 is comparable to the generating capacity of about 194 nuclear reactors.

- Forecast: 194 gigawatts in 2035
- Growth rate: +253% versus 2026 levels
- Reference conversion: 1 gigawatt ≒ the capacity of 1 traditional nuclear reactor
The key point here is that this is not current consumption, but a forecast of future demand. A 253% increase does not mean existing facilities merely double or triple within a few years; it points to a trajectory in which demand itself expands by an order of magnitude. The comparison to 194 nuclear reactors does not necessarily imply that nuclear power will supply it. It is simply a baseline meant to give readers an intuitive sense of scale. Still, the comparison works because it translates the unfamiliar unit of generating capacity into a familiar form of infrastructure—one that takes many years and enormous capital to build.

As evidence, this is a single forecast figure. But its implication, as a conclusion, is that it becomes impossible to avoid asking whether the pace of new power infrastructure can keep up with that trajectory. Demand may change scale over 10 years, but generation and transmission projects do not have short lead times. That asymmetry is what turns the “shift in ratios” and “geographic concentration” discussed in the following sections into more than just statistics.

## Share of Electricity Consumption — From 6% to 20%

The other major axis is how data centers’ share of total U.S. electricity consumption changes over time. The Kobeissi Letter lines up three points in time.

- Today: about 6%
- 2030: about 12%
- 2035: about 20%
What this sequence shows is not just growth in absolute consumption, but a structural increase in data centers’ “share” of the overall power system. From about 6% today to about 12% in 2030—effectively a doubling—and then to about 20% in 2035. In other words, the assumption is that one-fifth of all electricity consumed in the United States will go to data centers.

The evidence here is just three percentages, but the conclusion that follows is that data centers are no longer merely one category of electricity demand. They are becoming a major variable in grid planning itself. From the supply side, they are turning into a counterparty with a weight that can no longer be ignored, alongside industrial, residential, and other forms of demand.

The growth in share is not uniform, either. The move from about 6% today to about 12% in 2030 is a doubling; from there to about 20% in 2035 is another substantial step up. This two-stage pattern—doubling in the first half, then expanding further in the second—means that data centers’ slice of the pie keeps ratcheting upward throughout the decade. The point is not that demand settles at a new level in any given year, but that the baseline itself keeps shifting. That is the real significance of the sequence.

## Geographic Concentration of Load — PJM as a Chokepoint

The Kobeissi Letter makes clear that this growth will not be evenly distributed across the United States, but will instead be concentrated in “a handful of grid regions.” The specific example it names is PJM.

It describes PJM as a grid operator supplying electricity to the District of Columbia and 13 states, including Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Ohio.

- Example of concentration: PJM
- PJM coverage area: District of Columbia + 13 states
- States named explicitly: Virginia, Pennsylvania, Ohio
This fact of geographic concentration matters because it cannot be captured by looking only at nationwide averages. If demand is skewed toward certain regions, then national figures such as 194 gigawatts or 20% will not materialize smoothly across the country. Instead, disproportionate stress will hit certain grids first. The conclusion, then, is that the bottleneck is likely to emerge not across the United States as a whole, but first at these kinds of chokepoints.

## Where the Claim That “Electricity Is the Next Constraint” Fits

The newsletter ends its post with the line “Electricity is the next AI bottleneck,” and opens with “We need more power.”

Those two lines are The Kobeissi Letter’s own interpretation of the figures it presents. Taking together the 253% increase, the 20% share, the comparison to 194 nuclear reactors, and the concentration in PJM, it places the constraint not on compute resources or model performance, but on electricity.

It is worth separating fact from interpretation here. The figures—194 gigawatts, 6→12→20%, and PJM’s coverage area—are the data points the newsletter presents. “Electricity is the bottleneck” is the conclusion it draws from them. The numbers themselves do not directly prove whether supply will or will not keep up with demand. Even so, when the growth rate on the demand side is this steep, and the load this geographically concentrated, it is internally consistent to read the next point of contention as power supply, transmission, and siting constraints. In that sense, the post presents a compact package of numbers suggesting that the center of gravity in the AI debate is shifting—from what can be computed to where, and how, that computation can be powered.
