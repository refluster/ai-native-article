## Executive Summary

The source argues that generative AI use is moving from a phase of “just pour in tokens and keep hitting the strongest model” to a phase of asking what those tokens produce and where they should be allocated. The focus is not on whether to introduce the technology, but on the practical realities of management: treating AI token spend as something to be governed alongside labor costs and marketing budgets.

At the same time, the source positions this issue—first made visible in coding—as one that will expand into a company-wide challenge through the rise of autonomous agentization in sales, legal, expense reimbursement, recruiting, and other functions. The starting point for responding is visibility into “who is using which model, and how many tokens,” along with optimizing model selection for each task.

## The End of Token Maximizing, and the Rise of ROI-Focused “Token Management”

The core argument of the source is that the past year of generative AI usage can be summed up as a phase of “token maximizing,” and that in 2026 the discussion has shifted to “token management.”

- **Evidence**
- The source says that over the past year, generative AI use was in a phase of “throwing tokens at the problem, hammering the strongest model, and feeding it as much context as possible.”
- It also notes that AI vendors themselves “have been effectively subsidizing usage through flat-rate subscriptions, while treating growth in usage volume itself as the success metric.”
- On that basis, it explicitly states that “in 2026, a clear backlash against this structure has begun to emerge.”
- It continues: “From here on, AI will no longer be a tool you can use without limit; like labor and marketing spend, it will become something whose ROI is scrutinized.”
- It also explains that the term “token management” is discussed more technically overseas as “token optimization.”
- **Conclusion**
- The source’s central point is that the discussion is shifting from a race to expand AI adoption volume to a management phase that asks how token inputs map to business outcomes.
- The object of management here is not total usage volume itself, but allocation decisions: what those tokens produced, and where they should be directed next.
## What Uber and Satya Nadella’s Remarks Show About the Financialization of Token Spend

The source presents the fact that both user companies and vendors have begun speaking explicitly about token efficiency and cost control as evidence of a paradigm shift.

- **Evidence**
- On Uber, the source states: “Uber put up a Claude Code leaderboard internally and encouraged engineers to compete on usage, and as a result burned through its entire 2026 AI coding budget in just four months.”
- It quotes Uber COO Andrew Macdonald from a podcast as saying:
> “Going forward, we have to discuss token consumption and the costs that come with it alongside labor costs.” “If we can’t draw a straight line showing that the amount we used led to more features delivered to customers, then this tradeoff is hard to justify.”

- On Microsoft’s Satya Nadella, the source quotes remarks from “the Morgan Stanley TMT Conference in March 2026”:
> “Why are we using this many tokens? In terms of token efficiency, there are not a few cases where people are actually doing the worst possible thing.” “What everyone will be working on over the next year is tools and software to make AI more efficient.”

- The source frames this by saying, “The same awareness of the problem is beginning to be voiced by the companies selling AI as well.”
- **Conclusion**
- What the source shows is that token spend is not an operational mistake at a handful of companies, but a structural issue now shared by both AI users and foundation model providers.
- Uber’s example in particular shows how “driving adoption” can connect directly to budget overrun, while Nadella’s remarks suggest that the next competitive axis will not be raw model performance, but efficiency tools and control software.
## Toward “Salary Plus AI Token Spend”: A Cost Item on the Same Level as Hiring and Staffing

The source argues that with the spread of coding agents, the very definition of the effective cost per engineer is beginning to change.

- **Evidence**
- The source states flatly: “In development, there is no longer any option not to use coding agents.”
- At the same time, it writes: “Any company that cannot afford to put a certain level of token cost into this is, in effect, already dropping out of the competition.”
- It further says that “it is no longer unusual for the token cost consumed by an engineer to reach several tens of percent of salary, or even half of it.”
- As a result, it argues that “the cost of hiring one engineer now has to be considered not just as ‘salary,’ but as a package of ‘salary plus AI token spend.’”
- Speaking about LayerX internally, the source says: “The phase of simply using tokens for the sake of it is over; now we are in the phase of how to generate outcomes.”
- It then adds: “We have used tokens where we should, optimized where we should optimize, and even so, the total number of tokens used has not changed that much from the period when LayerX was doing token maximizing.”
- From that, it concludes: “I feel that once you get above a certain threshold, large-scale token usage has become a necessary condition for competition.”
- **Conclusion**
- The source’s position is that token spend should be treated not as a mere variable expense, but as a foundational cost that accompanies human staffing.
- The important point is that optimization does not always significantly reduce total token volume. That suggests the main issue is not “cutting back,” but deciding where high-cost usage is truly necessary.
## This Is Not Just About Coding: “All Work Will Become Coding-Agentized”

The source argues that what currently looks like a token-cost issue centered on engineers will spread in the same form to non-development departments.

- **Evidence**
- The source explicitly says: “The important point here is that this explosive token consumption is not limited to the special domain of coding.”
- It lists the relevant functions as “sales signal collection and proposal drafting, customer support inquiry handling, automated expense submission and approval, legal contract review, marketing ad operations, and HR recruiting screening.”
- It says these are “being redesigned one after another as autonomous agents.”
- And it notes that “autonomously executing agents consume amounts of tokens that are incomparable to the previous model of ‘a human asks one question and gets one answer.’”
- It continues: “The more agents come to do the work, the more all AI usage will converge on the same structure we are now seeing with coding agents.”
- It also explicitly states that “the management challenge of AI token cost, which currently appears to be ‘just an engineer problem,’ will soon become a company-wide management issue.”
- **Conclusion**
- The scope of the source’s argument extends beyond software development to the redesign of company-wide operations.
- Coding agents are only the leading case; the forecast is that every department will soon face the same structure of high-frequency, high-autonomy, high-token-consumption work.
## The Real Costs Hidden by Subscriptions, and the Response Sequence of Visibility → Model Routing

The source warns that current AI subscription pricing obscures the real cost structure, and that the gap could emerge later as a cost shock. Its proposed response has two stages: first visibility, then optimization of model selection.

- **Evidence**
- The source says that “the AI subscription fees we are paying right now are far below the real underlying cost.”
- It writes: “Today’s AI subscriptions are routinely being provided at a loss by the companies offering foundation models, and there is a large gap between the flat fee we pay and the actual cost being incurred.”
- It adds: “If you recalculate the same level of usage under API-based metered billing, the cost jumps by an order of magnitude.”
- As future risks, it points to the possibility that the subsidy will be passed on to customers through “a shift from flat-rate to token-based billing, steering users to higher-tier plans, or price increases.”
- In that case, the current intuition of “‘tens of thousands of yen per month per ID now; for a company with 100 employees, that means annual costs in the tens of millions of yen’” could jump to “annual costs for the company as a whole in the order of hundreds of millions or even billions of yen.”
- As the first thing to do, it writes: “Make it visible ‘who is using which model, and how many tokens.’ That’s it.”
- The question that follows visibility is: “Did that task really need that model?”
- As a concrete example, it says: “Using a top-tier reasoning model (Claude Opus 4.8 or GPT-5.5, for example—the latest models as of May 2026) for a question like ‘What’s the weather today?’ is obviously excessive.”
- It also notes that “people who are less familiar with AI tend to just choose the strongest model for now,” while “people who use AI deeply switch between models depending on the nature of the task.”
- As a technical measure, it describes prompt-based detection of “task/model mismatches,” returning guidance such as: “For this task, a lighter model would be more than enough.”
- As a more advanced state, it points to a setup in which “rather than people choosing a model each time, the system automatically assigns the optimal model,” with “model routing automatically optimizing cost behind the scenes.”
- **Conclusion**
- The source’s proposed order of response is clear: first, visibility into actual usage; second, proper matching of models to tasks.
- The essence of the problem is not simply that AI is expensive, but that under subscription pricing the real cost is invisible, making it easy for organizations to tolerate systematically overpowered model choices.
- In the agent era, ad hoc human judgment will not scale, so the ultimate unit of management shifts from individual thrift to automated routing implemented at the system level.
