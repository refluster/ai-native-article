The center of gravity in the AI debate is quietly shifting from “what it can do” to “how to measure it and how to run it.” Consider three developments that seem unrelated at first glance. On June 22, 2026, Elon Musk said on X that “AI will probably exceed the combined intelligence of all humans in 4 to 5 years.” That same June, Reuters reported that researcher John Jumper was expected to leave Google DeepMind and join Anthropic. And an RBC Capital Markets CIO survey showed in hard numbers that enterprise AI spending has shifted “from proof of concept to production.” Forecasts, talent moves, spending—these appear to belong to entirely different domains. In fact, they can be explained by the same principle. In every arena, AI “capability itself” is no longer the central unresolved issue; scarcity has moved to the layers around it.

## The Key Question Is Shifting from “Whether It Can” to “Whether It Can Be Measured”

What “Musk’s statement that ‘in 4 to 5 years AI will exceed all human intelligence,’ and the response that it is not quantifiable” captured was not whether the prediction itself was right or wrong. Because Musk’s statement came with neither concrete conditions for achievement nor any measurement standard, the response was not “that’s not true,” but rather, “the comparison may be possible, but it is meaningless.” In other words, the debate has shifted away from whether AI will surpass humans and toward a question of measurability: whether the metric of the “sum total of intelligence” is useful for decision-making in the first place.

That is revealing. Claims about the upper bound of capability no longer even serve as the main target of rebuttal. The rebuttal lands one level outside that, on the question of “how that capability should be measured in a way that matters.” Capability is now swallowed as a premise, and the design of measurement standards has become the true front line of dispute.

### The “Sum Total” May Be Measurable, but It Does Not Help Decisions

Even if the “sum total of intelligence” could be defined, it would not determine an investment decision or a hiring decision. Metrics that affect decision-making are those that split action paths: success rates on specific tasks, output per unit cost, or the detectability of errors. Musk’s statement was dismissed as “possible, but meaningless” precisely because it belonged to the former category, not the latter—a quantity that may be measurable but does not change behavior. That line is the core of why the debate has moved from capability to measurability. Who gets to define meaningful metrics is beginning to create value independently of how high or low capability itself is.

## What Is Scarce Is Not Research Capability but Its Integration

“John Jumper’s move to Anthropic, and what it reveals about the blurring of boundaries between research, product, and strategy in AI labs” should be read not merely as evidence of an increasingly fierce competition for talent, but as a change in role structure. The reason a Nobel Prize-caliber researcher’s move draws this much attention is that what he brings is not just “research capability.” The point is that the boundaries between research, product, and strategic leadership—roles that were once carried by separate people—are beginning to dissolve.

Put differently, what leading-edge labs are fighting over is not pure research output. What is scarce is the ability to turn research results into products and set their direction as strategy—in other words, the ability to integrate capability into a form that can actually be operated. The boundaries are melting because capability on its own is no longer the differentiator; what creates the gap now is integration.

## The Question of “Does It Work?” Is Settled, and Only “Can It Be Run?” Remains

“RBC’s CIO survey showing enterprise AI spending shifting ‘from proof of concept to production’” provides the most quantitative support for this shift. Of more than 100 CIOs and technology leaders, over half said “AI is already in production,” and 35% said they expected it to be in production within six months. 100% of respondents had budgeted for AI/LLMs, and 91% said they had created new budget lines for them. In the enterprise field, the question “can AI be used?” has already been settled.

What remains is the operational question. Most symbolic is that 90% said token budgets were “manageable.” Cost is no longer an unknown fear; it has become an operational variable that can be measured and governed. Vendor selection can be read through the same lens—the most frequently used tool was ChatGPT at 57%, far ahead of Claude (12%). That gap reflects less a superiority of capability than the strength of having already been built into the default operating base for production use.

### Defaults Are Chosen by Operations, Not Capability

It would be hasty to read ChatGPT’s 57% as a capability ranking. Given that 100% of respondents have budgets and many have already embedded AI into production operations, what that gap reflects is not “which model is the smartest,” but “which model became the default in the operational stack first.” Once embedded into operations, a default tends to persist through inertia even when capabilities are closely matched. Switching costs and revalidation costs outweigh marginal differences in capability. Here too, what is scarce is not capability itself, but securing the operational seat first.

## The Shared Principle: Scarcity Has Moved Outside Capability

Set these three facts side by side, and a single line emerges. The rebuttal to Musk’s statement says, “ask not about capability, but about the metric.” Jumper’s move says, “compete not for capability, but for integration.” The RBC survey says, “measure not capability, but operations.” Together, these facts suggest that in every AI-related domain, the stage in which capability itself was the central unresolved problem is coming to an end.

Why is that happening? Once capability clears a certain threshold, and once it rapidly generalizes, differences in capability produce only marginal value. When everyone can access models that are sufficiently strong, what determines the outcome shifts to “what you can do with that model, how reproducibly you can do it, and at what cost.” That is why scarcity slides outward—not into capability itself, but into the surrounding layers: measurement, meaning what should be measured for decision-making; integration, meaning the bundling of capability into products and strategy; and operations, meaning the governance of budgets and costs in production.

This principle can be stated in a falsifiable way. If capability itself were still the constraining factor, companies would not be saying token budgets are “manageable,” leading-edge labs would be fighting over pure research output rather than integrative roles, and superintelligence forecasts would be contested over whether they could be achieved rather than over the standard by which they should be measured. In every case, the observed facts point in the opposite direction. Therefore, the claim that “scarcity is shifting from capability to measurable operations” is, at least in the current phase, supported.

## Predictions and Implications

If this principle is correct, several changes are highly likely to continue. First, those who define the metrics will shape the market. The sense of “manageable” shown in the RBC survey is already evidence that measurement has become routine, and the next question will be how to design metrics that actually connect to results. The actors who control evaluation axes and definitions of cost per outcome are likely to build an advantage outside the capability race itself.

Second, the Jumper-style fusion of roles will spread beyond the labs. The scarcity of one person who can unify research, product, and strategy will be reproduced on the buyer side as well—people who can integrate domain knowledge with AI operations will become scarce across all functions. The dissolution of boundaries is not a lab-specific phenomenon; it is a general consequence of a world in which capability has become commoditized.

Third, ChatGPT’s 57% default is an advantage produced not by capability but by being embedded into the operational base, and that advantage compounds. Even if later entrants catch up on capability, defaults that are built into operations do not move easily.

The practical implication is straightforward. The phase of competing over “whether it can do that” will not last much longer. The real question is whether it can be run in production, in a measurable form, with reproducibility. Pulling this into my own domain of customer experience, consistency does not create value as a display of capability; it creates value only when it is designed as a standard that can be measured and operated. If scarcity has moved outward, then what deserves investment has likewise moved to the layer just outside capability.
