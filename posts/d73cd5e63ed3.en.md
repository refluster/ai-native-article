As AI capabilities improve, what determines winners and losers is no longer how smart the model is, but what you direct that power toward and how you decide whether to trust its output. Three facts that seem unrelated at first glance point in the same direction. George Sivulka of a16z wrote that “for the first time in history, humans are cheaper than software,” Anthropic’s migration guide says new models can execute autonomously for hours at a time, and Deloitte’s research shows that the success or failure of financial agent adoption is not determined by model intelligence. In fact, all of these can be explained by the same principle: intelligence itself is beginning to overflow, and what is scarce is the layer that “instructs and verifies” it.

## “Smartness” No Longer Decides the Outcome

“AI made humans cheaper” (Sivulka, a16z, July 14, 2026) flips the premise on its head. AI was supposed to replace human labor. What actually happened was the reverse: for the first time in history, humans became cheaper than software, and cheap agents then proceeded to reenact the same failures found in human organizations—waste, duplication, and incomplete instructions. Sivulka describes this as “you hired a million mediocre employees,” and identifies seven parallels between managing agents and the failures of human organizations. The core point is this: “what determines who wins is neither tokens nor models, but the person telling both what to do.” The problem has shifted from technology to management.

A completely different route arrives at the same conclusion in “Prerequisites for Introducing Agentic AI into Finance and Accounting.” In Deloitte’s research, 48% of finance professionals in strategic core roles have already adopted AI agents, far above the 18% among those in more supportive roles. That gap of nearly thirty points is not a difference in access to models—both groups can equally buy smart models. The gap emerged on the design side: deciding “what agents should do and in what business context they should be embedded.” Here too, what is scarce is not intelligence but the judgment that determines where to aim it.

### Cheap Inputs Do Not Differentiate

As economics teaches, once an input becomes cheap and abundant, it ceases to be a differentiator. Just as electricity and bandwidth became things you simply assume are there, smartness is sinking into the background as a given. Sivulka’s “humans are cheaper than software” and Deloitte’s “48% versus 18%” describe the same phenomenon from above and below. The former says the price of labor has collapsed; the latter says that even with that cost collapse, outcomes still diverge—and what creates that divergence is operating design. The moment low cost becomes the common starting point, competition shifts upstream—what to instruct—and downstream—how to trust the output.

## What Long-Running Autonomy Breaks Is “How We Wait”

The “Claude Fable 5 Migration Guide” supports this shift from the technology foundation side. In that guide, Anthropic explicitly says that the new models Claude Fable 5 (and Claude Mythos 5) behave differently from Claude Opus 4.8 and require updates to prompts and scaffolding. The biggest change is the lengthening of behavior: a single request may take several minutes at high effort, and autonomous execution may last for hours. That requires client timeouts and harnesses to be redesigned from synchronous blocking to asynchronous checks. The premise itself—“call the model and get an answer back”—breaks down, and in its place, front and center, comes the design of verification and management: “how to monitor an agent that keeps running, when to intervene, and what counts as completion.” This is Sivulka’s “the one who gives instructions wins,” restated at the implementation level.

### The Way We Trust Output Is Changing

The guide goes further, pointing out that older instructions telling the model to output reasoning in the response body can trigger rejection from `reasoning_extraction` in the new models. In other words, the very procedure by which we decide how to trust output has changed. The smarter models become—and the longer and more autonomously they run—the farther their internal reasoning moves from human view, and the less transparent it becomes. We have to give up the habit of “peeking at the intermediate thinking to feel reassured” and instead build separate mechanisms to “verify the result from the outside.” Improvements in capability come back to us directly as higher verification costs. That is why not only “instruction” but also “verification” emerges as a scarce resource.

## What Separates Success from Failure Is Redesigning the Operating Model

The three facts intersect most clearly in what Deloitte calls “redesigning the entire operating model.” The real prize in financial agents is not general-purpose tools but specialized agents embedded with business context, and what determines success or failure is not model intelligence itself but the operating model, including clean data foundations, auditable data lineage, and human verification. In that single sentence, Sivulka’s “management” and the migration guide’s “asynchronous verification” coexist in the language of actual practice. Clean data foundations and auditable lineage are “prerequisites for giving correct instructions,” while human verification is the downstream step “for deciding whether the output can be trusted.” Deloitte’s point that specialized agents are the real target is also part of the same story: more than general smartness, what matters is directing capability accurately into business context—in other words, the quality of the instruction.

## The Shared Principle: Scarcity Shifts to “Instruction and Verification”

The three facts—that humans have become cheaper than software, that models now run autonomously for hours, and that success in finance adoption is decided by operating design—together suggest one thing. **As model capabilities improve and costs fall, value and scarcity move away from “the model itself” and toward “the layer that instructs the model and verifies its output.”**

The logic of the induction is straightforward. If smartness were what created the gap, then whoever bought the smartest model should win. But across three domains—investor commentary (Sivulka), migration technology (the Fable 5 Migration Guide), and finance practice (Deloitte)—the same conclusion appears: outcomes are decided outside the model, namely by “what you instruct it to do (orchestration)” and “how you decide whether to trust the output (verification).” When three independent observations that are not citing one another all point in the same direction, the natural reading is not coincidence but structure. Intelligence that has become cheaper and stronger is becoming an everyday input like electricity or bandwidth. The only places differentiation remain are upstream and downstream—instruction and verification.

This principle is falsifiable. If, over the next year, the variable that best explains differences in outcomes turns out to be “which model was selected,” while investment in instruction design and verification systems shows no correlation with results, then this view is wrong. Conversely, if differences between two companies using the same model can be explained by differences in the design of instruction and verification, then the principle is supported. This is not a vague claim that “AI will change the world,” but a wagerable proposition: “what explains next year’s performance gaps will not be model choice but verification design.”

## Predictions and Implications

If this principle is correct, then the axis of organizational competition is highly likely to shift from “procuring smarter models” to “the operational design of instruction and verification.” The reason is that the three cases above, in independent domains, all point in the same direction at the same time. More concretely, three changes can be expected.

First, harnesses and workflows will be rebuilt from synchronous assumptions to asynchronous, verification-first assumptions (the direction already shown by the migration guide, and therefore highly likely). Once long-running agents are taken as a given, mechanisms for monitoring, interruption, and completion judgment will become a bigger implementation challenge than simply having a smart model. Second, the human role will shift from “execution” to “designing constraints and verifying outputs.” What the leading 48% in Deloitte’s research have in hand is not a smarter model but a more refined operating model—and the gap with the trailing 18% will likely widen further as a gap in the maturity of instruction and verification. Third, if both “cheap humans” and “cheap agents,” in Sivulka’s phrase, are equally prone to waste, then management itself—management that suppresses waste—becomes both the biggest cost factor and the biggest differentiator. If you have hired a million mediocre employees, the winner will be the one who directs them best and inspects their output most rigorously.

The practical implication for readers is clear. The next thing to invest in is not a smarter model but the quality of instruction and the mechanisms of verification. If you have time to spend agonizing over which model to choose, you would be better off spending that same time designing what to entrust to that model, and by what procedure its output will be verified before it is trusted. In an age when intelligence is abundant, what is scarce is not smartness itself, but the discipline to direct that smartness correctly and to doubt it correctly.
