## Executive Summary

In "Charts of the Week" (a16z, October 30, 2026), Moses Sternstein argues that the fear of an AI jobs apocalypse has faded from its May peak, and that job postings for technical roles are rising. Market anxiety has instead shifted to whether AI capex will pay back. The chart at the center of that worry, the "Rack Spread" of falling token prices against rising GPU rental rates, rests on small, biased samples, he writes, and should not be read as a definitive demand signal.

## Job fear is receding

Sternstein starts with Microsoft's employee buyout offer, made to about 60,000 employees, roughly a quarter of its workforce. If everyone accepted, it would be worth something like $10B. The company's CFO described the goal as "offer employees agency" and expected low uptake because internal opportunities remain strong. Few tried to turn it into an "AI is taking all the jobs" story; to the extent AI played a role, speculation pointed to Microsoft's capex commitments as the unspoken driver of cost cutting.

**Evidence (from the source)**

- News reports on AI and job loss have reverted to roughly where they were a year ago.
- Explicit "AI-related" layoff announcements in August and September were the lowest of the year.
- Indeed data show job postings for software engineers (SWEs), manufacturing, and especially engineering continuing an upward inflection from last year. HR postings have returned to their pre-pandemic baseline.
- Since October of last year, postings for Engineers are up about 30%, SWEs about 26%, and Manufacturing about 13%.
**Conclusion (the author's reading)**

The breathless declarations of widespread AI substitution lacked evidence then and lack it now, and AI may be raising demand both for the people who build the infrastructure and for those who put AI to work. He does not claim AI is leaving the workforce unchanged; he says the changes are likely still in the early innings.

### A side note on blasters

Drawing on Lightcast data, Sternstein notes that explosives and ordnance workers earn a midpoint of about $30/hr, roughly 20-25% above the median worker, and that about 71% of them are aged 34 or younger, young for a skilled trade. He himself cautions that median hourly pay varies substantially by state even after cost-of-living adjustment, and that composition and sample size (military munitions experts, special-effects crews, ammo-plant line workers) may be doing some of the work, so skepticism of the headline figures is in order. His conclusion stands anyway: for the young and hungry, it is a good time to learn to code, to weld, and to make things explode.

## AI-referred shoppers arrive with their wallets out

The second theme is AI and e-commerce. Sternstein says agentic shopping is still early, but online buying keeps growing.

- Visa reported roughly $46B in revenue (on $15.7T in volume) for the fiscal year, up nearly 15% year over year, the largest full-year revenue increase in 15 years excluding M&A and the pandemic recovery year. He notes Visa is not a perfect proxy for e-commerce but is in the ballpark.
- Bain's consumer survey shows rising shopping intentions for Black Friday, Prime Day and Cyber Monday; only 10% said they would avoid the e-commerce season entirely.
- Adobe data show AI-referred e-commerce traffic engages more, spends more time, views more pages, is more likely to convert and less likely to bounce, with a roughly 50% revenue uplift compared with non-AI-referred traffic.
- The average price per Meta ad (US and Canada) has grown 20%+ year over year for two consecutive quarters. CPMs had mostly stabilized through 2024 and 2025, so he calls it a step-change rather than a seasonal spike, driven apparently by Feed and Stories. Meta's use of AI to improve its engines may be part of the story, he says, but that is a suggestion, not a finding.
His conclusion is measured. Whether or not AI shopping agents are inbound, AI is clearly affecting consumer behavior and the economics up and down the stack. It is a boon for payments, discovery and the retailers themselves, but a rapidly changing landscape with potentially very different effects on incumbents and challengers.

## The Rack Spread: the bearish signal the market is reading

The third theme is the heart of the piece. In spring, Sternstein writes, the fear was that AI was so good it would take all the jobs; now the fear is that AI is not good enough to justify the capex on a matching timeline. He takes no view on the bulls or the bears and observes only that, for the moment, the bears preside.

**Market moves (from the source)**

- The cost of insuring the debt behind the buildout and the chipmakers has soared since the middle of the summer. He notes this is not all AI FUD: the cost of debt generally has risen, and heavy fresh issuance adds supply-demand pressure.
- Goldman's basket of "AI Winners" has given back all of its relative gains against its basket of "AI At-Risk." From January to June the winners won big; from July to October they fell back even faster than they rose.
**What the Rack Spread is**

The chart was recently dubbed the "Crunch Spread," but a16z Editor-at-Large Alex Danco has pointed out that "Rack Spread" is a better name: "rack" refers to server racks and sounds like "crack," as in the Crack Spread, the commodities indicator of the price difference between crude oil and its refined products.

The gist is that token prices are falling while GPU rental rates are rising. Cast GPUs as crude, tokens as the refined products and models as the refiners, and the story is that compute is in high demand while the token layer has a surplus. The implication is bad news for the model layer, since tokens become a fungible commodity, and bad news for the compute layer too, since if tokens are a commodity there is less reason to spend heavily on compute to train the next best thing.

**The author's rebuttal**

1. **The analogy is inapt.** Oil and its products are volume-bound: you cannot make more gasoline than the oil it comes from, so the Crack Spread caps refiner margins. GPUs and tokens have no equivalent unit. Token prices may fall while token volumes rise, so total spending on tokens can also rise, and the spread says little about the balance of power in a not-fixed pie. 2. **The data behind the indexes is small and biased.** Ramp, OpenRouter, Silicon Data and Ornn derive token volumes and spending from the subset of users who expose them to those vendors, which most large enterprises, the lion's share of demand, do not. Those who do are likely the ones most focused on managing token spend and routing to the lowest-cost models. He grants that these vendors are transparent about what they measure. 3. **Even at face value, spending is up.** On his napkin math, Ornn's blended price per token multiplied by OpenRouter's token volume gives about a 160% increase in token spending since May; Ramp's spend data gives roughly 122%. He stresses this is not a rigorous methodology, and that the two figures still differ substantially. 4. **The spend data is noisy.** An Atlanta Fed survey puts mean AI spend per employee above $1,000. Ramp's data point to a median of $15.70 per month, or $188 a year. The mean-versus-median gap may explain much of it, he says, but he cannot tell which is more correct.

**Conclusion**

Actual token spending and consumption are opaque. AI companies do not report earnings, and no panel is large or representative enough for a sound estimate. The proxies are interesting and useful, he writes, but they are too weak to be the centerpiece of either the FUD case or the permabull case. GPU index pricing is, for reasons he does not go into, a much stronger signal, though it too has limits.

## What to take from it

Three implications, each read from the figures in the text rather than stated by the author in these words.

- **The jobs debate and the investment debate are mirror images of the same AI fear.** In May the worry was that AI is too good; this autumn it is that AI is not good enough. By his account, neither moved the market on sufficient evidence.
- **Measuring demand is the weakest link.** Indexes built from small, biased samples are shaping the market's narrative about capex. For anyone measuring their own AI spend, the difference between mean and median, and between those who disclose and those who do not, is worth checking before deciding anything.
- **Keep the unit of verification consistent.** The roughly 50% revenue uplift describes shoppers who arrived via AI, not orders completed by an agent. Sternstein himself calls agentic shopping early. Keeping the two apart is a precondition for reading this kind of data.
