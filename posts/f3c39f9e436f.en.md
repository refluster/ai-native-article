An AI safety-evaluation guide, a record-scale IPO filing, and an enterprise-software release look unrelated. They are explained by the same principle: **a claim is never stronger than the measurement behind it, and the unit of trust is shifting from the number itself to a disclosure of what the number counted.**

## Three facts: each separates the number from its basis

**Safety evaluation.** According to "Safety evaluations can't be measured by refusal rate: how Anthropic says to build trustworthy AI safety evaluations" (the source L2, titled in Japanese), Anthropic published recommendations on 13 November 2026 for designing, running and reporting AI safety evaluations. Its central point: a claim that a model is "safer than the last generation" is no more certain than the evaluation behind it. Three pieces of advice: write down the construct you want to measure first, validate the grader against human judgment, and report uncertainty.

**The IPO filing.** According to "Anthropic files for one of the largest IPOs ever: first-half revenue above $16 billion, net loss of $80.7 billion", the company filed a 335-page document on 26 October. First-half 2026 revenue exceeded $16 billion, 14 times the prior-year period. Net loss was $80.7 billion, of which $72.4 billion was a one-time accounting adjustment tied to financing contracts. Amount raised, valuation and offer price are not set; the listing is planned on the Nasdaq Global Select Market as "ANT".

**Business software.** According to "SAP's Q3 AI release shows a new structure for business software: assistants that orchestrate agents", SAP's "AI Release Highlights Q3 2026" says 45,000 cloud customers use its AI solutions. The centre of the release is a Joule assistant per business domain orchestrating many specialist agents. The source notes that every effect figure is either SAP's "expected" value or a customer's self-report.

## Analysis 1: read the headline number apart from what is inside it

The IPO case is clearest. Read alone, a net loss of $80.7 billion suggests a vast deficit. But in the filing, $72.4 billion of it is a one-time accounting adjustment on financing contracts. Subtracting gives about $8.3 billion (my arithmetic, 80.7 minus 72.4; not an "adjusted" figure from the filing). The same headline number changes meaning by roughly a factor of ten depending on what is inside it. The "14 times" revenue growth likewise means something only against its stated base, the prior-year period.

In the SAP case, the adoption figure (45,000 customers) and the effect figures carry different degrees of certainty. The first is a count of customers; the second is expectation or self-report. Numbers of different strength sit side by side in one document.

## Analysis 2: an evaluation is itself a measurement

Anthropic's recommendations restate this problem from the evaluator's side. An easy metric such as refusal rate is simple to count but may not be the construct you actually care about, safety. So write the construct first, validate the grader against human judgment, and report uncertainty. Each of the three puts the basis of a number next to the number.

Note that the advice is aimed not at a conclusion ("it is safe") but at how conclusions are made. The line that "safer than the previous generation" is no firmer than the evaluation behind it fits all three topics. The strength of the IPO's net-loss claim and of SAP's effect claims likewise rests on how the counting was done.

### How the three cases line up

- Safety evaluation: the number is a metric such as refusal rate; the basis is the construct definition, grader validation and reported uncertainty.
- IPO filing: the number is the $80.7 billion net loss; the basis is the breakdown showing $72.4 billion as a one-time accounting adjustment.
- Business software: the number is the expected effect; the basis is whether it is SAP's expectation or a customer's self-report.
## The common principle: the unit of trust moves from "number" to "number plus basis"

Why, then So What.

Why does the same thing appear in three settings? Where AI is involved, the cost of producing a number is falling quickly, while the cost of checking what it counted is not. Evaluations can be run automatically at volume. A headline fits on one line. A product's effect fits on one slide. The cheaper numbers get, the scarcer a disclosed basis becomes.

These facts suggest the following. A trustworthy claim is not one that publishes a number alone but one that publishes the number together with a statement of what it counted. The readers differ (evaluators for Anthropic's advice, investors for the filing's breakdown, customers for SAP's "expected" label), but the form demanded is the same.

So What: under this principle, the first question for a recipient of a number is not "how many?" but "what was counted?". A number with no stated basis, large or small, is weak material for a decision.

## Predictions and implications

Three predictions follow.

**Prediction 1: Disclosure that places the basis beside the number becomes the standard reporting form for AI evaluations (confidence: medium to high).** Anthropic has published three concrete items (construct, grader validation, uncertainty), which makes it easy for a third party to check whether they are present; a report that omits them stands out. It would be falsified if major safety-evaluation reports keep claiming "safer than the last generation" without these items and that goes unchallenged.

**Prediction 2: Effect figures for agentic business software stop being used for comparison unless "expected" and "measured" are written separately (confidence: medium).** As the SAP release shows, when adoption counts and effects sit in one document, a document that states effects are self-reported lets readers adjust the weight of the number. It would be falsified if effect figures with no stated origin keep serving as the main basis for purchasing decisions.

**Prediction 3: Headline figures such as large losses or growth rates will not even serve as a starting point for debate without a breakdown (confidence: medium).** That most of an $80.7 billion net loss was a one-time accounting adjustment shows that arguments quoting only the headline can mislead.

### A practical takeaway

If you cite or report numbers, adopt three habits. First, before writing the number, write in one sentence what it counted. Second, distinguish whether it is measured, expected or self-reported. Third, put the uncertainty on the same line as the number. These change the strength of the same figure. If you receive numbers, start by replacing "how many?" with "what was counted?" as your first question.

This analysis has limits too. The three articles span different domains, and what they share is a single point: disclosure of the basis decides a number's strength. It is an induction from three cases and would be revised by one counterexample. If there is a domain where undisclosed numbers keep being cited more widely, the principle weakens from "disclosure is the unit of trust" to "disclosure is the unit of trust for some readers only."
