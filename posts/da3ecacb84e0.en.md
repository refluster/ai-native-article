a16z wrote about "the day agents become customers," the Nasdaq fell 5.52% the day after the US midterms, and a16z's "Charts of the Week" showed job-loss fears receding while worry moved to whether capex will pay back. The three look unrelated. They are explained by one principle. AI demand is not measured at the points where it settles (purchase, utilization, payback) but one step before them: traffic, share price, and price gaps. Those proxies are what get argued over, and what move capital.

## The first proxy: traffic is not purchase

"The day agents become customers: the entrance to the B2A economy a16z describes" covers a piece published on 11 November 2026 by Olivia Moore and Justine Moore. Its claim is that AI agents are becoming the main users of the internet, and that companies must move from "blocking bots" to "winning bots' purchases."

Two numbers support it. AI-referred traffic to Shopify stores was up 8x year on year (Q1 2026), and agentic-browser traffic was up 8x from August to October. The other support is a precedent: coding agents.

Everything observed here is a count of visits. The conclusion says "win the purchase," but a measurement of purchase itself is not among the article's evidence. Between a visit and a purchase, an agent has to compare, choose, and pay. An 8x multiple shows a crowded entrance, not that those steps work.

Even so, the conclusion asks for a strategic shift. What is measured is the entrance; what is being told to move is the exit. That asymmetry is the first fact.

### What the precedent shows

The choice of coding agents as the precedent deserves a note. In coding, the outcome (whether code is accepted) settles on the user's own screen, so the settlement point is close. A purchase involves external settlement points: payment, returns, trust. The domain where the precedent worked is one where the settlement point is at hand.

## The second proxy: the share price reflected permitting, not demand

According to "The day the election set off fears of an AI-infrastructure slowdown: the Nasdaq's biggest drop in 19 months," on 5 November 2026, right after Democrats took control of Congress in the US midterms, the Nasdaq Composite closed down 5.52% at 24,315.00. It was the largest drop in 19 months, more than 10% below its recent high, and into correction territory. Reuters attributed the selling to fears of tighter regulation of AI data-center construction.

That day, AI usage, contract value, and utilization did not change. What changed was the outlook for a supply-side condition: whether data centers can be built. The market still priced it as a slowdown in AI infrastructure within a day.

A share price is not a measure taken after demand has settled. It folds expected demand and the permits needed to serve it into one number. When the demand-side settlement point (who paid how much, for what) is invisible, price reacts hard to the variable that is visible, which is the direction of regulation. A 5.52% move indicates not that demand was lost, but how large the update was when only the outlook for constraints could be revised and demand could not be measured.

## The third proxy: the rack spread is demand's shadow

"Job fears recede, the rack spread remains" covers a "Charts of the Week" published by Moses Sternstein on 30 October 2026. Fear of AI-driven job loss has retreated from its May peak, and postings are up, mainly for technical roles. Market anxiety has moved to whether capex can be recovered.

The evidence cited for that is the divergence between token prices and GPU rental rates, the so-called rack spread. The article itself argues that the spread rests on a small, skewed sample and cannot be read as a definitive demand signal.

So for the final question, payback, what is observed is the difference between two prices. A price gap shows where supply's pricing sits relative to demand, not whether demand exists. Utilization and payment, which are demand itself, do not appear in this chart.

### What the shift in worry says

One more point stands out. In the same market, job fear receded and payback fear moved forward. The worry that "AI works too much" faded and the worry that "AI may not earn" grew. The two worries point in opposite directions, yet both rest on proxies: the movement of job postings and of a price gap. The market's story turns to match whichever indicator is visible at the time.

## The common principle: when settlement points go unmeasured, capital reacts to constraints

Line up the three facts. First, the B2A argument derives an exit strategy, winning purchases, from an entrance measurement, 8x traffic. Second, the Nasdaq's 5.52% fall moved on a supply-side constraint, the outlook for regulating data-center construction, not on any settled demand figure. Third, the rack spread, a price gap from a small, skewed sample, stands in for the final question of payback.

These facts suggest the following. The AI-demand debate is conducted not at the settlement points of purchase, utilization, and payback, but at observation points one step short of them. And those points do not mirror demand directly; they mirror constraints: regulation, supply-side pricing, a crowded entrance. While settlement points go unmeasured, capital moves on what can be measured, which means on constraints.

This principle can be refuted. If a settlement-point measure, such as the value of agent-driven purchases, were published, grew at the same multiple as traffic, and the market reacted to it more strongly than to regulatory news, the principle would weaken. If multiples keep updating while purchase value never appears, it would strengthen.

The three cases can be ordered by distance from the settlement point. For coding agents, the settlement point is in the user's hands and the gap to the proxy is small. For purchases, it lies beyond payment and the gap is larger. For data-center investment, it lies in payback years away, and the gap is largest. The larger the gap, the more easily price swings on constraint news.

## What follows

Three changes follow from this principle. They are inferences from the three L2 articles, and the probabilities are the author's judgment.

First, the next large market shock is more likely to be triggered by constraint news, such as regulation or financing, than by settled demand figures. I put it at about 65%. The grounds: the 5 November fall happened with no change in demand, and market worry has moved to the supply-side question of payback.

Second, a platform may begin to publish settlement-point metrics, such as the value of agent-driven purchases or the completion rate of agent transactions. The likelihood is moderate, close to even. Since 8x traffic calls for a strategic shift, the next question is conversion to purchase, and the platforms themselves are the ones able to measure it. But disclosure depends on the interests of whoever holds the data, so I cannot put the probability high.

Third, the rack-spread debate will not settle until the sample grows, because the article itself says the sample is small and skewed. Until then the spread works neither as evidence for demand nor against it, and serves only as a mirror of each commentator's position.

The practical takeaway for readers comes down to one step. When reading any claim about AI demand, first check what the number measures: the entrance (traffic), a price (share price or price gap), or the settlement point (purchase, utilization, payback). A claim built only on entrance and price numbers can reverse the moment a settlement-point figure appears. The same check applies to your own decisions. If you aim to win purchases, decide first to measure not visits but the number of times an agent chose and paid.
