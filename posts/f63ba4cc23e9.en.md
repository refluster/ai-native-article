## Executive Summary

McDonald's faces a proposed nationwide class action in federal court in Chicago alleging it violated U.S. antitrust law by coordinating menu prices across corporate-owned and franchised restaurants through an AI-powered pricing system. The dispute turns on whether pricing recommendations generated centrally from nonpublic data effectively align what are supposed to be independent price decisions. The company contests the claim, saying "AI does not set the price of a Big Mac or any other menu item."

## What the suit alleges

According to Claims Journal, as cited by Quartz, the plaintiff is an Illinois resident seeking to represent a class of potentially millions of McDonald's customers in the U.S. The complaint says McDonald's worked with independent franchisees to fix prices through algorithms built on nonpublic data, and that the arrangement dates to 2019.

The suit was filed days after Reuters published an investigation into the pricing system. Plaintiff's lawyer Lark Turner said the company is "leveraging its troves of data and its franchised system to nickel-and-dime consumers down to the last French fry."

## The system at issue

Per PYMNTS, the system is a machine-learning tool that analyzes transaction data from McDonald's roughly 14,000 U.S. locations and generates pricing recommendations for individual products. The Reuters report, as described by PYMNTS, says it accounts for regional buying patterns and assessments of consumer price sensitivity across markets.

The plaintiffs' theory: locations that would otherwise compete on price instead receive guidance routed through a centralized system fed by nonpublic data, which nudges franchisees that might set their own prices toward common figures and undermines head-to-head rivalry.

## McDonald's response

McDonald's called the allegations speculative and uninformed. It says three things:

- Franchisees independently determine the prices at their restaurants.
- They are not required to accept any pricing recommendation.
- Relying on data analytics and pricing software is common across the business world and not unique to McDonald's.
## The price gap cited

PYMNTS pointed to Reuters data showing a Big Mac at $5.69 in one corporate location in Fresno, California and $6.89 at another about two miles away. Reuters said it could not establish whether the pricing technology caused the difference. Read carefully, the gap shows that prices vary between nearby locations; it is not evidence of the price convergence the suit alleges.

## Context and implications

Claims Journal notes that in recent years U.S. plaintiffs have brought a surge of class actions accusing companies of using algorithms or AI to fix prices on hotel rooms, apartment rentals and other goods. This suit joins that line.

Those claims have not been proven, and filing a lawsuit does not establish that McDonald's or its franchisees violated antitrust law. What is established so far: the suit was filed, the company is contesting it fully, and the question at its center is whether the output of a central system that only recommends can be treated as coordinating otherwise independent price decisions.

**Conclusion.** The legal exposure of an AI pricing tool sits less in model accuracy than in operating design: how far independent operators follow a central output built from pooled nonpublic data. McDonald's stress on the absence of any obligation to accept recommendations shows that this design is the heart of the dispute. Resolving it awaits the court.
