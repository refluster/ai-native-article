## Executive Summary

In "State of Markets II" (1H'26, 100+ charts), a16z's David George argues that tech has become the "everything cycle", contributing about 76% of the S&P 500's total earnings growth in 2026 (as of late August). The theme has rotated from bits to atoms, and demand for compute is still outpacing supply. Yet AI adoption is broad but shallow: nearly 30% of S&P 500 companies report some "quantifiable impact" of AI, while only about 2% report any tracked metric.

## Evidence: tech is now the earnings growth story

- The source is a16z's "State of Markets II", subtitled "100+ Charts On The State of Markets (1H'26)", by David George, dated September 30, 2026.
- Tech took off in capital markets after the GFC as a high-growth, capital-light alternative, with large embedded operating leverage thanks to software's near-zero marginal cost.
- Since 2023, tech "is the earnings growth story", contributing about 76% of the S&P 500's total earnings growth in 2026 (as of late August).
- In the author's framing, durable goods such as houses, dishwashers and cars once defined the cycle; tech has taken their place. "Tech is the everything cycle, now."
## Evidence: from bits to atoms

- The theme of the year (and beyond) is the rotation from bits to atoms. Software dominated the previous cycle; hardware is now the star.
- The AI buildout has surged demand for traditionally sleepy, cyclical, capital-intensive industries such as semiconductors, power and networking. That demand has been funded largely by the historically massive profits of the world's largest tech companies, functionally converting hyperscaler free cash flow into semiconductor free cash flow, and increasingly by debt.
- Beyond AI, the piece cites global infrastructure needs measured in trillions, rising defense spending, grids grappling with the electrification of everything, reshored manufacturing, and robots and robotaxis inbound.
- Both public and private equity are funding compute, memory, power, robotics, manufacturing and defense with an intensity not seen in decades "(if not longer)".
## Evidence: GPU obsolescence and shallow adoption

- Demand for compute is still outpacing supply. A prominent bear argued that GPUs become obsolete in 3-4 years; the piece answers that A100s are still useful, as rental prices show.
- Rental rates, and thereby residual values, are supposed to fall over time, but that is not what has happened. Pricing for the latest chips is climbing and older chips hold their prices; even the A100 is pricing at or above where it began the year.
- The author says neither compute nor model advances have been zero-sum: better, cheaper intelligence accrues value to the whole ecosystem, with older chips and models retaining substantial value.
- At the same time, adoption is immature. Nearly 30% of S&P 500 companies report some "quantifiable impact" of AI, but only about 2% report any tracked metric. For agentic use cases, only a tiny share of the user base is deploying agents at scale.
- On the consumer side, as of April barely about 2% of US households were paying for an AI service. The number is higher now and growing, but still tiny in the big scheme of things.
## Evidence: "SaaS-Prove-It", not "-Pocalypse"

- The year began with declarations that software was dead and the "SaaSpocalypse" was nigh. The reality was more nuanced: a more discerning sell-off, with AI (or the threat of AI) a factor but not the only one.
- Since the end of ZIRP, companies have traded growth for profitability. In 2022 the market was full of high-growth, mostly unprofitable software businesses; by 2026 about 75% are profitable, but only about 30% are growing 20% or more.
- Fast-growing companies still trade at multiples in line with historical averages (though below ZIRP peaks), but there are fewer of them, so the sector as a whole was repriced.
- The author's verdict: no apocalypse for software, but "definitely a 'prove it'".
## Where a16z thinks this goes

- AI will expand the surface area of demand: more mature adoption across enterprise and consumer, and new frontiers in robotics, biotech, health and AD.
- The technology is improving at an exponential pace, and the authors are "pretty confident" this cycle will not be like any previous one.
## Conclusion: what to take from it

The following is our interpretation of the source's facts.

- The source's numbers describe two layers at once. At the market level, tech delivers about 76% of earnings growth and GPUs run hot. At the company level, only about 2% of firms track an AI metric. Capital has moved first; measurement has not caught up.
- "SaaS-Prove-It" and "broad but shallow" adoption converge on one demand: both software and AI adoption now have to show growth or impact in numbers.
- Much of the source's case rests on 100+ charts; what this article can check is limited to the figures and claims stated in the text. a16z is also an investor, and its bullish framing should be read with that in mind.
