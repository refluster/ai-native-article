Accelerating CTO departures. A paradox where using more AI leaves people more exhausted, not less. A $45 wage premium at data centers and a 32.5x jump in AI agent token consumption. Three seemingly unrelated observations — and they turn out to share a single underlying principle. What AI is eliminating is "execution." What it can't eliminate, and what the market is suddenly bidding up, is "judgment." The problem is that the reward for judgment hasn't been redistributed as fast as judgment itself has become scarce.

## The individual's burden: what "AI fatigue" actually reveals

An analysis titled "What AI cut was the work, what it added was the judgment" tackles a paradox: using AI should shorten the time a task takes, yet the day still ends in pure exhaustion. Its conclusion is blunt — AI automated the "work," not the "judgment." Five burdens — supervision, decision-making, verification, handling the incomplete, and updating — sit entirely outside what AI automates, and land, intact, on the human. Tellingly, the prescribed fix isn't "use AI less" but "redesign judgment itself." Work-hours fall, but the total volume of judgment does not; if anything, the density of decisions packed into each remaining hour rises. Fatigue, on this reading, isn't a function of hours worked but of how many decisions get cycled through per hour. It looks, at first glance, like a problem of individual workload allocation — but it shares a structure with the next two observations that is hard to ignore.

## The executive's burden: what accelerating CTO exits reveal about capital structure

Testimony gathered around "Why is CTO attrition accelerating in the AI era" (citing a Pragmatic Engineer piece, an X post with 196K impressions) shows the same burden concentrating at the executive layer. A CTO is handed two demands that can be operationally contradictory — "go AI-native" and "cut headcount 20–50%" — simultaneously, and must make that call inside a capital structure where preferred shares erode common-share value ahead of the executive team's own stake. Leaner engineering teams eliminate the very layer — middle management — that used to distribute judgment; what remains concentrates on the CTO alone. One respondent, who left their CTO role and went independent just last month, corroborated the pattern as a lived case, not an abstraction. The sharpest irony: a "Member of Technical Staff" doing hands-on technical judgment at an AI lab is now better compensated than a startup CTO. Sitting at the top of the org chart and capturing the reward for judgment are no longer the same thing. A compensation design built for an era when title tracked the weight of judgment is still in place in an era where the weight of judgment has come unmoored from title — and that mismatch, not a lack of capability or will, is plausibly why CTOs are walking.

## Capital's pricing of judgment: what data-center wages and token burn reveal about the real economy

Numbers from a16z's Moses Sternstein, in the August 21, 2026 "Charts of the Week," show this repricing of judgment reaching capital-market indicators directly. Data-center jobs command wage premiums of up to 64%; the top decile of firms by token output is running 32.5x the industry's typical level; meanwhile legacy automation tools are visibly losing traffic to newer, agentic players like Gumloop. This is evidence that the AI boom has moved past being an ETF-flow narrative and into wages and token consumption — real-economy indicators. The 32.5x token figure means AI isn't merely substituting for human work; the compute underwriting judgment itself is scaling exponentially — both the compute spent per unit of judgment and the wage paid per unit of judgment are spiking together. The market is redirecting wages and capital not toward plain automation tools, but toward the layer that actually wields AI as a lever for judgment — specialized data-center talent, and the operators running high-frequency, judgment-supporting agent workloads. But that redirection is still concentrated in a narrow slice of firms and individuals who can operate AI at that level; it has not yet flowed back to the CTOs and knowledge workers absorbing the added burden of judgment itself.

## The unifying principle: judgment has grown scarce faster than its reward has redistributed

Lay the three observations side by side and one inference follows. AI is rapidly driving down the cost of execution, while judgment itself resists automation and grows relatively scarcer. These facts suggest that AI is not shrinking the total pool of value so much as relocating *where* that value sits — from execution to judgment. Individual exhaustion, CTO attrition, and the surge in data-center wages and token burn are different cross-sections of the same shift. The catch is that the shift unfolds in two stages. In stage one, the market price of judgment spikes — MTS roles out-earn CTOs, data-center jobs draw premiums up to 64%, top-decile firms burn 32.5x the tokens — all early evidence that pricing is being bid up for whoever wields judgment. In stage two, the people actually carrying that judgment should see their compensation and authority redesigned to match — but existing organizational structures, capital structures, and pay systems are still built for the execution era. Preferred stock eating common value first, work designs that dump supervision, verification, and updating entirely onto the individual, and lean-team org charts that strip out the middle-management layer that used to distribute judgment — all of these are leftovers from an era when execution, not judgment, was the scarce thing. The market has begun pricing scarce judgment at the capital level; the redistribution to the individuals and executives who actually exercise that judgment is lagging behind. That lag between "pricing" and "redistribution" is the single principle running through all three observations.

## What this predicts, and what it implies

If this principle holds, three things should become observable over the next one to two years. First, departures should keep accelerating among people carrying an outsized judgment burden — startup CTOs, leads of lean engineering teams — and pay structures should shift toward something closer to the MTS model: a higher cash-to-equity ratio, tied to the volume of judgment exercised rather than to title. Second, the response to AI fatigue should shift, at the organizational level, from "use AI less" to "redesign judgment itself" — new tools and roles emerging to share the burden of supervision, verification, and updating (a dedicated judgment-review function, mechanisms for auto-summarizing and handing off judgment). Third, the wage premiums currently concentrated in data-center construction and infrastructure roles should start extending to roles that verify and supervise judgment itself, not just roles that build the infrastructure underneath it. If the principle is wrong, attrition and fatigue should settle back down even without any of these structural rewrites — pay structures staying as they are while the numbers quietly normalize would falsify the hypothesis. The practical takeaway is straightforward: the first thing an organization adopting AI should redesign isn't the scope of automation — it's the contract governing how the burden and reward of judgment get distributed. Organizations that skip that redesign will quietly bleed the people who carry judgment. Organizations that rewrite the contract first will be the ones judgment-bearing talent chooses, once judgment becomes the scarce resource everywhere else already treats it as.
