What AI is really replacing is not human work, but the cost of coordination that connects people to one another. Applied Intuition argues that the bottleneck in physical AI is the integration system; one experiment used AI to guide deliberation among 277 people and narrow 94 proposals down to 3; Netflix prioritizes “systems thinking” over narrow specialization in hiring. These three examples come from different fields and involve different actors, but in fact they can all be explained by a single principle. In every case, individual capability is left unchanged while only the seams between people are redesigned.

## How to Read the Fact That It Became 20 Times Faster Without Raising Capability

In “The Next AI Moat Isn’t the Model,” Peter Ludwig framed deployed physical AI as the product of two variables: “model capability” × the throughput of the engineering systems surrounding the model. His core point was the asymmetry that, while the industry is pouring everything into the first variable, the second remains stuck at a decade-old level premised on quarterly releases and integration teams of 100 people.

What deserves careful attention here is the actual content of what he names as that second variable. Quarterly releases and integration teams of 100 are not measures of intelligence. Both describe the structure of handoffs itself: how many people touch a change, and how many times it has to wait, before it reaches production. The reported observation that development cycles became roughly 20 times faster with Dana, the agent platform the company built for internal use, is also separated from any claim that the model became smarter. What got faster was not capability, but the way capability is delivered.

That said, this 20x figure is the only quantitative support the article offers, and it is also a self-reported internal case. There is no need to accept the magnitude of the number at face value. What matters is what the number is measuring—not the time required for a unit task, but the cycle from integration to deployment.

## Without Making 277 People Smarter, It Reduced 94 Proposals to 3

The experiment recorded in “277 People Answer the Question of 250 Years of Nationhood” shows this reading in even purer form. On July 4, 2026, Unanimous AI had 277 randomly selected Americans engage in a 20-minute online deliberation, dividing participants into parallel groups of 4 to 5 people and using “Thinkscape,” in which a swarm of AI agents coordinates those groups in real time, to converge 94 initial proposals into 3 final proposals (No. 1 = the internet, No. 2 = medical advances, No. 3 = the spread of democracy).

In this experiment, not a single participant became smarter. No one was trained, and no knowledge was supplied by AI. What was removed was the scale constraint that real-time deliberation only works with groups of around 4 to 5 people—in other words, the limit of coordination. When the system’s creator, Louis Rosenberg, said the result was “100% human intelligence” and that AI’s role lies not in replacing humans but in connecting them, that is usually read as a cautious qualification. But in the reading advanced here, it is the strongest possible claim. If the supply of intelligence was zero and the outcome still changed, then what produced the outcome was connection itself.

## The “Systems Thinker” Is the Internalization of Coordination Costs

The third case is the same movement seen from the organizational side. In “Why Netflix Hires ‘Systems Thinkers’ Over ‘Experts,’” CPTO Elizabeth Stone identifies systems thinking as the hiring skill she values most in the age of AI, and places side by side a preference for cross-domain integration over narrow specialization, AI literacy as a company-wide baseline, and operationalizing excellence as a system. The article ties all of this together with a single line: the cheaper generation becomes, the rarer the judgment required to wire things together becomes.

To make cross-domain integration a hiring criterion is to take the connections that had previously been handled by the org chart as handoffs between specialists and place them inside one person’s head. The question is no longer how many specialists to assemble, but how many domains one person can span. Coordination stops being an external procedure and is internalized within the individual. The organizational answer to cheaper coordination was neither to add people nor to cut them, but to reduce the number of seams per decision.

The article’s other two points are directed the same way. Making AI literacy a company-wide baseline is a measure to avoid creating a translation step between departments that have literacy and departments that do not, and operationalizing excellence as a system is a measure to avoid making quality depend on handoffs in the form of individual visual inspection. All four points line up not as measures to increase the total amount of capability, but as measures to strip away the procedures wedged between one capability and another. It is also worth noting that only this third example relies on the classical means of human hiring. Even without building an agent platform, the same principle can be implemented through organizational design.

## The Shared Principle: What Got Cheaper Was Not Production but Coordination

Taken together, these three facts suggest that the main battlefield of AI-driven change lies not in production but in coordination. In physical AI, what was redesigned was the choreography of integration and deployment; in the 277-person deliberation, it was the barrier imposed by group size; in Netflix’s hiring, it was the handoff burden that comes with specialization. The common thread is that outcomes moved simply because the number of handoffs was reduced while the capability variable remained fixed.

Viewed through this principle, Ludwig’s multiplicative formula takes on a different meaning. The second variable can be read not as “engineering capability,” but as an indicator of distance: how many hands a single decision must pass through before it reaches production. The same yardstick applies to the other two cases. In the deliberation experiment, the unit of decision-making expanded from 4 to 5 people to 277 people, and at Netflix, the number of domains one person can span became part of the hiring metric. In all three cases, the number of handoffs per unit was reduced.

Accordingly, the observable form of the effect looks like this: the impact of AI adoption appears not as faster individual tasks, but as larger units of decision-making. Shorter task time is a byproduct; the main event is the disappearance of seams. This claim has a clear falsification condition: if, in organizations that have seriously adopted AI, productivity improves sustainably while release intervals, approval stages, and handoff counts remain completely unchanged, then this principle is wrong. In all three cases discussed here, the seams are what move first.

## Forecasts and Implications

First, what can be said with high confidence is this: organizations that measure the effects of AI investment in terms of time saved on individual tasks will continue to underestimate those effects. The reason is that Applied Intuition’s 20x figure measured not the speed of unit work, but the cycle from integration to deployment. Task-level measurement excludes the very place where the effect appears—between one process and the next.

Second, with moderate confidence, we can predict that process structure will shift before headcount structure does. What gets reduced is not the number of people, but the number of approval stages, handoffs, and waits for integration. The “quarterly releases” and “integration teams of 100 people” that Ludwig names are exactly the kinds of things likely to break down in that order. Conversely, if cases where the number of process steps stays fixed while only headcount declines become the dominant pattern, then by the falsification condition above, the principle argued for here should be rejected.

Third, the axes of hiring and evaluation will multiply. This does not mean the value of specialization disappears, but as Netflix suggests, “the number of domains one person can span” will become a yardstick alongside it. In an environment where coordination costs have fallen, a design that gathers specialists and connects them is more likely to lose out to a design that gathers individuals who have internalized those connections.

The practical implication can be narrowed to one thing. The metrics your organization should count are the number of hands a single change passes through before it reaches production, and the number of waits along the way. If tools have made individual tasks faster but those numbers are the same as last year, then what you bought was capability alone—you did not buy coordination. What the 277-person experiment showed was the reverse side of that same fact: even if you add no capability at all and change only coordination, outcomes still move.
