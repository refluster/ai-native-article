## Executive Summary

On Monday, October 26, Anthropic filed paperwork for what could be the biggest initial public offering ever. According to the 335-page filing, first-half 2026 revenue topped $16 billion, 14 times the same period a year earlier, while the net loss reached $80.7 billion, of which $72.4 billion was a one-time accounting adjustment tied to financing agreements. The amount raised, final valuation and offering price are all still undetermined; the company plans to list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker "ANT."

## What was filed, and how big it is

According to the Associated Press (Barbara Ortutay and Bernard Condon, updated October 26, 2026), San Francisco-based Anthropic filed its IPO paperwork on Monday. The filing sets up a Wall Street debut next month that could eclipse the record $86 billion raised by SpaceX in its June IPO. AP reports that an Anthropic IPO would also put the company ahead of OpenAI in going public.

- Venue and ticker: Nasdaq Global Select Market, symbol "ANT" (per the company's regulatory filing)
- Amount raised: as much as $100 billion according to reports; the final figure remains uncertain
- Valuation: from $965 billion, set by private investors earlier this year, to a reported $1.5 trillion or possibly $2 trillion. At $1.5 trillion, Anthropic would rank among the world's most valuable public companies alongside Tesla. The final valuation has not been established
- Use of proceeds: in the filing the company said it could use some of the money to acquire other businesses and technology and to invest in infrastructure to take on rivals
## Results: rapid growth, enormous losses

The filing runs 335 pages. The main figures AP reports:

- Revenue: more than $16 billion in the first half of 2026, 14 times the same period last year
- Net loss: $80.7 billion in the first half of 2026 (versus $13 billion a year earlier), including a one-time $72.4 billion charge for an accounting adjustment related to financing agreements
- Profitability: like many other tech companies going public, the maker of the Claude chatbot has yet to turn a profit
AP notes that Anthropic, OpenAI and SpaceX's AI division are all losing more money than they make because of the huge costs of the resources needed to build AI, which is fueling concerns of a bubble.

## Capital needs and market conditions

The article points to the need for cash as a possible reason Anthropic is rushing to the public markets this year rather than waiting until next year, as OpenAI plans. It adds the possibility that demand for AI shares could drop or even collapse, investors' jitters about the sector's massive financing needs, and several tech companies having pulled IPO plans recently.

David Hsu, a management professor at the University of Pennsylvania, sees the filing more as a move to diversify the company's financial base. "Obviously, they feel great, or at least good enough, on the safety aspects relative to OpenAI, and so that puts them in a position to really stay on track now to go public (first)," Hsu said.

## Governance and what it means for the founders

The filing contains some unusual provisions:

- Voting control: Anthropic's seven co-founders, including Dario Amodei and his sister Daniela (the company's president), receive 50.1% of shareholder votes through a special class of shares
- Long-Term Benefit Trust: independent, financially disinterested members hold the power to appoint directors, designed to ensure the company does not put financial interests ahead of public safety
- Founder stakes: the filing does not state ownership directly, but other disclosed figures suggest each of the seven will end up with 1.4% of the company, a windfall of as much as $28 billion each should the company go public at the top expected value. Per Forbes, Dario (43) and Daniela (39) are already worth $15.5 billion each
## What the risk factors reveal

The "risk factors" section, required of every public company, runs nearly 100 pages here, and AP calls it riveting reading.

- Quotidian risks: failing to retain key staff or to navigate regulatory roadblocks
- Severe risks: "malicious actors" using AI models to build chemical weapons, authoritarian rulers using the technology to crack down on their citizens, and the possibility that its AI models could go rogue and "pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity"
The article also notes that the technology threatens major job disruption by reducing the need for human labor, and that a string of hacking incidents by rogue AI agents has raised fears the technology will slip free of human control.

## Calls for a slowdown, and the clash with Washington

In September, Amodei said the AI industry should slow its fast-moving development to give safety measures time to catch up. Without that, he warned, AI could be capable of letting loose a swarm of agents that could take over the entire internet within six to 12 months. He offered a plan on his website to increase checks on the industry, noting that Anthropic already undertakes part of it on its own; other steps, he said, would require coordination across the industry and with governments around the world, including authoritarian ones.

The relationship with the U.S. government is part of the backdrop:

- February: the Trump administration accused Anthropic of endangering national security and designated it a supply chain risk
- March: Anthropic sued the Pentagon over what it called an "unlawful campaign of retaliation" for its refusal to allow unrestricted military use of its technology
- Rulings: a California judge ruled in Anthropic's favor in the first case, while a Washington, D.C. judge sided with the government in a second, narrower case over a different rule the Pentagon is using to try to declare Anthropic a supply chain risk. Both sides are appealing
- OpenAI made its own deal to work with the Pentagon hours after the government punished Anthropic
## Assessment: what is evidenced, what is open

**Evidenced (facts the source reports from the filing):** first-half revenue above $16 billion; a net loss of $80.7 billion, of which $72.4 billion is a one-time accounting adjustment; seven co-founders holding 50.1% of votes; a Long-Term Benefit Trust with power to appoint directors; the Nasdaq listing and "ANT" ticker.

**Open (what the source itself marks as unestablished):** the amount raised, the final valuation and the offering price. The $1.5–2 trillion valuation and the up-to-$100 billion raise are "according to reports," not figures fixed in the filing. The 1.4% founder stakes are AP's inference from other disclosed figures, not a number the filing states.

**Implication:** even with revenue growing 14-fold year over year, the cost of building AI still exceeds what it earns. The filing is the first large test of whether AI companies can keep funding enormous compute investment with public-market capital. At the same time, concentrated voting power and the Long-Term Benefit Trust make what new investors are buying more complex than in a typical listing. The source itself suggests the plan could change if the market sours.
