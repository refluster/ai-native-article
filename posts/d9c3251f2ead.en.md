## Executive Summary

On July 2, 2026, The Information reported that Anthropic is in discussions with Samsung about developing a new custom AI chip. The move follows OpenAI’s announcement roughly a week earlier of its inference processor “Jalapeño,” developed in partnership with Broadcom, and is part of a broader shift by major AI companies toward in-house silicon amid semiconductor shortages and efforts to reduce reliance on Nvidia. At the same time, Anthropic itself has said that “a diversified hardware stack including chips from Google, Amazon and Nvidia will remain key to our compute strategy,” meaning the talks are best understood not as a replacement for its current setup but as the addition of another option.

## Facts: The Core of the Reporting and the Timeline

First, here is a chronological整理 of the facts that can be confirmed. The initial report in this case came from The Information, but reports that Anthropic was considering its own chips had been surfacing intermittently even before that.

- **April 2026**: Reuters reported that Anthropic was considering manufacturing its own AI chips. The current report about talks with Samsung serves as a follow-up indicating that the April speculation has advanced into discussions with a specific counterpart.
- **June 24, 2026**: OpenAI announced the custom inference processor “Jalapeño” in partnership with Broadcom. The chip is said to deliver superior “performance-per-watt” compared with competing chips.
- **July 2, 2026 (Thursday)**: The Information reported on talks between Anthropic and Samsung over a custom AI chip. This is the primary source covered in this briefing.
What matters here is that the report describes a “decision” neither made nor finalized, but talks still at the “discussions” stage. According to the article, Anthropic has **not yet decided the chip’s use case, how it would be integrated into servers, or its power specifications**. In other words, the design essentials—such as whether it is intended for training or inference, and where exactly it would fit into existing infrastructure—still cannot be determined from the public information available.

## Facts: The Custom Silicon Map Across Companies

The article places this development not as an isolated Anthropic move, but within a broader landscape in which major companies are all pursuing custom silicon in parallel. Limiting the map strictly to facts that appear in the reporting, the positioning looks like this:

- **Google**: Offers its own custom TPUs.
- **Amazon**: Offers its own custom silicon, “Trainium.”
- **OpenAI**: Partnered with Broadcom to announce the custom processor “Jalapeño,” explicitly positioned for inference.
- **Samsung**: Has partnered with Nvidia and is building an AI chip factory in South Korea. It is also in talks with Google on chip manufacturing.
- **Anthropic**: Is now in talks with Samsung. Officially, it has stated that “a diversified hardware stack including chips from Google, Amazon and Nvidia will remain key to our compute strategy.”
What this map makes clear is that Samsung is not acting as a manufacturing partner tied to a single customer. Rather, it is simultaneously engaging with multiple AI-related players—Nvidia, Google, and Anthropic at the discussion stage. On the manufacturing side, foundry/factory capacity is becoming the connective node linking the chip designs of major AI companies.

## Implications: Why In-House Chips, and Why Now?

From here, this briefing turns to implications that can reasonably be drawn from the facts above, while keeping them distinct from the facts themselves.

The article points to two core drivers. One is **chip shortages**; the other is **reducing dependence on Nvidia**. Together, these straightforwardly explain the problem custom silicon is meant to solve. As long as GPU procurement is bottlenecked by the supply plans of a single outside vendor, the ceiling on an AI company’s compute capacity remains subordinate to that one company’s production and allocation decisions. In-house chips could become a way to partially break that dependency.

The timing is also suggestive. Only about a week separates OpenAI’s Jalapeño announcement on June 24 and the Anthropic–Samsung report on July 2. That temporal proximity itself supports the view that major players are moving toward custom silicon at nearly the same moment, suggesting this is not a one-off judgment by individual firms but a response to shared industry-wide pressure. At the same time, the article also shows that the information needed to judge whether Anthropic can actually realize those benefits—namely its intended use and specifications—is still missing. At this stage, strong motivation and uncertain implementation coexist.

## Implications: Anthropic’s Trade-Offs and Open Questions

The key point in Anthropic’s official comment is that the emphasis is not on “leaving Nvidia,” but on **“diversified”** infrastructure. The talks with Samsung are most faithfully read, based on the company’s public wording, not as an effort to replace its existing Google, Amazon, and Nvidia setup, but as an attempt to add one more option. Rather than fully migrating to a custom chip, the posture implied by that single word is one of spreading risk by broadening both suppliers and design options.

Until the three unresolved points—use case, server integration, and power specifications—are clarified, there is no way to determine where this chip would sit within the existing mix of TPUs, Trainium, and GPUs. In contrast to OpenAI, which explicitly positioned Jalapeño for inference, Anthropic’s design intent cannot yet be pinned down from the currently available public information. For that reason, summarizing this as “Anthropic is building an inference chip” goes beyond what the reporting supports. More precisely, the limit of what is known right now is: “Anthropic is in talks with Samsung about a custom chip whose use case has not yet been determined.”

Taken together with Samsung’s broader context—its partnership with Nvidia, its factory in South Korea, and its manufacturing talks with Google—a two-sided motivation comes into view. For Anthropic, the appeal is access to manufacturing capacity; for Samsung, it is diversification of its customer base. The next things to watch are whether the talks advance into concrete partnership terms, and whether Anthropic discloses either the chip’s use case or its specifications. If those two points begin to move, this story will shift stages—from observation to strategy.
