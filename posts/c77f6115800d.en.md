Execution is being parallelized and made cheaper, and value is moving away from individual acts of execution toward the layer above that organizes them. Three seemingly unrelated facts illustrate this. There is the “America at 250 deliberation experiment,” in which 277 people deliberated for 20 minutes on the 250th anniversary of the founding of the United States; the “Anthropic Economic Index June 2026 edition,” which analyzed Claude usage logs; and the “quant swarm design theory,” in which 6 AI agents run quantitative research. Their subject matter and scale are different. But in fact, all three can be explained by the same principle: when execution is distributed across many actors and parallelized, the scarce role becomes the one that stands in the “orchestration layer.”

## The orchestrating party can be either AI or human

What these three facts share is a structure in which many independent execution units run in parallel and are coordinated from a layer above. What matters is that who does the orchestrating is not fixed.

In the “America at 250 deliberation experiment,” the orchestrating party was AI. Unanimous AI’s “Thinkscape” split 277 people into parallel groups of 4 to 5, and a swarm of AI agents organized the discussion in real time, converging 94 initial proposals into the final 3 proposals (1st = the internet, 2nd = medical advances, 3rd = the spread of democracy). Its designer, Louis Rosenberg, argues that this output was “100% human intelligence,” and that the role of AI is not to replace humans but to connect them. In other words, AI did not step into execution itself (thinking); it handled only the orchestration layer.

In the “quant swarm design theory,” the orchestrating party flips, and becomes human. Roan, a backend developer, assigns each of the 6 stages of quant alpha research—from reading arXiv papers to factor decomposition—to a dedicated agent, and runs them in parallel 24 hours a day. The core of this approach is a role shift: “humans stop acting as the pipeline that validates hypotheses one by one, and move to the side that designs the loop.” Here, the executors are a swarm of agents, and the human has moved up into the orchestration layer.

The actors are opposites—AI in one case, humans in the other—but the structure is the same. Execution is distributed across many actors, and value is concentrated at the single point that organizes them.

## The higher you stand in the orchestration layer, the more optimistic you become about the future

The “Anthropic Economic Index June 2026 edition” shows in numbers how this structure affects subjective perception. In that edition, “Cadences” connected Claude usage logs to a self-reported survey of about 9,700 people, and reported as its main finding the “automation paradox.” Users who delegated a higher share of their work to AI were less pessimistic about the impact on their jobs in the following year—and were, in fact, more optimistic.

Naively, one might expect the opposite. The more you hand over execution, the more anxious you should become that your job will be taken away. But the observations showed the reverse. This paradox becomes easy to explain if we read it as a move into the orchestration layer. Those who delegate execution are not losing themselves as executors; they are raising their position to that of the organizer. That is why they see the future not as a threat, but as an expansion of the pieces they can command.

Another finding in the same edition points in the same direction. People’s self-reported sense of AI capability consistently exceeded the “observed exposure” captured in the usage logs. People feel that “AI can do” more than they have actually touched firsthand. That is exactly the field of vision of someone standing in the orchestration layer. They are sensing capability not by how much work they themselves have done by hand, but by the total volume of execution running in parallel under their direction.

## The rigor of the orchestration layer is rebuilt through “separation”

But moving up into the orchestration layer comes at a cost. The rigor that existed when you personally verified executions one by one cannot simply be preserved as-is. You can no longer inspect each parallelized execution individually.

The “quant swarm design theory” states a clear answer to this problem. It places “maker-checker separation”—splitting the generating role and the verification role into different models—at the core of rigor. Instead of having a human inspect each individual execution, the function of verification itself is rebuilt into the structure.

The same idea appears in the “America at 250 deliberation experiment.” What converged 94 proposals into 3 was not persuasion by individual participants, but the design of a convergence algorithm that organized the parallel groups. Quality does not reside in each individual utterance; it resides in the design of how they are organized.

### A caveat: this is design theory, not measured evidence

That said, much of what the “quant swarm design theory” argues is presented not as measured evidence but as design theory, and the text also doubles as promotion for “Slate,” a tool recommended by Boris Cherny, Head of Claude Code at Anthropic, whose comments it cites. The superiority of the orchestration layer is still in the process of being validated, and this point should be discounted accordingly.

## A shared principle — the parallelization of execution pushes value up one level

Put these three facts side by side, and the following inference emerges. The more execution is distributed across many actors and parallelized, the lower the scarcity of any individual execution, and the more value is concentrated in the layer above that organizes them—these facts suggest exactly that.

Why does this happen? If there is only one execution, the executor and the coordinator cannot be separated, and value resides in the execution itself. But the moment execution becomes 6-way parallel, as in the “quant swarm design theory,” or 277 people in parallel, as in the “America at 250 deliberation experiment,” the bottleneck shifts away from any individual execution and toward the design of orchestration: how to divide work, how to make it converge, and how to verify it. Execution is replicable and interchangeable, but the way it is organized is singular and determines the quality of the whole. That is why scarcity moves to the orchestration layer.

Then why do orchestrators become optimistic (So What)? The automation paradox in the “Anthropic Economic Index” is the subjective image of this value shift. Those who stand in the orchestration layer measure themselves not by what their own hands produce, but by the total volume of execution running in parallel under their direction. That is why their felt sense of capability exceeds observed exposure, and why they see the future not as a threat but as expansion. Optimism is not a matter of personality; it is a function of position.

What matters is that this principle takes a falsifiable form. If the principle were wrong, then users who delegated more should have been more pessimistic, and swarms that only orchestrate should have seen quality collapse in the absence of human verifiers. But the observations showed the opposite in both cases—delegators were more optimistic, and rigor was restored through separation as a design of orchestration. This alignment is what distinguishes the principle from a mere slogan.

## Predictions and implications

First, treatment of the orchestration layer and the execution layer will diverge (high probability). The more execution is parallelized and outsourced, the more the value of any individual execution diminishes, and compensation and discretion will concentrate in the orchestration layer. The optimism captured by the “Anthropic Economic Index” is optimism biased toward those who have succeeded in standing in the orchestration layer; it does not capture the subjective experience of those who remain in the execution layer. This asymmetry is grounded in the same edition’s observation that delegation ratio and optimism were correlated.

Second, competitive advantage will shift to “the design of orchestration” (medium probability). If everyone can run the same swarm of agents in parallel, the differentiator becomes the design of division, convergence, and verification. As with the convergence design in the “America at 250 deliberation experiment” and the maker-checker separation in the “quant swarm design theory,” the way things are organized becomes the asset that is hardest to imitate. That said, because the latter remains design theory rather than measured evidence, the scale of this advantage is not yet certain.

Third, whether the orchestrating party is AI or human does not determine where value resides (medium probability). In the “America at 250 deliberation experiment,” AI organized humans; in the “quant swarm design theory,” humans organized AI. Whoever stands in the orchestration layer, value resides there. The question is not “AI or human?” but “Am I in the execution layer, or in the orchestration layer?”

For readers, the practical implication can be narrowed to one thing. Redraw your own work not as a single execution, but as a loop that can be orchestrated. When execution can be split into parallel units, and convergence and verification can be established as design, only then does value truly move up to the next layer.
