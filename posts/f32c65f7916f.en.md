As intelligence itself has become cheap and abundant, the center of gravity in competition and constraint has shifted to the “outer layers” that operate models, direct them, and supply them—that is the core argument of this article.

Chinese open models overtook U.S. ones by accounting for 41% of downloads on Hugging Face. It has been argued that the more agents you hire, the more human organizational failures get replayed, and that outcomes are decided by “those who give instructions.” Most of the emissions growth at Google and Amazon came from data center construction and chip procurement outside their direct control. These three developments may appear unrelated, but in fact they can be explained by the same principle. The model itself is becoming a commodity, while what remains scarce and decisive is escaping to the upstream and downstream edges around it.

## Models Have Become Commoditized in the Middle of the Stack

### “Who has the smartest model?” is no longer the main battlefield

The figures presented in L2’s “The Main Battleground in AI Competition Has Shifted From the ‘Frontier’ to the ‘Production Floor’” are clear. In spring 2026, Chinese open-weight models accounted for 41% of downloads on Hugging Face, surpassing U.S.-made models. On OpenRouter, the six most-used top models are all open models from Chinese companies, while Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.7 sits in 7th place.

The significance of this is not that “China won.” It is that the axis of competition has shifted from “who has the smartest model” to “which model handles most real-world production use.” Competing for first place on the frontier and being chosen on the production floor are now different games. Models that can be downloaded, whose weights are open, and that can be swapped for one another are becoming interchangeable parts in the middle of the stack. Being state-of-the-art no longer automatically translates into economic value.

## Cheap Intelligence Does Not Work by Itself

### What separates winners from losers is “the one giving instructions”

L2’s “AI Made Humans Cheap—7 Parallels That ‘Tokens and Loops’ Force Onto Agent Management” captures the same shift from the demand side. In “You Just Hired a Million Bad Employees” (July 14, 2026), contributed by George Sivulka to a16z, the assumption that AI replaces labor is turned on its head. For the first time in history, humans have become cheaper than software, while agent waste—pointless tokens and endless loops—simply reenacts the same failures that human organizations have always suffered from.

The core point here, as Sivulka argues, is not technology but management. Even if intelligence becomes cheap and infinitely abundant, that alone does not create value. What determines the outcome is “the one who gives instructions on what to do to both humans and agents.” In other words, the moment model intelligence becomes saturated and inexpensive, value flees from inside the model to the edge of “instruction and operation” that gives it direction. Being able to hire a million units of cheap labor is not an advantage; it is merely the exposure of a management problem.

## Constraints Have Sunk Into the Physical “Supply Layer”

### The cost shows up not in “running the model” but in “building the foundation”

L2’s “The ‘Real Cost’ of AI—Rising Emissions and the Net Zero Wall Reflected in Sustainability Reports from Google and Amazon” shows the same structure from the downstream side. With AI expansion as the backdrop, both companies’ carbon emissions increased: Google by 25% year over year, and Amazon by 16%.

What is decisive is the breakdown of that increase. Most of it came not from Scope 1 and 2, which the companies directly control, but from Scope 3—data center construction and the procurement of GPUs and memory. In other words, the real cost of AI lies not in the electricity used to “run” models but in the physical supply layer required to “build” and “source” the foundation they sit on. And because that layer is not directly controlled by either company, meeting net zero commitments becomes even harder. The constraint has moved away from computation inside the model and into the supply network laid beneath it.

## The Common Principle—Models Have Become a Middle Layer, and Scarcity Has Escaped to Both Ends

These three facts suggest that the model itself has become a “cheap, abundant, and interchangeable middle layer,” while what remains scarce and decisive has shifted to two edges. The upstream edge is “instruction and operation”—who assigns work to agents, and which models get run on the production floor. The downstream edge is “supply and construction”—who builds and procures the physical foundation of data centers and chips. Both value and constraint are seeping out of the center of the model and pooling at the two ends.

Why does this happen? When something becomes cheap and abundant, it ceases to be the rate-limiting factor (the bottleneck). The bottleneck moves to whatever is still scarce. The flood of open models made “smartness” no longer scarce (Source 1), and endlessly abundant agents made “intelligence” no longer scarce (Source 2). That leaves two scarce resources: the management and orchestration needed to direct that intelligence properly, and the construction and procurement needed to make it physically real. The former is the soft edge; the latter is the hard edge. The model is squeezed between them, thinning into a commodity.

This principle is falsifiable. If frontier-model intelligence were still the decisive constraint, then we should observe the following: U.S.-made frontier models would still hold the top position in real-world operations; hiring the very best AI would bring victory regardless of management quality; and AI costs would be concentrated in the electricity used to run models (Scope 2). But all three sources point in the opposite direction. Readers could object to this argument by saying, “If a sufficiently large capability leap arrives, value will reconcentrate in frontier models.” That claim is testable. We need only see whether the next major model leap pulls real-world market share, organizational success, and cost concentration back toward the center of the model. This article is betting that it will not.

## Forecasts and Implications—Those Who Stake Out the Edges Will Win

First, the basis of corporate differentiation will shift from “which model you have” to “how you direct it, where you run it, and what you supply it with” (high confidence). The basis for this is the change in coordinates shown by Source 1. Now that adoption on the production floor has detached from frontier rankings, advantage resides in the quality of orchestration layers and infrastructure procurement. Capital invested in operational design and supply will likely generate better returns than capital spent trying to compete for first place with an in-house model.

Second, the scarce skill will not be “the ability to build models” but “the ability to direct agents” (medium to high confidence). The metaphor in Source 2—“a million bad employees”—maps directly onto talent. If having massive amounts of cheap intelligence merely exposes a management problem, then scarcity will concentrate in the management and orchestration skills that can solve that problem. The center of gravity in engineering will also shift away from model training and toward the design, supervision, and evaluation of agent fleets.

Third, the economics of AI will be bottlenecked by physical supply—construction, chips, and memory (high confidence). As Source 3 shows, the main driver of cost is Scope 3, which moves more slowly than the unit price of compute and is not directly controllable by the parties involved. As a result, net zero commitments will recede, and the floor under AI costs will be determined not by the price of computation but by the speed of physical construction. More than falling GPU prices, what will matter is substations, land, and the semiconductor supply chain.

The practical implication converges on a single point: place both investment and learning not in the middle of the model, but at the edges. As an individual, that means developing “the skill of giving instructions.” As an organization, it means “designing operations” and “securing supply.” The frontier model itself will eventually become an intermediate component that anyone can obtain. The real differentiation will come from what you make that component do, and what you run it on—the two edges outside the model.
