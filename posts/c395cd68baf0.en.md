## Executive Summary

Goldman Sachs Research forecasts that the spread of agentic AI will cause the volume of tokens processed by LLMs to increase 24-fold from 2026 to 2030, reaching 120 quadrillion tokens per month. At the same time, the unit cost of inference is falling at an annual rate of 60–70%, which, according to senior semiconductor and IT services equity analyst Jim Schneider, means hyperscalers are headed not toward runaway spending but toward a “margin inflection.” This article organizes that argument around four dimensions: demand, cost, supply constraints, and asymmetry in adoption.

## Token Consumption Will Increase 24-Fold by 2030 — The Demand-Side Numbers

Schneider defines agentic AI as something that “does not simply respond to a query, but autonomously executes a series of tasks.” A traditional chatbot answers a prompt like “Tell me about this,” while an agent handles a chain of instructions: “Do this, then do that.” That cascading workflow drives token consumption—tokens being the units of text an LLM processes, and therefore effectively units of compute—to an entirely different order of magnitude.

Goldman simulated use cases such as travel, including online trip booking, and inquiries to call centers, and calculated token volumes as follows:

- Token consumption will increase **24-fold** from 2026 to 2030, reaching **120 quadrillion tokens per month**.
- Consumer-side usage alone—including online shopping and smartphone task delegation—will increase **12-fold**.
- The number of daily queries to LLMs will grow at **40% annually (CAGR)**, reaching **11 billion** by 2030.
The scale is so large that Schneider himself remarked that while he does not know “what comes after quadrillion,” it is enormous regardless. The important point is that this demand explosion is arriving at exactly the moment when investors are questioning “the sustainability of the huge capex hyperscalers are putting into chips and data centers.”

## Falling Costs Create a “Margin Inflection”

Even as demand surges, compute costs are falling rapidly. According to Schneider, semiconductor providers are cutting the per-token cost of inference by **60–70% annually**, driven by improvements in chip efficiency and new efficiencies in AI data center architecture. He describes it as a “very, very rapid rate of decline.”

This leads to what Goldman calls a “margin inflection.”

- Semiconductor makers are already operating at **gross margins above 70%**, so that is not the issue.
- The pressure is on the hyperscalers. Most of their free cash flow (FCF) is being absorbed by capex, putting pressure on gross margins.
- But because “costs are falling faster than prices,” Goldman expects a positive inflection in hyperscaler gross margins within the **next 3–12 months**.
As Schneider explains, “Investor concern is the sustainability of capex, but the answer lies in the basic economics. If gross margins rise, operating cash flow rises, and that creates room to keep spending.” That view depends on demand growth and cost declines continuing at the same time.

## Supply Will Not Keep Up — The Time Constraints of Chip Production

That said, in the short term supply will be the bottleneck. Building a new chip fab takes about three years, while use cases are changing much faster than that. As Schneider points out, “A year ago we were not even talking about agents, and now we are.”

- The industry is responding to “the capacity that was needed six months ago,” but the goalposts are moving faster than that.
- As a result, shortages will continue for **at least the next 12 months**.
- Schneider believes “in two years we will probably catch up.”
These supply constraints are a realistic, time-based caveat to the otherwise positive picture of booming demand and falling costs.

## Adoption Will Not Be Uniform — Asymmetry Across Consumers, Enterprises, and Workloads

What distinguishes Goldman’s analysis from simple optimism is its emphasis on asymmetry in adoption.

As evidence, Schneider points to the following:

- On the consumer side, agents that already operate smartphones on users’ behalf have emerged in China, carrying out tasks like “book me a flight to Singapore” and “organize my inbox, remove spam, and sort email by business priority” in the background. The market is entering the stage of “always-on” background agents.
- In contrast, adoption in the enterprise is more complex and takes longer. Writing code and software is far more involved than booking an airline ticket: it requires testing, retesting, integration with other code, documentation, and the ability to function within compliance and budget constraints.
- Adoption-rate forecasts show a “very long tail,” with the share of knowledge workers using agentic AI reaching **12% in 2030** and **37% in 2040**.
Moreover, economics differ by workload. Coding is efficient because agents can handle it autonomously and return quickly, but in the case of real-time voice agents Goldman notes that, because of “time dependency” and “latency” characteristics, **the cost of humans was still lower than the cost of LLMs** at this point.

In conclusion, Schneider argues that the “margin inflection” for hyperscalers and model providers differs sharply from the market’s dominant view that AI adoption will simply keep increasing costs until the model becomes unsustainable. The evolution will be uneven and nonlinear, and not every player is at the same stage. “All players are lifted on the upside, but at different speeds” — and especially in terms of operating cash flow, differentiation among hyperscalers is set to become increasingly visible.
