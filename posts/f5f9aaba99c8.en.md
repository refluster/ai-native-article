## Executive Summary

OpenAI is reportedly planning not to release its latest model, “GPT-5.6,” to the general public, and instead distribute it first only to a limited set of partners. According to reporting by The Information, this came at the request of the Trump administration, and during the preview period the government will “approve access customer by customer.” For an administration that initially championed a “hands-off” approach, this means it has begun taking hold of the release decision itself for frontier models in advance—marking a shift in the center of gravity of regulation away from deployment and use, and toward the earlier question of whether a model is released at all.

## What Happened: GPT-5.6’s “Staged Release”

As reported by TechCrunch, citing The Information, the release of OpenAI’s latest model, “GPT-5.6,” will take a different form from previous launches.

- OpenAI plans not to make the new model publicly available, and instead share it only with **a very small number of close partners**, reportedly because “the Trump administration told it to.”
- In an internal meeting this week, CEO Sam Altman reportedly told employees that during the preview period the government would be “**approving access customer by customer**.”
- Altman also reportedly said that if the limited release goes well, OpenAI hopes to follow it with a broader public release “**a couple of weeks later**.”
- According to The Information, the new model is not only undergoing review by the administration, but OpenAI staff also “**worked closely**” with the government on this release.
- The two government offices said to have requested the limited release are the **Office of the National Cyber Director** and the **Office of Science and Technology Policy**.
The key factual point here is that control over distribution has, in effect, shifted from the vendor to the government side. An arrangement under which access is approved “customer by customer” means the government is making individualized decisions not only about whether a model can be released, but about who can access it.

## A Shift Away From “Hands-Off”: The June 2 Executive Order

TechCrunch places this development in the context of a broader change in the administration’s posture.

- The Trump administration had initially presented itself as taking a “**hands-off**” approach to AI.
- But in recent months it has turned toward seeking federal oversight of new models.
- This month (**June 2, 2026**), President Trump signed an executive order directing certain AI companies to **voluntarily submit new models to the government for testing and evaluation before making them publicly available**.
The conclusion to draw is that the request surrounding GPT-5.6 can be read as the first case in which the framework of “pre-release government evaluation” signaled by that executive order has appeared not merely as voluntary submission, but as concrete intervention in the method of release itself. The article summarizes this as the administration trying to make OpenAI “**do what Anthropic is already doing voluntarily**.”

## Anthropic’s “Project Glasswing” as a Precedent

To help frame the OpenAI case, the article points to Anthropic’s earlier example as a useful comparison.

- Earlier this year, Anthropic announced that it would offer its new frontier cyber model, “**Claude Mythos**,” **only to a small number of partners** through a program called “**Project Glasswing**,” prompting a fair amount of controversy.
- Anthropic’s argument was that the model was “**too powerful**,” and that if misused it could do more harm than good.
That said, the assessment is far from settled. The article itself notes that observers continue to debate whether Anthropic’s rhetoric was merely a “marketing ploy” or a legitimate attempt to prevent misuse of a powerful model, adding that “the answer is probably somewhere in the middle.” In other words, the explanation that a model must be tightly limited because it is “too powerful” can serve both as a national-security rationale and as a form of positioning—and that ambiguity carries over directly into government-led limited releases as well.

## Why Cyber Capabilities Are the Central Issue

The article also explains what, concretely, is meant by the “national security concerns” said to justify the limited release.

- Cybercriminals have long used automation tools, but in the age of generative AI they now have unprecedented amounts of “digital ammunition.”
- LLMs have been shown to be adept at **creating malware**, and some can even **autonomously carry out entire ransomware attacks**.
- The specific concern with frontier cyber tools like Mythos is that they are said to be able to **discover and exploit software vulnerabilities at speeds far beyond what any human analyst could manage**. Since much software contains hidden bugs that can serve as entry points, this creates an obvious and serious problem for any organization running complex infrastructure.
At the same time, the article attaches an important caveat: because these models are not publicly available, it is difficult to determine how much of a threat they actually pose. There is a circularity here: the decision to close off a model on the basis of its dangerous capabilities also makes it harder to verify those capabilities from the outside.

## Implications (So-what): “Public Release Itself” Becomes the Regulatory Target

Based on the facts above, the implications can be organized into three main points.

First, this reflects a shift in the center of gravity of regulation. Rather than export controls or use restrictions, the government has intervened in release design itself—whether a model should be generally released or narrowed into a staged rollout. GPT-5.6’s “customer-by-customer” approval is the clearest expression of that.

Second, it marks a move from voluntariness to de facto prior review. The June 2 executive order called for “voluntary submission,” but in the case of GPT-5.6 government agencies are directly involved in how the release is structured. In practice, the line between voluntary action and official request is becoming increasingly blurred.

Third, it creates a trade-off in verifiability. If a model is closed on national-security grounds, outside parties simultaneously lose the means to verify the national-security claims being made about it. The fact that opinion remains divided over Anthropic’s explanation that the model was “too powerful” is also a reflection of that inability to verify. Governance of frontier models is shifting away from the question of capability escalation itself, and toward a more fundamental question of governance: who gets to stop a release, and who gets to verify that judgment.
