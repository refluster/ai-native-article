In an era when AI takes on real operational work, what becomes scarce is neither labor nor inspection, but the design itself of deciding “what should be delegated.”

85% of organizations say they want to shift to agentic AI within the next 3 years, while 76% say “their current operations and infrastructure cannot keep up” (MIT Tech Review Insights, “Agentic AI doesn’t redraw the org chart—it redesigns it”). Mercari has placed Toshiya Kimura in a single seat as CHRO, CAIO, and CTO Japan Business (“Mercari appoints Toshiya Kimura as CHRO, CAIO, and CTO Japan Business”). And Takayuki Fukatsu argues that the value of engineers is shifting from “reviewability” to “delegatability” (“From ‘Reviewability’ to ‘Delegatability’”). These three examples may look disconnected, but in fact they can be explained by the same principle: the center of gravity of human work is moving from “execution” to “the design of delegation.”

## Why Layering on Top Fails — The Fault Line ABT Exposed

The numbers presented in the MIT Tech Review Insights survey (based on reporting with Ema and PwC) capture, in a single frame, the gap between the will to transition and present-day reality. 85% say they want to move to agentic AI, and 76% say their current operations and infrastructure cannot keep up. If both are true, then what is lacking is not the capability of AI, but the organizational vessel meant to receive it.

The core of the article lies in the metaphor of “taping agents onto human-shaped workflows.” Many companies simply paste agents on top of existing operating models that were designed for humans. Approval flows, job descriptions, evaluation metrics—all of them were built on the assumption that “humans execute tasks one by one.” Put agents into that system—agents that do not wait, that operate in parallel, and that do not rest—and the container breaks before the agents do.

That is why ABT (Agentic Business Transformation) demands not a tool rollout, but a redesign of three layers: the technology stack, the workforce, and the metrics of success. The third point is especially important. Unless an organization rethinks “what counts as success,” it will continue measuring new ways of working with old rulers. This is not a question of “who does the work.” It is a question of redrawing the boundary line: what should be delegated to machines, and what should remain in human hands?

## Why It Is Consolidated Into One Seat — Mercari’s CHRO × CAIO × CTO

If ABT argues that organizations must redesign three layers, then Mercari’s personnel move shows what that design judgment looks like when physically concentrated into a single point in the organization. The titles given to Toshiya Kimura are threefold: CHRO (Chief Human Resources Officer), CAIO (Chief AI Officer), and CTO Japan Business.

What this integration shows is a recognition that AI strategy and HR strategy are no longer separate decisions. Deciding which work should be delegated to agents is, at the same time, equivalent to deciding what kind of talent to hire, how to evaluate people, and where humans should remain in the loop. Where you draw the boundary of delegation determines the shape of the org chart itself. That is why there is no longer room to keep the person responsible for AI separate from the person responsible for people; the two are bundled into a single seat.

What the ABT research stated in general terms—“redesign the metrics”—Mercari has underwritten through a concrete organizational move: “concentrate in one person the authority to redesign the metrics.” This is not merely role blurring. It is an organizational recrystallization around a new function: the design of delegation.

## From “Inspection” to “Delegation” — Redefining the Engineering Profession

What is happening at the organizational level is being repeated at the level of individual professional capability. Takayuki Fukatsu’s essay argues that the standard by which software engineers are valued is shifting from “reviewability” to “delegatability.”

Until now, the human role upstream from AI assumed that people could “read and inspect” AI-generated implementations. But once the amount and speed of generation exceed the human capacity for close reading, the inspection model—verifying line by line—breaks down. The question then is no longer whether the output can be inspected, but whether one can design “what range can be safely delegated with confidence.” While parts of implementation are replaced by AI, the human center of gravity expands into the domains that assume the premises and responsibilities of delegation: design, requirements definition, and decision-making. The article’s depiction of blurred professional boundaries is simply another name for this shift in the center of gravity.

Here, the three articles converge on a single point. The reason organizations are redesigning their operating models, the reason Mercari is bundling three executive roles, and the reason engineers are ceasing to be inspectors all point to the same thing: the work that remains after execution is relinquished is the work of designing delegation and bearing responsibility for it.

## Shared Principle: Work Is Moving to the “Design of Delegation Boundaries”

These three facts suggest a single principle: as AI absorbs execution, the scarce human function shifts away from “labor” and “inspection” and toward the **design of delegation boundaries**—that is, deciding “what should be delegated to machines, under what conditions, and who bears responsibility for the outcome, and how.”

The inductive logic is as follows. First, layering on top fails because legacy operating models were built on the assumption that “humans execute and inspect tasks one by one” (ABT). Second, the authority to redesign that assumption has been consolidated into a single seat, because AI strategy and HR strategy can no longer be separated (Mercari). Third, the collapse of that same assumption extends to the individual, replacing the role of inspector with that of designer of delegation (Fukatsu). Across three different levels of resolution—organization, structure, and individual—the same fault line appears: the boundary between “the person who executes” and “the person who designs delegation.” Since all three facts sit on that same line, the most plausible reading is that the “blurring of roles” now being observed does not mean roles are disappearing. It means roles are moving and concentrating around a new focal point.

This principle is falsifiable. If, in the age of agents, successful organizations turn out not to specialize delegation design but instead operate effectively with “everyone delegating on the side,” or if the dominant model becomes companies that continue to grow while keeping AI leadership and HR leadership separate, then this reading will prove wrong. But the three pieces of evidence we have right now all point in the opposite direction: toward concentrating design into a single point.

## Forecasts and Implications

First forecast. More organizations will explicitly establish “delegation design” as a specialized professional function (confidence: high). The grounds are that Mercari has already institutionalized it in a single seat, and that ABT identifies redesigned metrics as a condition for success. The title will not necessarily be CAIO. But a role will emerge at the intersection of people and technology: one that decides which work is handed to agents under what conditions, and that bears responsibility when that delegation fails.

Second forecast. Individual evaluation criteria will shift from “what can you build?” to “what can you safely delegate?” (confidence: medium). Fukatsu’s notion of delegatability can spread beyond engineers to every profession that must articulate requirements, design the boundaries of delegation, and take responsibility for results. Still, this forecast is only medium confidence—not because inspection will disappear entirely, but because a more moderate outcome is also plausible, one in which inspection simply changes form, from “close reading of everything” to “boundary design and sampling.”

The practical implication is simple. Do not design the introduction of agents as a project to “replace human work with tools.” That is layering on top, and it is the trap the 76% have fallen into. The question is not “which tasks should we automate?” It is “which judgments should remain in human hands, what should be delegated and under what conditions, and who takes responsibility when it goes wrong?” Design the boundary of delegation first, then redesign the metrics by which that boundary is measured. Only organizations that begin there can rescue the aspirations of the 85% from the reality of the 76%.
