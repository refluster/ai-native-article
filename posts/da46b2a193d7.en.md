## Executive Summary

On 10 October 2026, Microsoft Chairman and CEO Satya Nadella published "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era" as an X article, proposing that frontier models be treated as insider risks. The premise is not malice: any sufficiently capable actor with access to important systems can make mistakes or be compromised. The central claim is that the supply of intelligence (the model) must be separated from the authority over it, with the controls placed outside the model.

## Starting point: behavior can no longer be traced to a code path

Nadella begins with what differs from traditional software.

- As traditional software spread across the economy over several decades, there were tools and capability to trace behavior to a specific code path.
- That mechanistic understanding eludes today's Super Intelligence systems. Model behaviors and outputs cannot be attributed to specific training-data inputs or configurations of model weights.
- Yet complex agentic systems and models are being deployed with access to our most sensitive data and the ability to take mission-critical actions on our behalf.
Two conclusions follow. Responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf cannot be outsourced, and a model provider's assurances do not relieve us of it. Nor can Super Intelligence be treated as a set of nested black boxes whose recommendations, answers and actions we simply accept or reject.

## The proposal: models as insider risks

The design goal is a contained system whose behavior can be observed, whose limits can be tested, and whose actions can always be contained. Nadella restates this as separating the supply of intelligence from the authority over it.

Setting aside the hard problem of alignment, the approach starts from engineering containment and governance: surround non-deterministic models with strong deterministic system design, human controls and reliable operating procedures, and establish industry standards where existing ones are insufficient.

The concrete frame is to treat frontier closed and open-weight models like insider risks. Nadella states the reasoning: not because they are necessarily malicious, but because any sufficiently capable actor with access to important systems can make mistakes or be compromised, and the architecture of containment and control must account for that.

Enterprises have refined the handling of powerful insiders for decades. The practices Nadella lists are to establish identity, limit privileges, log activity and create containment boundaries. In his account, these principles are now beginning to be applied to Super Intelligence inside the enterprise.

## Transparency alone is not enough

The starting point is model chain-of-thought (CoT) transparency as a non-negotiable. "Neuralese" cannot be a justification for opaque model reasoning.

But CoT transparency alone is neither sufficient nor dependable, because we do not yet know how to make model outputs themselves consistently faithful or transparent.

Models should be used to adversarially test and verify each other, Nadella says. The result, however, can be an opaque model inside an opaque orchestration layer, watched by another opaque model: nested black boxes.

## Put the controls outside the model

His answer draws on an information-security principle dating back to the 1970s: a program must not be able to bypass or tamper with the mechanisms that enforce its permissions. Today this means three separations.

- Separate the model from the harness that orchestrates its work.
- Separate the model from the action space that defines what it can do.
- Externalize controls and safeguards.
The controls that govern what a model can access and what actions it can take must sit outside the model.

## Seven principles of observability

Nadella says these systems should be designed around principles of observability, and lists seven.

- **Model diversity**: no one model should become the sole dependency for an important outcome or be responsible for verifying its own work.
- **Observe everything**: every meaningful model action must leave tamper-proof, human-readable evidence. If it cannot be observed, it cannot be trusted. It must be possible to reproduce how an outcome was achieved without relying on the model to attest to it.
- **Verifiability**: continuously test the entire system, including failures, attacks, edge cases and system changes, not just successful tasks.
- **Independent controls**: organizations should be able to independently determine what a model can access and what actions it can take.
- **Independent auditability**: validation must be independent of the intelligence being validated. No single model should control both a system's behavior and the evidence needed to determine whether that behavior is aligned with the original intent.
- **Containment**: assume a model is compromised and contain it from the start. Like an emergency brake, an authorized person should always be able to pause or shut down a model mid-task. More advanced models will require more advanced containment technologies that need to be standardized.
- **Incident disclosure**: when these systems fail or are compromised, affected parties need timely disclosure, and there must be mechanisms to share what went wrong, which controls failed and how to prevent a recurrence, with learnings shared industry-wide. This should include implementation details that change the behavior of agents at runtime.
## Conclusion: design so the model needs the least trust

The article closes with one sentence: the most trustworthy Super Intelligence system will not be the one with the model we trust most, but the one that enables us to trust the model the least.

## Implications (our reading)

What precedes is the source's argument; what follows is this article's own reading.

- The locus of trust moves. Nadella shifts trust from the model's character or the provider's assurances to deterministic controls and evidence that live outside the model. The decision about what surrounds a model weighs more than the decision about which model to pick.
- Verifier independence. The principle that no single model should verify its own work asks anyone designing multi-agent operations to separate the roles of author and reviewer.
- Evidence requirements. "Reproducible without relying on the model to attest" requires logs recorded through a path independent of the model's output.
- Standing of the piece. The author is the Chairman and CEO of Microsoft, and the post showed 13.8M views when fetched. The content is a proposal, not a settled standard or regulation; the call for industry standards where existing ones are insufficient itself indicates that such standards are not yet in place.
