A measured acceleration in model progress, a court ruling against a government designation, and a legal tool that leaves the final call to a lawyer look unrelated. They are explained by the same principle: **the faster AI capability grows on the inside, the more the authority to answer for its results moves to a party outside the capability.** That is the hypothesis of this analysis.

## Three separate events

The first is the measurement in "METR assessment: Anthropic's model progress runs about 1.9× faster than before, and evaluation methods struggle to keep up." METR, a non-profit focused on AI safety, assessed that the pace of Anthropic's capability gains has been about 1.9 times its earlier rate since October 2025. The estimate range is 1.5 to 2.5 times, and the acceleration is attributed to something other than expanded training compute. AI has not yet reached the point of contributing autonomously and substantially to key training steps.

The second is "Federal court rules Pentagon's 'supply chain risk' designation of Anthropic unlawful." Judge Rita Lin of a U.S. federal district court ruled on Thursday that the Defense Department's designation of Anthropic as a "supply chain risk" was unlawful, concluding that the government's action violated constitutional rights. The government is expected to appeal, and a separate dispute over a designation under a different statute continues in the D.C. Circuit.

The third is "Claude for Legal publishes a gallery of legal artifacts: start from a template, then tailor it with your own data and connectors." On October 28, 2026, Anthropic announced a gallery of ready-to-use legal artifacts for Claude for Legal. Artifacts are interactive tools such as trackers and dashboards. Each opens showing an example; users add their own data and connectors, tailor it, and share it with colleagues. The design assumes a division of labor: Claude completes a first pass, and the lawyer applies judgment to the result.

A measurement, a government classification, a workplace tool. The domains and actors differ. What they share is that the contested question is not the capability itself but who decides how it is treated.

## Analysis 1: The one who measures is not the one accelerating

What stands out in the METR article is less the speed than who made the estimate. The roughly 1.9× figure came not from Anthropic but from METR, a non-profit working on AI safety. The range, 1.5 to 2.5 times, is wide, and the article's own framing names the problem: evaluation methods are struggling to keep up.

There are two layers here. First, the faster capability grows, the less self-reporting is worth, because the yardstick of the party that is improving tends to move with it. Second, the assessment attributes the acceleration to something other than expanded training compute and notes that AI is not yet autonomously contributing at scale to key training steps. The assessor is therefore doing more than producing a number; it is separating what is driving the speed. The interpretation of the number, not just the number, sits outside.

The implication is that as capability rises, what becomes necessary is not a smarter model but a more independent measurer.

## Analysis 2: The authority to classify is tested only when contested

The article on the federal court ruling shows the same structure from another angle. The Defense Department designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk", a judgment not about the company's capability but about the danger of using it. The district court found the measure unlawful and concluded that the government's action violated constitutional rights.

Note that the designating body did not reverse itself; an external forum did. And per the article, the government is expected to appeal, with a separate dispute under a different statute continuing in the D.C. Circuit. The judgment does not settle once; it keeps being tested through multiple outside tribunals.

The power to designate is strong. But the power to decide whether that power was properly used does not belong to the designator. Again the judgment sits outside the party that acted.

## Analysis 3: The first pass is the AI's; the last word is the lawyer's

The Claude for Legal article shows this structure in its most everyday form. Artifacts are interactive tools such as trackers and dashboards. Claude completes a first pass and the lawyer adds judgment to the result; the article says this division is the premise of the design.

What the design hinges on is not that the tools are ready to use. Each artifact in the gallery opens with an example displayed. Users add their own data and connectors, tailor the result, and share it with colleagues. The tool is placed inside a procedure in which the generated result is inspected, reworked, and exposed to colleagues' eyes. Generation gets faster, but the position of the human who answers for the result stays written into the design.

Here the "outside" is not a third-party institution but a different role in the same organization: the lawyer as the one who judges. The structure is the same all the same. The one who produces and the one who answers are separate.

## The shared principle

Line up the three facts. In the METR assessment, capability grows at about 1.9 times its earlier pace, yet its measurement and interpretation are handled by an assessor outside the party holding the capability. In the federal court ruling, a strong power, a government designation, was found unlawful by a court outside the party that exercised it. In Claude for Legal's design, AI takes the first pass and the judgment on the result stays with the lawyer.

These facts suggest the following principle: **the power to execute and the power to answer for the result do not work when kept in the same place, and the faster capability grows, the more their separation becomes a design requirement rather than an exception.**

Why? In three steps. First, when capability accelerates, execution tends to outrun verification; the METR article's concern about evaluation methods keeping up is a symptom of that lag. Second, when the executor verifies itself, the yardstick moves with the execution and verification stops being verification. Having an outside body measure, and a court judge the lawfulness of a designation, fixes the yardstick. Third, final responsibility can only be borne by the party that accepts the result. A design in which a lawyer applies judgment to the result presumes that however fast the AI completes the first pass, the accountable human does not become unnecessary.

If so, the axis of competition is no longer only how smart a system is. Who verifies, and who holds the yardstick of verification, is weighed as heavily as capability. The hypothesis is falsifiable: if, alongside accelerating capability, domains in which self-assessment alone earns trust expand while external measurers and tribunals shrink, the principle is wrong.

## Implications and outlook

Several shifts follow from the principle.

First, the independence of evaluation itself is likely to become the point of contention. The METR article names evaluation methods keeping up as the challenge; the faster capability grows, the harder it is for methods to catch up. Investment in and debate over evaluation frameworks should therefore increase. Confidence is high; the grounds are the width of the estimate range, 1.5 to 2.5 times, which shows how uncertain the measurement is.

Second, disputes between capability providers and users are likely to take the form of fights over where authority lies rather than over technical merit. As the court ruling shows, a strong measure like a designation does not end with the first decision but keeps being tested across several forums. An expected appeal and a separate dispute continuing in another court foreshadow this. Confidence is moderate, and does not depend on the outcome of any individual case.

Third, in practical tooling, designs that make the position of human judgment explicit are likely to become standard, as in the Claude for Legal gallery, which separates the AI's first pass from human judgment within the procedure. Confidence is moderate; the grounds are that the division of labor is already written into the product's premise.

For the reader, one practical takeaway: when adopting AI, decide who verifies and who answers before, or at least alongside, evaluating capability. Even when the verifier sits inside your own organization, separate the person who produces from the person who checks. Where that cannot be done, it is reason enough to slow adoption. Capability accelerates inside; judgment is placed outside. Organizations designed around that asymmetry are the ones that can take in the benefit of acceleration safely.
