The center of gravity in evaluating AI is shifting from what comes out to how it was made. But only the unit of evaluation has moved; the means for third parties to verify that process have not kept up.

There are three facts that seem unrelated at first glance. Capital expenditure by the top 5 U.S. hyperscalers is expected to reach about $720 billion in 2026, already moving the prices of semiconductors, electricity, and imported goods (“AI investment has become a ‘macro cycle’”). On July 9, 2026, Google introduced a new “How this ad was made” panel for ads in Search, YouTube, and Discover (“Google adds ‘How this ad was made’ panel to ads”). And a survey has emerged that reframes the trustworthiness of LLM agents not in terms of the correctness of their final answers but in terms of the traceability of their execution process (“Can’t be measured by ‘the correctness of the final answer’”). Macroeconomics, ad products, and agent research. In fact, all three can be explained by the same principle.

## Capital is being invested in process, while outputs still are not being measured

LSEG macro analyst Erwan Jacob argues that AI is no longer just a technology theme; it has already become a force in the macroeconomy. What the figure of about $720 billion in projected 2026 capital expenditure by the top 5 companies is moving is not the price of products created by AI. It is the price of semiconductors, the price of electricity, and the price of imported goods. In other words, what the market is pricing right now is not output but process.

The most important part of Jacob’s point is not the scale but the sequence. AI’s effect on prices is “inflationary in the investment phase, disinflationary in the diffusion phase,” and those two phases do not arrive simultaneously but sequentially. What makes the judgment so difficult for central banks is that what can be observed is the cost of the process, while the benefits appear later, in a different phase, under different indicators. As long as we remain in the investment phase, the data in hand consists only of the process’s resource consumption, not the value of the output.

### What can be observed and what we want to judge are out of alignment

There is a structural mismatch here. The question we want to answer is an output-side one—“Will this investment pay off?”—but what we can observe are only process-side figures. $720 billion can be measured definitively as spending, but what that spending ultimately produced cannot be measured with the same precision until the latter half of that sequential relationship arrives.

## What is disclosed is not the content of the ad, but how it was made

Google’s implementation is an example of this shift in center of gravity being fixed into product specifications. What the “How this ad was made” panel shows users in My Ad Center is not whether the claims in the ad are true. It shows whether the ad was created or edited with AI—in other words, how it was made. Building on SynthID signal embedding and the 2023 disclosure requirements for election ads, Google implemented AI-use disclosure as a standard product feature.

And the notable part is the two-tier design. Ads created with Google’s own generative AI tools receive disclosure labels automatically. If third-party tools were used, advertisers must disclose that manually. Depending on regional regulations, the label is also shown on the ad surface itself.

The meaning of this split is clear. The moment process becomes the unit of evaluation, the boundary between the range in which that process can be automatically attested and the range that can only depend on disclosure becomes exposed as a product specification. Google can guarantee only what happens inside its own tools. Outside that boundary, the design is to trust the declaration.

## The unit of trust is shifting from “correctness” to “provenance”

The survey by Yiqi Wang et al., “From Agent Traces to Trust” (arXiv:2606.04990v4), formalizes the same shift from the research side. It reframes the trustworthiness of LLM agents not as “the correctness of the final answer” but as “the traceability of the execution process,” and names the state in which process has dropped out the **process-level accountability gap**.

The paper classifies agent execution across six dimensions: trace source, evidence unit, provenance relation, granularity/timing, representation format, and trust function. What matters here is that provenance is treated not as a single log but as a design object. The argument is that we need designs that connect which evidence supports which claim to verification, debugging, safety controls, and auditing.

At the same time, the survey acknowledges the gaps in its own field. Using tool use and memory as concrete examples, it points to gaps in defenses against indirect prompt injection, in tracking memory contamination, and in the absence of end-to-end benchmarks. In the very same document that proposes shifting the unit of evaluation, it says there is still no way to measure what lies at the destination of that shift.

## Common principle: the unit of evaluation has moved to process, but verification capability has not yet moved with it

The three facts differ in object, timescale, and field. What they share is a two-tier structure.

First, whether in pricing, disclosure, or judgments of trust, the unit of evaluation has shifted from output to the generative process. The market is pricing not AI’s output but the process of building AI, Google is disclosing not ad content but how it was made, and research has made not answer correctness but execution traceability the condition of trust.

Second, the ability of third parties to verify that process has not kept up with the shift. Google leaves everything outside its own tools to advertisers’ manual disclosure. The survey explicitly states the absence of end-to-end benchmarks. Macro analysis can observe the costs of the investment phase, but it cannot observe the benefits of the diffusion phase with the same precision. In every case, a gap remains between “declared process” and “verified process.”

These facts suggest the following. When the object of evaluation shifts from output to process, verification capability does not come along automatically. The reason is a fundamental asymmetry. Outputs can be retrieved and re-inspected at any time after they are completed. Processes cannot. A process can only be recorded while it is happening, and a process that was not recorded cannot be reconstructed afterward. Verifying outputs is a matter of “going to look.” Verifying process is a matter of “recording in advance.”

That is why this shift does not happen after verification methods are in place, but before they are. No one waits. Regulation arrives on its effective date. Capital moves without waiting for judgment. Agents are still running today. As a result, a structural gap opens up between the unit of evaluation and verification capability. The name **process-level accountability gap** was coined within agent research, but what it points to extends across fields.

This claim is falsifiable. If a mechanism appears that makes it possible to verify process after the fact as easily as we can verify outputs, then this principle collapses.

## Predictions and implications

First, what is highly likely to happen is that, for the time being, the gap will be filled by self-reporting. The basis for this is Google’s implementation itself. It left everything outside Google’s own tools to advertisers’ manual disclosure because that is the cheapest way to fill the gap. Expanding the range of automatic attestation requires cooperation from the other side; adding one more disclosure field is something you can do on your own. The same choice is rational for other operators as well, and for a while “declared process” will become the de facto standard.

Second, with a moderate probability, provenance data itself will become an asset. What the survey’s six-dimensional classification showed is that provenance is not a single log but a design object. And if it is a design object, that also means it is a point of differentiation. Who records what, and at what granularity, will later become a source of bargaining power. The side without records cannot verify the claims made by the side that has them.

Third, there is a macroeconomic implication. If the inflationary effects of the investment phase and the disinflationary effects of the diffusion phase are sequential, then there will necessarily be a period between them in which “costs can be observed but benefits cannot.” The $720 billion is pushing up semiconductor and electricity prices now; whatever results from that will push prices down later. The length of this period will become a variable determining the political and social tolerance for AI investment.

There is one practical implication to focus on. When your organization makes something with AI, check whether the verification you have prepared asks only “Is the output correct?” Process records cannot be created later. Any process you do not record today will permanently not exist when someone asks for it in the future. Even if you begin recording only after disclosure or auditing is demanded, the range you can trace back is zero.

That is why starting to record before anyone is asking for it yet has meaning beyond its cost. In a world where process has become the unit of evaluation, the mere existence or absence of records determines the upper bound of what you will be able to claim later.
