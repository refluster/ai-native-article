## Executive Summary

Anthropic's Economics team has published a scenario-explorer model, built on the technical report "Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI" (Korinek, Jones, Sacher, Cotter, McCrory, and others, 2026), showing 2030 US GDP could land anywhere from 1.6% to 32.4% above a no-AI baseline depending on how fast AI capabilities advance and diffuse. But all three scenarios share a second finding: the faster the growth, the more of it flows to capital rather than wages. In the most extreme scenario, knowledge-worker wages fall by more than 10% by 2030 and unemployment rises past typical recessionary levels.

## The model's core idea: jobs as bundles of tasks

The model treats every occupation as a bundle of tasks drawn from the US Department of Labor's O*NET taxonomy. Using a nurse's day as the illustration, AI can affect a task in four ways: leaving it unchanged, augmenting it (drafting discharge instructions, remote patient monitoring), automating it (charting vitals, ordering ward supplies), or creating an entirely new task (checking how well an AI triages patients, reviewing an AI-proposed care plan). Some tasks — bathing a patient, for instance — remain explicitly human-only. The model's premise is that AI reshapes what a job is made of rather than eliminating jobs wholesale.

## Three scenarios, GDP diverging from +1.6% to +32.4%

The model lays out three 2030 scenarios:

- **Modest** — AI's economic impact resembles the internet's. 2030 GDP is +1.6% versus a no-AI baseline, at $34.1T.
- **Substantial** — By 2030, AI is capable of doing half of all knowledge work, most of it autonomously, but only half of that capable work is actually adopted — most knowledge-work tasks are still done without AI. GDP is +8.3%, at $36.3T.
- **Extreme** — AI is more productive than humans at the vast majority of knowledge-work tasks, doing nearly all of them autonomously, and creates essentially no new tasks for people. This would likely require recursively self-improving AI adopted quickly. Annual GDP growth reaches 15%, doubling the economy every 4.5 years; 2030 GDP is +32.4%, at $44.4T.
## Labor vs. capital: growth tilts further toward capital as it accelerates

The model's central finding is distributional: today roughly 60 cents of every economic dollar goes to labor and 40 cents to capital, and that split shifts further toward capital the faster the scenario:

- Modest: 59.4% labor / 40.6% capital (capital's share up 0.6 points)
- Substantial: 56.1% labor / 43.9% capital (up 3.9 points)
- Extreme: 45.2% labor / 54.8% capital (up 14.8 points)
In the Extreme scenario the economy as a whole is far wealthier, yet total labor income is barely changed by 2030 — meaning the challenge shifts from "will growth happen" to "how broadly will its gains be shared."

## Wages and jobs move unevenly across workers

The wage effects are asymmetric. In the Substantial scenario, knowledge-worker wages stay essentially flat while wages rise for manual occupations (like construction) whose demand grows on the back of AI-driven productivity elsewhere. In the Extreme scenario this asymmetry widens sharply: knowledge-worker wages fall more than 10% by 2030 while wages for other workers rise significantly. On jobs, more transformative scenarios force more knowledge workers — coders, call-center agents — to switch into occupations less exposed to AI, such as electrician or nurse. Because switching occupations takes time, this reallocation temporarily pushes unemployment up; in the Extreme scenario, the model projects unemployment rising past levels typical of a recession.

## The public's own predictions already point toward "Substantial"

In August, Anthropic surveyed more than 10,000 Americans (a general-public sample of n=10,980 and a site-visitor sample of n=25,608) on their expectations for AI capability, adoption, autonomy, productivity, and how long it would take to find a new job after being displaced. Feeding the typical respondent's answers back into the model implies an outcome close to the Substantial scenario — 2030 GDP about 10% higher than without AI, and unemployment around 5%. About 10% of respondents, meanwhile, gave answers consistent with the Extreme scenario — a minority, but not a negligible one, already pricing in the most transformative future on offer.

## What the model leaves out

Anthropic states the model's limits explicitly: it excludes policy responses, business cycles, aggregate-demand effects from data-center buildout, and catastrophic risks, and it does not model scenarios involving highly capable robots. External economists who reviewed the report (including Daron Acemoglu and David Autor) raised further concerns — the model does not track individual workers, so it can only paint a coarse picture of displacement costs, and it remains an open question whether AI-exposed occupations will actually shrink rather than grow. Anthropic frames this as version 1.0, a tool for thinking about what different technological paths would imply for the economy rather than a forecast — one it expects to revise as the underlying research develops.
