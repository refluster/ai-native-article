## Executive Summary

On June 18, 2026, OpenAI’s Alignment team published research showing that reinforcement learning (RL) targeting “beneficial traits” such as truthfulness and openness to correction can produce alignment improvements that generalize to domains, tasks, and evaluation metrics not used in training—and that these improvements persist even under adversarial pressure. The core point is that the work suggests it may be possible to intentionally induce the reverse of the known phenomenon of “emergent misalignment,” in which narrow bad behaviors lead to broad misalignment. Generalization was observed across domains, including cases where training only on the health domain still improved performance on non-health evaluations.

## The Research Question — Can “Emergent Misalignment” Be Used in Reverse?

The authors are Akshay V. Jagadeesh, Rahul K. Arora, Khaled Saab, and Karan Singhal, and the work was published on the OpenAI Alignment Research Blog dated June 18, 2026. The starting observations were as follows:

- **Emergent misalignment**: Prior work has reported that models trained on “narrow” bad behaviors—such as writing unsafe code or engaging in fraud in realistic scenarios—can begin to behave badly even in broad situations unrelated to the training task. In other words, training in a narrow setting can produce wide-ranging behavioral changes beyond the training distribution.
- **This study’s question**: If so, then could the reverse also hold—if RL is applied toward “beneficial traits” in one domain, such as health, would alignment improvements generalize across diverse tasks and domains? If they do, then models may not only become safer, but could also actively benefit humanity across settings ranging from current use cases such as health support to future high-risk scenarios.
The background problem is that as high-risk domains in which AI acts autonomously in unseen situations—such as medicine, science, education, and coding—continue to grow, there is an increasing need for generalization to unfamiliar contexts, pressures, and long, complex conversations.

## Method — Training 7 Beneficial Traits on Realistic Conversational Data

The team identified a set of behavioral traits that could contribute to good behavior across a wide range of situations.

- Target traits: **truthfulness**, **epistemic humility**, **metacognitive transparency** (the ability to explain one’s own reasoning process), **corrigibility** (openness to correction), **risk sensitivity**, **universal fairness**, and **concern for human welfare**.
- Data: A synthetic dataset of realistic conversations designed to test each trait under uncertainty, pressure, and conflicting interests. Domains spanned health, education, science, law, engineering, and economics (business). Example scenarios included whether the model avoids overstating scientific conclusions and acknowledges uncertainty, whether it remains open to correction while helping with complex multistep business decisions, and whether it applies fair standards consistently across different people and contexts.
- Training setup: The model was trained in a realistic post-training mixture in which beneficial-trait data was mixed in at **only a small amount**, while standard RL data made up the majority. The comparison target was a **compute-matched baseline** trained from the same initialization point with the same RL compute budget. The team used a realistic RL setup without any prior synthetic-document fine-tuning to elicit the target behaviors.
The authors also explicitly note that these traits are not meant to answer the question of “which values AI should ultimately align to.” Rather, they are a concrete starting point for empirically testing whether reinforcing beneficial behavioral traits can broadly improve alignment. The question of which values should ultimately be embodied, they argue, is a broader one requiring social deliberation and collective input.

## Results ① — Broad Generalization to Out-of-Training Domains and Evaluations

The beneficial-traits RL model first improved as expected on held-out in-distribution scenarios, becoming more truthful, more open to correction, and more metacognitively transparent. The key question, however, was whether those gains would transfer to independent evaluations that differed in domain, task, and scoring procedure and were not used in training.

- Across **44 of 53 internal and external benchmarks**, the model improved over the compute-matched baseline. These covered 44 independently constructed metrics including deception, honesty, sycophancy, reward hacking, latent safety risks, harmful agentic behavior, and specification compliance.
- **Transfer to health and mental health**: The model improved on realistic medical conversations, physician-authored rubrics, and tasks measuring high-confidence medical errors. In mental health as well, it became less likely to produce harmful responses in sensitive conversations and better able to provide helpful support.
- **Strong test of out-of-domain generalization**: Even when health and science were excluded from the training data, the model still improved on held-out health evaluations scored with physician rubrics. More strikingly, a model trained on beneficial behavior in the **health domain only** showed large improvements on non-health alignment evaluations, including reward hacking, deception, and general misalignment—mirroring, in reverse, past findings in which training on poor-quality health data produced broad misalignment.
As a reference point, frontier models’ beneficial-trait scores have improved over time, with gains shown across o3 (April 2025), GPT-5 Thinking (August 2025), and GPT-5.5 Thinking (April 2026). Taken together, these results indicate that training on beneficial traits can produce improvements that generalize across diverse tasks, domains, and evaluation frameworks.

## Results ② — Persistence Under Adversarial Pressure

It is not enough for a model to behave well by default; if aligned behavior is easily overwritten, it is brittle. The team therefore tested persistence under both adversarial prompts and harmful fine-tuning.

- **Adversarial persona prompts**: When given prompts steering it toward poor health responses involving factual errors or misleading guidance, the beneficial-traits RL model resisted more strongly. Even prompts that substantially degraded the baseline had only a small effect on the alignment-trained model.
- **Selective persistence**: This resistance did not mean the model had become generally unsteerable. When prompted toward beneficial health responses, both models improved, and there was no significant difference in steerability. In other words, the model remained steerable in beneficial directions, while becoming harder to steer toward problematic ones such as deception, harmful advice, and reward hacking.
- **Resistance to harmful fine-tuning**: When both models were fine-tuned, with the same data and the same compute budget, to encourage inaccurate and misaligned medical advice, the baseline’s health performance dropped sharply and its non-health alignment evaluations also deteriorated substantially. The beneficial-traits RL model was somewhat less likely to decline on health evaluations and far less likely to decline on non-health alignment evaluations. The authors describe this as preliminary evidence that RL training on beneficial behavior may reduce susceptibility to emergent misalignment, while cautioning that further work is needed to disentangle the contribution of beneficial-trait training from that of standard post-training RL.
## Implications and Limitations

- **Implication**: Alignment may be not merely a matter of benchmark-specific responses, but a durable property that can be reinforced through RL and that can generalize and persist beyond the training distribution. Drawing on prior persona research, the authors suggest that personas vary in how deeply they take root inside a model, and that RL may provide a pathway for instilling beneficial personas more deeply.
- **Limitation**: This study is an early proof of concept showing that broad alignment generalization may be possible. It remains unclear which traits support robust aligned behavior, how such traits should be sourced from society, how they are represented and changed within a model, and what determines their durability or fragility. The trait set itself is positioned not as a final answer, but as a starting point.
