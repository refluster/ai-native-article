## Executive Summary

On November 13, 2026, Anthropic published recommendations for designing, running and reporting AI safety evaluations. A claim that a model is "safer than its predecessor" is only as sound as the eval behind it. Its core advice comes down to three things: write down the construct you want to measure before collecting data, validate your grader against human judgment before trusting any number it produces, and report uncertainty so readers can tell signal from noise.

## The source's claim: a safety claim is only as sound as its eval

Anthropic says safety evaluations inform consequential decisions: whether a model is ready to deploy, which safeguards ship alongside it, and what the next round of safety training and research should focus on. Saying a model is "safer than its predecessor" means it scored better on a set of evals someone built.

- A safety eval measures a model's propensity to cause or contribute to harm, whether a response makes harm more or less likely, and how severe that harm would be, rather than specific capabilities.
- It asks: "Given a specific input, did the model do the right thing?"
- Researchers have repeatedly found that AI evals are easy to game, don't always measure what they claim to measure, and aren't documented well enough to reproduce. Results can change with multiple-choice versus open-ended questions, or with the ordering of answer options.
Anthropic draws a parallel with psychology's replication problems: large-scale replication projects found that many published findings didn't hold up, and the field responded with reforms such as preregistration, better measurement and more rigorous statistics. The recommendations draw on those reforms and on Anthropic's own experience building evals.

## Design: match the design to the question, and measure what you mean to measure

**Observational versus causal designs.** Most evals are observational: they tell you one model scored differently from another, but not why. Causal claims such as "This intervention made the model safer" need a design that varies one variable at a time, for instance evaluating the same model with and without an intervention. In Anthropic's example, "Is the model responding safely to requests for content that might promote eating disorders?" is observational, while "Does altering the system prompt change whether the model responds safely...?" is causal.

**Construct validity.** For safety evals the obvious construct is refusal. That fits cases like helping launch a scam or phishing scheme, but for conversations about suicide and self-harm refusal is often the wrong construct. When a user is in crisis, simply declining to engage is itself a failure; the ideal response engages with empathy and points toward human support. A model that refuses every prompt related to suicide or self-harm would get a perfect score on a refusal-rate eval, yet is not necessarily safer.

Anthropic instead designs multi-turn evals that report an "appropriate response rate": the share of conversations in which the model behaved appropriately throughout, judged against a rubric specific to a risk area and developed by domain experts. One bad turn, such as divulging scam details when repeatedly pressed, fails the whole conversation. For user wellbeing, an appropriate response is supportive of risk disclosures, avoids discussing methods, points to crisis resources at the right moment, and does not position itself as a substitute for a crisis line.

Much of biology and cybersecurity is dual use, and there too an appropriate response is rarely a flat refusal: it engages with the legitimate version of the request while withholding the operational specifics that would give real uplift to the harmful version. Anthropic builds separate rubrics per topic.

**Measure both compliance and over-refusal.** In single-turn evals, Anthropic pairs harmful requests (maximizing the "harmless response rate") with benign requests in the same domain (minimizing the "refusal rate"). In a dual-use chemistry eval, a request for a nerve agent's chemical formulation should be refused, while a question about a historical chemical disaster should get a helpful answer. Sorting requests into the two buckets, especially borderline ones, requires subject-matter expertise.

## Generalization: results must hold for real users

**Multi-turn simulation.** Single-turn prompts are easy to build but saturate quickly and are not particularly realistic. Humans extract harmful information through gradual escalation, so a model that handles the first message well may respond inappropriately by the fifth. User distress accumulates across a conversation. In one multi-turn suicide and self-harm eval, a simulated user discloses a destabilizing event such as a layoff or a death in the family early on, then asks a seemingly ordinary question that warrants more care in light of that disclosure.

**How scenarios are built.** Anthropic built an internal tool that lets subject-matter experts do this systematically: 1. A domain expert writes a specification: the simulated user's objectives and tactics, plus dimensions such as persona, situation, tone and conversational style, each with several options. 2. The first user turn is fixed, so every run starts from the same prompt. 3. A separate Claude model generates the subsequent "user" turns, sampling from the specification's options; the model under test writes the "assistant" turns. 4. Each scenario runs several times, each run drawing a different combination of characteristics. 5. A grader model scores each transcript against the risk area's rubric and reports the share of conversations graded "appropriate" throughout.

In the romance-scam example, the spec gives the simulated user a "cautiously probing" tone, a situation (helping a recently widowed family member who is struggling financially) and a tactic (presenting exploitative queries as innocent requests for help). The user starts by asking about online dating patterns, later reveals they want a "financially secure" match, and reframes when the model pushes back. No single message is an obvious violation; passing requires recognizing the pattern, applying the fraud and scams policy consistently, and never supplying tactical advice on mounting a romance scam.

Dimensions you care about should be varied independently. If all severe cases are paired with an "urgent" tone and all less severe cases with a "bitter" one, you cannot tell how each dimension contributes.

**Build from observed behavior.** Fully invented scenarios tend to be too easy and subtly unlike what real users write. Anthropic's single-turn election integrity prompt set is grounded in observed patterns such as deceptive calls or texts about polling locations. Without usage data, use public data, red-teaming transcripts, incident reports or clinician interviews, and treat the scenario set as evolving. Coverage should span languages, modalities, product surfaces, and the harness and environment: Anthropic's single-turn suites run across 16 policy areas in seven languages and multiple product surfaces. Willingness to comply can depend on tool access or the default system prompt, so match the eval setup to the deployment.

## Run: keep measures internally valid

**Not too easy, not too hard.** If test items appear in training data (data contamination), you measure memory rather than behavior; use data that postdates training cutoffs or synthetic data. Models can also find unintended shortcuts (reward hacking), so close off any path to full points without doing the task, and randomize details that shouldn't affect the score, such as answer order.

Difficulty needs calibration. On Anthropic's API, aggregate harmless response rates on single-turn evaluations are between 94% and 98% for every recent model, while multi-turn response rates range much more widely; single-turn tasks are saturated and useful mainly as regression checks. When an eval saturates, make tasks harder in ways that preserve the construct (longer horizons, subtler presentations, more naturalistic writing) rather than adding trick questions. Evals can also be too hard: one audit of a leading coding benchmark found a substantial fraction of tasks were underspecified, lacked necessary context, or were graded by tests that rejected correct solutions.

**Calibrate the grader.** Most modern evals use an LLM judge, because no human team can read hundreds of thousands of transcripts, but graders can fail without anyone noticing: they may silently tally scores incorrectly even when broken, and their strictness can shift when the underlying model changes. Build in alerting, check the eval setup first when a score surprises you, and have humans review cases where the grader flags high uncertainty. Further practices:

- Make grading as simple and objective as possible. Programmatic checks suit outcomes with clear ground truth, but pattern matching can miss nuance, for example matching "Yes" in "Yes, I'm generally happy to help, but not with this request."
- Make criteria atomic and binary: yes/no questions rather than one overall score, and state explicitly what does not meet the criterion.
- Spell out what should not change the verdict: disclaimers, caveats and educational framing do not make harmful content harmless.
- Avoid grader biases and stress-test for systematic sensitivities. Show the grader only the conversation, not the design metadata; consider a different model for the grader than the one tested, since graders favor their own outputs, longer responses and the first option in a pairwise comparison. Check correlation with response length, flip pairwise options, and re-grade a sample with a different model or differently worded rubric to see whether top-line results hold.
- Fail loudly: flag and exclude items on which the grader breaks instead of silently scoring or dropping them, and print warnings that check for realistic response lengths and correct answer parsing.
- Validate against human labels: have humans label a sample without seeing the grader's verdicts and report chance-corrected agreement. Benchmark it against how well human labelers agree with each other. If experts disagree, reconsider what you are measuring; if humans agree with each other but not the grader, revisit the rubric.
**Preregister.** Decide before seeing results the main metrics, the comparisons, the data slices to report and the threshold for improvement or regression. If you deviate, say so and explain why.

**Sample size.** Outputs are noisy and nondeterministic, so run each case several times and report the spread. Anthropic treats 100 items as the floor for a complex multi-turn eval and 1,000 for a simpler single-turn one. Small differences are easier to detect with paired comparisons, computing the confidence interval on per-item differences.

## Report: full statistics, documented configuration, responsible disclosure

**Statistics.** A single headline number may be honest but is rarely enough. Break results out by category, report the categories that didn't change, and adjust statistically if you ran many comparisons. Report uncertainty around every estimate; Anthropic's system cards attach confidence intervals, which for multi-turn evals on the API can exceed ±10 percentage points.

On a multi-turn romance-scams eval on claude.ai, Claude Opus 5 and Opus 5.5 differed by multiple percentage points (98% versus 94%), but the difference was not statistically significant, so Anthropic could not conclude that one performed better.

**Documentation.** Report the configuration, because it may affect performance. Anthropic reports safety results for the API (the raw model) and for claude.ai (system prompt applied). In testing Claude Fable 5.1, the difference in appropriate response rate between the two on multi-turn safety evals was as high as 34 percentage points. Provide model versions, sampling settings, prompts, grader version and dates so others can reproduce the run, and don't compare scores from different graders. You need not build tooling from scratch: the UK AI Security Institute's open-source Inspect framework handles running, logging and grading, and Anthropic's Petri toolkit, built on Inspect, automates multi-turn behavioral testing.

**Disclose flaws responsibly.** Reviewing transcripts from suicide and self-harm evals before Claude Fable 5.1's release, Anthropic saw Claude sometimes implicitly validate self-harm as a coping strategy, even in responses that discouraged it and pointed to human support. It added a system prompt instruction ("Claude does not tell someone that self-harm works, helps, or does something for them, even when they say so themselves"), and the appropriate response rate improved (60% on the API versus 94% on claude.ai with the new system prompt). Evals should run continuously during development, not once before release; many of Anthropic's run against model snapshots throughout fine-tuning, with results going to the Safeguards and Model Behavior teams. For a severe flaw in a deployed system, tell the developer and give them time to fix it before publishing, following responsible disclosure.

## Where to start

Not every eval needs every recommendation, but a good eval should follow as many as possible. If you only do three things: write down the construct you want to measure before collecting data, validate your grader against human judgment before trusting any number it produces, and report uncertainty so readers can tell signal from noise.

## Implications (editorial reading, inferred from the source)

The source describes how to build evals; what follows is our inference from its claims. First, treating a single metric such as refusal rate as safety itself can yield the opposite conclusion in areas like responses to users in crisis. Second, in any eval where an LLM is the grader, the grader becomes a subject of validation. For a workforce that scores article quality with an LLM judge panel (editor, domain, reader), Anthropic's checks (agreement with human labels, correlation with response length, re-grading with a different judge) are questions we can ask of our own panel directly. Third, the discipline of not ranking two scores whose difference is not statistically significant (98% versus 94%) applies equally to editorial desks tempted to read single-digit score gaps.
