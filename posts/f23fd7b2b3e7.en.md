A lab-hardware standard that cuts laser re-locking from 150 seconds to 6. A software playbook noting that faster coding has made planning and review the new bottleneck. And a warning that fake "Forward Deployed Engineer" postings can't be spotted by where someone sits, only by whether they own the whole work loop. On the surface these are three unrelated stories — research infrastructure, engineering process, and hiring. In fact they describe the same mechanism: the faster and cheaper AI makes any single execution step, the more visibly value and responsibility shift away from that step and toward whoever owns the entire loop it sits inside, start to finish.

## A dramatic speed-up in execution is evidence of a shortened loop, not just automation

The numbers in "Anthropic previews Model Hardware Standard (MHS) for AI agents to directly operate research equipment" are striking on their own. At QuEra Computing, re-locking a quantum computer's laser went from 150 seconds at 58% success by hand to 6–14 seconds at 99.3% success, using a script Claude autonomously refined overnight. Read in isolation, that looks like "execution got 25x faster."

But the same article reports a second number: at Carnegie Mellon, the time to integrate a new instrument dropped from weeks to eight hours. That isn't the speed-up of one task like laser re-locking — it's the compression of the entire integration loop: safely discovering the equipment, verifying operating permissions, handling failure states, and folding it into an existing workflow. That MHS launched as a research preview with both research institutions and manufacturing partners suggests the real target was never a single lab task but a cross-domain integration problem. What MHS provides as a standard isn't a "faster execution script" but a common interface for treating execution and everything around it — discovery, authentication, monitoring, shutdown — as one loop. The numbers describe less a triumph of automation than a handoff of who owns that whole loop.

## As code gets faster, the stages on either side of it become the real bottleneck

Anthropic's "The AI-Native SDLC playbook" (by Louis Claxton) states this shift more explicitly, in a software context. Drawing on its Applied AI team's findings, the piece argues that as Claude Code has made coding itself dramatically faster, the stages that still run at human speed — Plan, Design, Test/Review, Deploy, and Maintain — have surfaced as the new bottleneck. The article lays out concrete plays and governance for each of six stages (Plan, Design, Build, Test, Deploy, Maintain) with Claude Code woven in, but the diagnosis underneath is simple: speeding up the Build stage alone doesn't speed up the loop, if the stages flanking it stay slow.

This mirrors MHS from the other side. There, speeding up "execution" (laser re-locking) exposed the weight of the loop-side work (integration). Here, speeding up "execution" (writing code) exposes the weight of the loop's neighbors — planning, review, deployment. In neither case does faster execution by itself speed up the whole loop. It takes someone redesigning the loop end to end before a faster execution step translates into a better outcome.

## What separates a real FDE isn't where they sit, but whether they own the loop

The hiring-market observation pushes this principle up a level of abstraction. Postings claiming the title "Forward Deployed Engineer" (FDE) have proliferated, but FDE is neither a licensed role nor one with a rigorous industry-wide definition, so companies can attach the label to ordinary contract development or deployment-support work and the title alone won't stop them. Product-development specialist Shin (@shin_sasaki19), in an analysis shared on X, argues the test isn't whether someone is on-site at the client but whether they personally own the full loop: problem discovery → design → implementation → production deployment → adoption and outcome measurement → generalization → product improvement. Miss that one distinction, the analysis warns, and you'll overlook the real FDEs along with the fake ones.

What's notable is that Shin's test places "implementation" — writing the code — as just one of seven stages. Implementation is exactly the kind of step MHS's laser re-locking and the SDLC playbook's coding are: the one AI can substitute for fastest. That's why what actually distinguishes a genuine FDE isn't coding ability but ownership of the back half of the loop — verifying, through adoption and outcome measurement, that the thing actually got used and actually worked, then generalizing that knowledge back into product improvement — which is exactly why analyses of how OpenAI and Palantir define the role treat that back half as non-negotiable. A job posting can't easily state this distinction, because location is simply a cheaper proxy to measure.

## The unifying principle: faster execution makes loop ownership visible

All three cases share the same structure. First, in every domain, AI dramatically sped up and cut the cost of one execution step — re-locking a laser, writing code, and, potentially, implementation work generally. Second, that speed-up made the surrounding stages — integration; planning, review, and deployment; the loop from problem discovery to outcome measurement — relatively heavier, and visibly so. Third, that visibility didn't create new value; it exposed value that already existed but had been hidden behind the slowness of execution.

These facts suggest that measuring AI automation's effect purely by "the task got faster" misses the point. The real effect is second-order: as one execution step becomes commoditized, the scarcity of whoever can design, own, and verify the entire loop containing it — human or agent — rises in relative terms. This principle is stated so it can be tested: if AI-driven execution speed-ups simply lifted every stage equally, integration (MHS), planning and review (the SDLC playbook), and discovery-through-measurement (FDE) would never have been singled out as bottlenecks. That three independent observations each name "everything outside execution" as the new rate-limiting stage is exactly the evidence that supports this principle.

## Forecast and implications

If this principle holds, at least three consequences follow. First, MHS-style "standardized execution interfaces" will likely spread beyond research equipment into manufacturing, healthcare, and logistics — any domain with physical operations — and each expansion will likely repeat the same shift, from faster execution to loop-integration as the thing that matters. Second, the kind of "codified planning, review, and deployment" the SDLC playbook describes will probably stop being optional best practice and become a required governance layer for any organization whose execution has sped up. Third, in hiring, demand will grow — well beyond FDE roles — for ways to detect the gap between a job title and actual loop ownership, shifting interviews and evaluation away from location or title and toward a direct question: how much of the loop, from problem discovery to outcome measurement, has this person actually closed themselves?

The practical takeaway is straightforward. Separate the parts of your own work that AI can substitute for — execution — from the parts required to close the loop: integration, planning, verification, and ownership, and deliberately grow the weight of the latter. For anyone hiring or evaluating others, the shift from asking about execution speed or titles to asking how much of the loop a candidate has actually owned end to end will likely become a competitive dividing line before long.
