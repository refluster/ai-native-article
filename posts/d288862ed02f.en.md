## Executive Summary

A joint study by researchers from Duke University, Stanford Graduate School of Business, and Harvard Business School, among others, tracked the founders of 100 Indian technology companies for roughly two years. Firms whose founders received advice on formal people management ended up with 28% more employees than those that did not, saw their probability of business failure drop by 10 percentage points, and had survival rates rise from 75% to 85%. What produced the gap was not advanced HR technology but three unglamorous practices: setting explicit goals, checking progress on a regular cadence, and giving feedback that makes the next action obvious.

## What the two-year study of 100 firms actually measured

The source post cites "When Does Advice Impact Startup Performance?" by Aaron Chatterji, Solène M. Delecourt, Sharique Hasan, and Rembrand Koning (Strategic Management Journal version, DOI:10.1002/smj.2987). The research team followed the founders of 100 Indian technology companies for about two years, tracking growth and survival.

The reported differences reduce to three figures:

- Headcount: firms advised by founders practising formal people management ended up **28% larger**
- Probability of business failure: **down 10 percentage points**
- Survival rate: **from 75% to 85%**
What is being measured here is not whether someone is a good manager but **whether they received advice about a specific set of management practices**. The intervention targeted procedure, not founder calibre — and that is the design point that matters. Procedures transfer. That is why advice moved the outcome at all.

## The three unglamorous practices growing firms actually ran

None of the three, as the post organises them, is "an AI-driven performance review or a morning assembly that moves people to tears."

**1. Make the goal explicit.** "I'd like you to grow revenue" or "I'd like you to be more proactive" are not goals but impressions, the post argues. A goal needs at least three components: what gets completed, by when, and what counts as done. "I'll push hard on new business this month" has no completion condition; "contact 30 prospects by month-end and generate 5 sales conversations" defines the work, the deadline, and the passing line.

**2. Check progress on a cadence.** The post likens the progress check to a rendezvous point on a mountain climb. Even on separate routes, you meet at a fixed time, confirm where you are, correct the path, and continue. Without a rendezvous point, you discover the problem only once you are already lost. Four items are enough: what has been completed since last time, what is in progress, what is stalled, and what will be finished by the next check. The requirement is not meeting length but **whether it was settled who does what next**.

**3. Give feedback that names the next action.** Feedback is neither praise nor reprimand, the post argues, but putting the other person in a state where the next move is clear. "That deck was hard to follow" is a personal impression and changes no behaviour. By contrast: "the conclusion didn't appear until page 5, which made it hard for the reader to decide; next time put the conclusion and the decision you want on page 1" carries the three necessary parts — what happened, what resulted, and what to change next.

What the three share is that each produces a state in which **the recipient's next move is uniquely determined**. The goal fixes the passing line, the progress check fixes the current position, the feedback fixes what changes. Read another way: ambiguity is removed three times over.

## Frequency was the variable — weekly, not monthly

The most concrete element in the post's account of the research concerns frequency. Founders **advised to set goals and give feedback weekly rather than monthly saw larger subsequent company growth**.

The claim is that the period of the practice, not the kind of practice, moved the result. Two firms can run all three and still differ: with a long interval, "it had actually stalled halfway," "the other side never replied," and "I didn't know how, so I left it" all surface at once at month-end. On a weekly cadence, the problem gets corrected while it is still small.

The following is an implication drawn from the source rather than a conclusion of the study: if frequency is what pays, the value of these three practices may lie less in the quality of the instruction than in **the speed at which error is detected**. Goals, checks, and feedback are instrumentation for finding drift early and correcting it cheaply — not a device for getting the plan right on the first attempt.

## Where "delegating" stops and "abandoning" begins

At this point the obvious objection is micromanagement. The post draws the line cleanly: **continuously intervening in how the work is done is micromanagement; aligning the goal and the current position is a progress check.**

A firm that delegates sets the goal, checks progress along the way, asks what is blocking, reviews the result, and decides the next action. A firm that abandons hands over the work, does not check until the deadline, assigns blame once something goes wrong, and closes the matter with "they weren't capable enough." In the post's phrasing, handing over work and never checking until the day it is due "is not management. It is close to a game of chance."

The distinguishing criterion is not the frequency of intervention but **its target**. Intervening in method removes discretion; synchronising the goal and the current position sets up the preconditions for discretion. The same weekly meeting can be management or surveillance depending on what it handles.

## The reach of this finding, and its limits

The post carries its own caveat, and it is worth recording: "this study covered 100 firms, and it is not a claim that doing these three things will necessarily produce 28% growth."

Three constraints matter for a reader. First, the population is founders of Indian technology companies; the research guarantees no generalisation to other regions, other sectors, or large enterprises. Second, the sample is 100 firms, and the reported differences are average tendencies rather than per-firm guarantees. Third, the primary input for this piece is a post on X: the figures — 28%, 10 percentage points, 75% to 85% — and the account of weekly cadence are that post's summary of the research. For the original, see the DOI above.

What survives those limits is the point the post closes on: the scope is not confined to organisations. What am I completing by when, how far along am I now, what do I change next — a state in which those three are undecided has the same structure whether the subject is a direct report or oneself. An operation that sets goals but never checks position is equivalent in exactly one respect: the check is missing.
