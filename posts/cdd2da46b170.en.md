## Executive Summary

In a recent survey, 85% of organizations said they “want to become agentic within 3 years,” while 76% said “their current operations and infrastructure cannot support that change.” At the heart of this article, based on reporting by MIT Technology Review Insights with practitioners from Ema and PwC, is the idea that many companies are simply layering AI agents on top of operating models designed for humans. Prasun Shah of PwC UK describes this as putting “sticky tape” on a broken business model. The article presents **Agentic Business Transformation (ABT)**, a term advanced by Ema, as a framework for rebuilding organizations around three pillars: the technology stack, the workforce, and the metrics used to measure success.

## The “sticky tape problem” — Why layering agents onto existing models destroys value

The article begins by quantifying the gap between ambition and execution. While 85% of organizations want to shift to an agentic model within the next 3 years, 76% say their current operations and infrastructure cannot support that transition because they are not prepared in terms of talent, processes, and workflows.

Prasun Shah, global CTO and chief AI officer for workforce consulting at PwC UK Consulting, explains the cause this way: organizations are not reimagining the operating model itself; instead, they are piling AI agents on top of existing work. In his words, companies are “embedding AI employees into operating models designed for humans,” which is “like applying sticky tape to parts of a broken operating model.”

- This kind of layering prevents organizations from realizing the true value of agentic AI and brings on disillusionment faster.
- That true value lies in agents’ ability to “execute complete workflows with limited human intervention.” They can coordinate complex tasks, make decisions independently, adapt to changing circumstances, and iteratively improve performance.
- In early proof-of-value areas such as customer service, HR, and sales, BCG estimates that at scale, AI agents can reduce process time by 30–50% and cut time spent on low-value work by 25–40% (as cited in the article). But that capability also comes with greater complexity and the need for company-wide transformation.
## The vocabulary of “ABT” — Why the existing language falls short

Ema, an enterprise agentic AI platform company, calls this shift **Agentic Business Transformation (ABT)**. The term was coined last year with HFS Research to fill a gap in the existing vocabulary around AI agents and provide companies with a new framework for thinking about technology adoption.

Surojit Chatterjee, Ema’s CEO and founder, explains why the language needs to change: “None of the existing vocabulary captures the full picture of this shift. Digital transformation was the move from paper to software. AI transformation meant adding artificial intelligence to existing processes. Co-pilot means AI assisting people with various tasks. But ABT is a different category altogether — it means integrating AI agents into the fabric of the organization.”

For Shah, this dedicated term — ABT — helps “drive the need to redesign the organization as a whole — the operating model, workflows, decision rights, and performance management systems.” What he emphasizes is “everything required to make agents not just point tools or productivity aids, but active participants in value creation.”

According to Ema, ABT consists of 3 core pillars: the organization’s technology stack, its workforce, and the metrics by which success is measured.

## Pillar 1: AI agents as connective tissue

The first pillar is the technology stack. Chatterjee says: “Your existing technology stack is designed around application-centric workflows operated by humans. When the actor becomes an AI agent operating in parallel across multiple systems at machine speed, that stack has to be rebuilt.”

According to Shah, organizations need to shift away from linear processes and collections of steps toward something fundamentally different: the “rewiring” of work itself. That is because the value of AI agents does not come from being “another layer” in the existing tech stack, but from acting as the “connective tissue” across and between layers — coordinating high-level tasks, and retrieving and interpreting data from multiple discrete applications. By making decisions based on the ability to contextualize, AI agents can create “true competitive differentiation” for businesses. “That is the next battleground,” he says.

To build this connective tissue, leaders need to adapt the technology stack so AI agents can make higher-quality decisions, and prioritize simultaneous access to multiple datasets and applications in order to cultivate tacit knowledge. “Organizations that make this architectural shift become truly adaptive,” Chatterjee says. “When a new business requirement emerges, they do not need to wait 6 months for a software vendor to build a feature. They can configure an AI employee in natural language and connect it to the necessary systems. The time from business requirement to production workflow shrinks from months to days.”

## Pillar 2: A redesigned workforce structure

As use cases for AI agents expand, leaders need to think through the implications for workforce dynamics — the second pillar of ABT.

Today’s workforce structures have changed little from the hierarchical models of early industrialization. Processes are standardized to maximize efficiency and scale, tasks are clearly divided across strategic business units (SBUs), and employees are promoted based on their ability to optimize the output of lower-level teams. But once AI agents enter the picture — agents that can execute, coordinate, and optimize tasks, often without managerial coordination — the lines of that established hierarchy begin to blur.

- In a workforce structure where AI agents and humans coexist, managers are freed from much of the work tied to execution, while taking on a new responsibility: managing hybrid teams. According to Shah, managers must be able to deal with “issues around trust, explainability, psychological safety, and even status dynamics.”
- The impact extends beyond management. McKinsey predicts that by 2030, three-quarters of current jobs will require redesign, reskilling, or redeployment, and says organizations need to move quickly to rethink hiring, retention, and compensation.
## Pillar 3: From output to outcome

The third and final pillar is success metrics. As AI agents take on more ownership of core operations and work collaboratively with humans, traditional metrics focused on activity and output — such as the number of calls handled or reports submitted — stop making sense.

Chatterjee says: “Once you add AI employees to the workforce structure, activity metrics become meaningless or actively misleading. AI employees can handle 1,000 customer interactions in the time it takes a human to handle 10. If you measure success by the number of interactions processed, you can conclude that AI is performing brilliantly while missing whether any of those interactions actually led to customer satisfaction, retention, or revenue.”

To correct this, companies need to create a new set of metrics focused not on output but on outcome. These are metrics that measure the broader benefit or change achieved, rather than counting individual deliverables.

- As one example, a large enterprise customer of Ema revised its metrics, shifting away from “tool metrics” such as cost per query and AI accuracy, and toward outcomes such as “the percentage of contracts reviewed without human escalation.” As a result, the ROI measured from agentic AI tripled in 2 quarters. According to Chatterjee, this customer “stopped building point solutions for high-volume, low-complexity workflows and started placing AI employees where the outcome value was highest.”
- Shah notes that introducing new metrics may also require a full reconfiguration of compensation, talent management processes, and accountability and ownership across the organization. In mixed teams of humans and AI, ethical and fiduciary responsibility remains with humans, while operational accountability becomes much more distributed in ways that reflect the structural role of AI agents.
## Conclusion: Laying the groundwork for system-level change

This shift toward outcomes raises a new set of questions that senior leaders will need to wrestle with. Shah offers examples: when an AI employee makes a mistake, who is responsible? What happens when AI and human judgment diverge? What guardrails should be put in place to protect customers?

The article’s conclusion is restrained but clear. System-level change is incremental, and it remains a complex area of inquiry that even experts are still working through. But by starting internal conversations around the core pillars of ABT — the workforce, the technology stack, and success metrics — leaders can begin preparing their organizations to absorb AI agents at the system level and start closing the gap between ambition and execution.

One note of caution for readers is worth making explicit here. This article was produced by MIT Technology Review’s custom content division, “Insights,” and not by the editorial newsroom. It relies primarily on the views of interested parties: Ema, which advocates the ABT framework, and practitioners from PwC, whose business is consulting. That does not invalidate the argument, but it does mean readers should discount accordingly. Even so, the core claim — don’t tape agents onto the existing org chart; rebuild the operating model, workforce, and metrics across those 3 layers — is a hypothesis worth testing for organizations putting AI agents into real-world operation.
