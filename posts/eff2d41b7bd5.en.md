The biggest gains do not come from doing something better. They come the moment you decide not to do something at all. Three events that seem unrelated at first glance—Databricks declaring that it will dissolve the boundary between transactions and analytics, Elon Musk’s five-step process centered on “delete before you optimize,” and résumé data showing that Anthropic’s 1,680 engineers are not PhD researchers but “builders”—are, in fact, all explained by a single underlying principle. The source of value is moving from refining what exists within given constraints to eliminating the constraints themselves.

## Question What Is Supposedly Worth Refining—The False Binary Databricks Removed

What L2’s “Beyond the Database: What Databricks Envisions” reported was not a story of optimization, but of deletion. At its Data + AI Summit, the company unveiled its LTAP (Lake Transactional/Analytical Processing) vision, which integrates transaction processing (OLTP) and analytical processing (OLAP) on a single copy of data. Analyst Brad Shimmin (Futurum) positioned this as the dismantling of the “strict binary” that has defined data management architecture for decades.

What matters here is that Databricks did not simply make data movement between two systems “faster.” It removed the premise that those systems had to be separate in the first place. Why? The article’s stated motivation is clear: to resolve, at the infrastructure level, the practical bottleneck that agents cannot write back into core systems. In other words, the long-assumed structure of separating OLTP and OLAP had itself become a shackle in the age of agents. The plumbing everyone thought needed optimization turned out not to need to exist at all—that is the first example.

## Sequence Has Value—Musk’s “Algorithm”

Why is “deleting what should not exist” so effective? The clearest answer appears in L2’s “Elon Musk’s ‘Algorithm.’” Musk’s five-step process, repeated at SpaceX and Tesla, derives its value less from the steps themselves than from their order, the article argues.

Most engineers begin with optimization, acceleration, or automation. Musk puts “question every requirement” and “delete” before any of that. The core idea is distilled in Musk’s own words: “The most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist.”

That single sentence sheds light on the Databricks case from another angle. The decades spent silently accepting the OLTP/OLAP binary and endlessly accelerating the plumbing between the two were, precisely, a case of “optimizing a thing that should not exist.” What Musk’s process shows is that deletion and optimization are not equivalent options. They have a clear sequence. First question. Then delete. Only then optimize what remains. Reverse the order, and you end up polishing the very thing you should have removed.

### Automation Comes Last

It is also telling that automation appears as the final step in Musk’s process. Automation through AI and agents, unless it comes after confirming that the target is something that should exist, becomes a mechanism for reproducing mistakes at speed. Automation that skips deletion risks becoming the most sophisticated form of waste.

## Question the “Requirements” Themselves—Anthropic’s Hiring Data

The third item, L2’s “The 1,680 People Anthropic Is Actually Hiring,” shows that this principle applies not only to technical architecture and process, but also to organizational “requirements.” An analysis published by recruiter seb in 2026年6月 examined the backgrounds of 1,680 actual engineers among the 5,306 people on LinkedIn listing Anthropic as their current employer.

The conclusion: not researchers, but builders. Only 13.7% held PhDs, the median pre-Anthropic experience was 12.2 years, and the largest source of talent was not competing AI labs, but Google. The common image of a frontier AI lab as a research institution “filled with PhDs” is, at least at the engineering layer, out of step with reality.

This, too, is a story of deletion. Anthropic is effectively removing the hiring requirement the industry has implicitly optimized around: “You need a PhD to build frontier AI.” Rather than refining a metric like PhD ratio, it questioned the assumption that this metric should be a requirement at all. Borrowing Musk’s framing, the “PhD requirement” was a requirement that should not have existed, and optimizing it—hiring more PhDs—would amount to the kind of mistake smart recruiters make.

The fact that the largest source of talent was Google, not competing AI labs, follows the same logic. Another implicit requirement—“frontier talent can only be hired from cutting-edge labs”—has also been removed. Once you question each requirement one by one, what remains is neither title nor prestige of prior employer, but implementation experience as a builder, reflected in the median 12.2 years of pre-hire experience. That is the one thing Anthropic sharpened after deleting everything else.

## The Shared Principle—Deletion Precedes Optimization

Put these three facts side by side, and a single principle emerges. They suggest that the source of competitive advantage is shifting from “optimization within constraints” to “deletion of the constraints themselves.” Databricks removed a boundary in data architecture, Musk removed assumptions embedded in process, and Anthropic removed requirements in hiring. The layers differ, but in each case progress came from stripping away structures that had long been treated as givens.

Why is this principle moving to the foreground now? One interpretation is that optimization has become cheaper than ever. The work of refining what exists inside a given constraint—speeding up plumbing, shortening code, improving metrics—is being rapidly commoditized by AI. When optimization becomes universally accessible, the scarce capability shifts to judging which constraints should be removed. The more optimization is mechanized, the more value moves upward to the layer above it: questioning assumptions.

What must be emphasized here is sequence. In all three cases, deletion comes before optimization. Databricks removed the binary and then refined the integrated foundation. Musk deletes requirements and only then automates. Anthropic abandons the PhD requirement and only then raises the quality of its builders. Deletion and optimization are not equal alternatives. Deletion always comes first—that is the falsifiable core of this principle. If organizations that “optimize first and delete later” continue to win, then the principle is wrong.

## Predictions and Implications

If this principle is correct, then several changes can be predicted with fairly high confidence.

First, competition in products and architecture is likely to be decided not by “feature optimization” but by “assumption deletion.” If Databricks’s bet pays off, the winner will not be the company that bridged OLTP and OLAP the fastest, but the company that erased the boundary first. Similar “boundary deletion” will likely spread beyond the data layer into areas such as authentication, billing, and permissions—domains that have long been treated as separate components. Confidence here is moderate: technical integration comes with migration costs, and there will of course be domains where the binary remains rational.

Second, in organizations and hiring, “taking inventory of requirements” will become a source of competitive advantage. If the Anthropic case generalizes, the winning teams will not be those that optimally gather people who satisfy existing requirements, but those that discard unnecessary requirements first. PhDs, years of experience in a specific language, tenure at a particular company—organizations that ask whether these are “requirements that should exist in the first place” will effectively expand their talent pool.

For practitioners, the implication can be reduced to one point. Whenever you feel the urge to optimize or automate something, follow Musk’s sequence first. “Should this process, this boundary, this requirement exist at all?” In an era when optimization can be delegated to AI, the most valuable work left to humans is not choosing what to refine, but deleting what never needed refinement in the first place. That is the first move if you want to avoid creating the most sophisticated kind of waste.
