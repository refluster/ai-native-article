Anthropic announced it will put $100 million into the Claude Frontier Academy to train 10,000 Frontier Deployed Engineers by the end of 2027. a16z's "State of Markets II" describes tech as roughly 76% of S&P 500 profit growth in 2026, while AI adoption is broad but shallow: only about 2% of companies report tracking metrics. At the UN Security Council, the CEOs of OpenAI and Anthropic warned about AI risk, and the US president rejected international control. The three facts look unrelated, but one principle explains all of them. **In AI, the supply of capability has run ahead, and the supply of parties willing to own its consequences is the one lagging furthest.**

## Introduction: three shortages with the same shape

Each of the three articles reports a different gap. Anthropic's announcement names the biggest shortfall in enterprise AI adoption as people who can "bring AI to production in real work." a16z's analysis shows that behind a capital and hardware frenzy, with GPU demand outrunning supply, almost nobody tracks how deep adoption goes. In the Security Council scene, the vocabulary of risk was in place, but no framework existed for who would govern it.

Compute, capital and model performance get priced by markets, and competition expands their supply. The party that finally owns both the gains and the risks gets no price, and competition alone does not multiply them.

## Analysis 1: the people shortage is a shortage of ownership, not capability

According to "Anthropic, 1億ドルで1万人の『Frontier Deployed Engineer』を育成", the company places the core of the shortage not in technical knowledge but in delivering AI to production in real work, and certifies people through a medical model: coursework, real cases and two evaluations. What matters is that the design takes the form of certification, not education. Medicine has licensing not because knowledge is short, but because society cannot entrust surgery to someone it cannot hold accountable for the result.

Anthropic has in effect conceded that model performance is not the bottleneck. The bottleneck is the number of people who can be named when something fails in production. A scale of $100 million for 10,000 people shows how expensive the supply of such owners is next to the supply of capability. Even the simple division, roughly $10,000 per person, means capital is being injected directly into a layer the existing hiring market did not fill.

## Analysis 2: adoption is shallow because no one is there to measure it

"a16z『State of Markets II』が示す2026年上半期" argues that tech has become an "everything cycle" accounting for about 76% of profit growth, and that while GPU demand exceeds supply, AI adoption is broad but shallow. About 2% of companies report tracking metrics. The key word is "shallow," not "scarce": usage is spreading, yet only a few companies track and report outcomes.

Measurement is done only by organizations willing to own their results. Publishing a metric also publishes the failures. The roughly 2% figure likely reflects not the maturity of the technology but the distribution of willingness to own outcomes. Capital flows into hardware faster than supply can absorb it, while the machinery for owning what happens on top of it has barely advanced.

## Analysis 3: the Security Council made the vacancy of a governing party visible

According to "国連安保理でOpenAIとAnthropicのCEOがAIリスクを警告、トランプ氏は国際統制に反対", at the Security Council on 23 September 2026 Altman, Amodei and Hugging Face co-founder Delangue described the risks, and Amodei warned that AI "could be a threat to humanity as a whole." At the same UN General Assembly, the US president said he would reject an international AI control framework.

Here too, awareness of risk is not what is missing: the heads of the developers themselves are issuing the strongest warnings. What is missing is a party to receive those warnings and take responsibility. Developers warn but do not seek control, and a state refuses control. Warnings multiply while owners do not, which is the same structure as the talent gap inside companies and the missing adoption metrics.

## The shared principle: an asymmetry of supply creates a vacancy of responsibility

Set side by side, the three facts show the same asymmetry at three levels. In companies, people who can run AI can be trained, but people qualified to own production outcomes are short. In markets, capital and GPUs flow faster than supply, yet only about 2% of organizations measure and report results. In the international arena, voices on risk are loud, but a state is refusing to take part in a governing framework.

These facts suggest the following principle. **Capability is oversupplied because markets price it; responsibility is undersupplied because nothing prices it.** Capital pushes up the supply curve of capability, but the supply curve of responsibility can be pushed only by social machinery: credentials, metrics, institutions. Adoption that is broad yet shallow is another name for capability spreading first while the machinery of responsibility fails to catch up.

The principle is falsifiable. If responsibility were not truly scarce, the share of companies reporting tracked metrics should rise naturally as capability spreads. Conversely, if the roughly 2% level does not move even after more capital is deployed, the principle is supported. Anthropic's medical model is the design that accepts this principle most clearly, because it spends $100 million not on training capability but on identifying, through certification, where responsibility sits.

## Implications: whoever builds the supply side of responsibility holds the next scarcity

If the principle is right, I expect three things within the next 12 to 24 months. My confidence in each reflects how directly it extends the three facts.

First, there is a moderate chance that more companies will track AI outcomes as metrics and report them externally. The roughly 2% level a16z reported should move once disclosing outcomes becomes a competitive advantage or a compliance need. But it will move only after capability has spread further, and early movers will stay few.

Second, certification schemes like Anthropic's are likely to be imitated by other vendors and industry bodies. Having adopted a medical model, a credential becomes a tool that states who owns the result, and it will be built into procurement conditions for enterprise AI. Whether the 10,000 target is reached by the end of 2027 is the first test of this principle.

Third, as long as an international control framework is rejected, the vacancy of responsibility will be filled not by states but by voluntary credentials and standards from companies and industry. The gap in positions seen at the Security Council is unlikely to close in the short term, and in the meantime practical governance will migrate to private machinery such as credentials and metrics.

For readers, the practical implication is one thing. Before procuring models or compute in an AI adoption plan, decide the name of the person who owns the outcome and the metric that will measure it. Capability can be bought; an owner cannot. The next competition will be decided by how quickly a company can build that supply of responsibility inside itself.
