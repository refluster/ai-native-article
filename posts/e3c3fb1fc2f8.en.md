## Executive Summary

SAP, one of the world's biggest software companies, has suspended most travel and hiring since last month because of AI's soaring cost, according to an internal SAP email obtained by 404 Media. The only exceptions are for travel or hires that are themselves AI-related. Bloomberg had already reported on the email and the freezes back in July, but a current SAP employee says the policy is still in effect as of August.

## What was frozen

According to the internal SAP email obtained by 404 Media, SAP says it needs to "be disciplined in how we spend," and that discipline includes continuing to freeze hires and travel. The only carve-out is for anything tied to AI. Bloomberg had already reported on the freeze in July, but a current SAP employee told 404 Media the bans are "still in effect." 404 Media granted the employee anonymity to protect them from retaliation.

## What was said internally

The employee said SAP held a global employee meeting recently at which the freeze was raised again. At the same time, SAP is in the middle of rolling out a newly created AI tool to the entire company. Of that rollout, the employee said it is something "which I can only imagine massively increases the costs." In other words, the very AI tool SAP is pushing company-wide may be adding to the cost pressure the company says it is trying to contain — a contradiction flagged by someone inside the company itself.

## Not just an SAP story

404 Media frames the email as evidence that "companies both big and small are coming to grips with the reality of AI's cost." Rather than being the sweeping cost-saver it was pitched as, the report says AI is in some cases forcing companies to throttle employees' AI use as spending spirals out of control, sending them scrambling for other ways to keep costs down.

## What it means

SAP's case shows that adopting AI is not simply "efficiency" — it is generating a distinct kind of pressure on corporate cost structures. Freezing something as fundamental as travel and hiring in order to protect AI-related spending signals that AI investment is starting to compete directly with existing resource allocation. And the fact that this information only surfaced through a leaked internal email says something on its own: companies are not eager to discuss the real state of their AI costs in public. That a company the size of SAP treats AI spending as sanctioned while tightening nearly everything else is a signal worth noting for any organization weighing its own AI investment — the cost conversation is worth having in financial terms early, not after the freeze memo goes out.
