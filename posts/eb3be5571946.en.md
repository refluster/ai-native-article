The cheaper generative AI makes intelligence, the more value shifts from “the model itself” to the judgment required to wire that model into production. That single sentence runs through three facts that seem unrelated. Netflix says it chooses systems thinkers over specialists in hiring; reporting on finance departments says the success or failure of agent adoption is determined not by how smart the model is, but by how operations are designed; and in the AI market, downloads have shifted from the newest, strongest models to open models from China. In fact, all three can be explained by the same principle. The more generation becomes surplus, the more the non-surplus resource—the judgment to wire it in—acquires price.

## Hiring Criteria Shift from “People Who Make” to “People Who Connect”

According to “Why Netflix Hires ‘Systems Thinkers’ Over ‘Specialists’ — Organizational Design in the AI Era,” the company’s CPTO, Elizabeth Stone, identified “systems thinking” as the hiring skill she values most in the age of AI. Hiring for cross-domain integrative ability rather than narrow specialization, treating AI literacy as a company-wide baseline, and operationalizing excellence as a system—taken together, these points indicate the declining relative status of the ability to produce outputs.

### Why Narrow Specialization Is Losing Value

Generative AI can now produce at high volume and low cost the kinds of outputs that once only specialists could create—code, copy, analytical drafts. Once the capacity to make becomes abundant, what becomes scarce is the judgment to decide “where and how to connect it.” That article distilled exactly this into a single line: “The cheaper generation becomes, the scarcer the judgment to wire it becomes than the ability to make.” Netflix hiring integrators over specialists is not a moral preference. It is resource allocation based on recognizing which resources are abundant and which are not.

## What Decides Winners and Losers Is Not Model Intelligence but Operational Design

The article “Prerequisites for Introducing Agentic AI into Finance and Accounting” describes the same shift from inside the organization. Its core point is that the real contender for agentic AI in finance and accounting is not a general-purpose tool, but a specialized agent built around business context. And in Deloitte research, 48% of finance professionals who play a central strategic role had already introduced AI agents, far above the 18% among those in more supporting roles.

### The Gap Came Not from “Intelligence” but from “Wiring”

What matters is what produced that 48% versus 18% gap. The article is explicit: what separates successful from unsuccessful adoption is not the intelligence of the model itself, but the redesign of the entire operating model, including a clean data foundation, auditable data lineage, and human validation. In other words, even when given the same model, the group that could wire it into a verifiable workflow won, while the group that simply handed everything over lost. Here too, what is scarce is not the model, but the judgment required to connect the model to a trustworthy operational setup. The strategically central cohort moved first because they were in the position to design the wiring.

## The Main Theater of Competition Is Shifting from “The Smartest Model” to “The Model That Handles Most Real Work”

The third article, “The Main Theater of AI Competition Is Moving from the ‘Frontier’ to the ‘Production Floor’ — Hugging Face Shows the Reversal of Open Models,” makes the same principle visible at market scale. In spring 2026, 41% of downloads on Hugging Face were Chinese open-weight models, surpassing those from the United States. On OpenRouter, the top 6 most-used models were all open models from Chinese companies, while Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.7 ranked only 7th.

### “Who Is the Smartest” Is No Longer the Main Axis of Competition

The article’s conclusion is straightforward. The focus of AI competition is shifting from “who has the smartest model” to “which model handles the majority of real-world operations.” The model at the peak of the frontier does not necessarily carry most production workloads. Rather, open models that are smart enough, cheap enough, and easy to wire into one’s own business are increasingly taking over the production floor. Here too, value is moving away from the peak of “ability to make,” represented by maximum performance, toward the total volume that gets wired into real operations.

## A Common Principle — Surplus Resources Lose Value, and Wiring Becomes the Scarce Asset

These three facts suggest a single principle: **the cheaper and more abundant intelligence and output become, the more value shifts from the “model/specialist” that produces them to the judgment required to wire them into trustworthy production.** As basic economics would predict, resources that become surplus lose their price, while the resources that remain at the bottleneck capture it. The three articles each isolate, from different settings, the same structure between resources that have become surplus (general-purpose generative capability, the latest and strongest models, specialized making power) and resources that remain bottlenecks (integration judgment, operational design, connection to production use).

Why this shift happens can be deduced from the nature of AI itself. Generative AI is an output machine with marginal cost close to zero, and intelligence is rapidly commoditized through replication, distribution, and open release. The 41% figure on Hugging Face demonstrates that even frontier-level intelligence can be followed and diffused within months. By contrast, deciding “on top of which data lineage,” “with whose validation in between,” and “into which business process” to connect that output is not something that can be replicated. A data foundation, the human judgment that performs validation, and the integrative capacity to hold the whole picture across domains are all organization-specific assets that a competitor cannot obtain simply by buying the same model. The integrative capacity Netflix talks about and the operating-model redesign described in the finance article are simply two names for the same non-replicable wiring. That is why the makers become abundant while the connectors become scarce.

So what? This principle flips what organizations should invest in and what people should learn. Having a smarter model, or mastering a narrower specialty, does not by itself create a defensible advantage. Advantage resides in the ability to wire intelligence into forms that are verifiable, auditable, and connected to business operations. This claim is falsifiable. If frontier models once again come to dominate production share, and the winning strategy becomes not building the wiring but simply handing everything over to “the one smartest model,” then this principle should be rejected. All three articles point in the opposite direction.

## Forecast and Implications — The “Wiring Layer” Will Become the Next Scarce Function

If this principle is correct, the situation will change in the following ways.

First, hiring and evaluation standards will increasingly tilt, across organizations, toward “wiring capability” (high probability). The basis for this is Netflix’s explicit shift in policy, and the fact that it is not a one-off move but one accompanied by institutionalization—operating excellence “as a system.” This does not mean specialization itself becomes unnecessary. It means the relative value of specialists who cannot connect their expertise across domains will decline. Over the next 1–2 years, I expect to see more roles whose titles include terms like “integration,” “orchestration,” and “validation.”

Second, the success or failure of agent adoption will shift upstream from “model selection” to “building data lineage and validation flows” (high probability). The reason is that the 48% versus 18% gap in the finance article is explained not by differences in models, but by the presence or absence of operational design. Organizations that do not have a clean data foundation and auditable lineage before adoption will remain on the 18% side no matter how smart a model they buy.

Third, the model market will disperse not toward “one strongest model dominates” but toward “many models that are smart enough” (medium probability). The evidence is the reversal seen in OpenRouter, where the top 6 are all open models and Claude Opus 4.7 remains in 7th place, though regulation and safety requirements could still reinforce frontier concentration. That is why this is only medium probability. To add one point: this does not mean frontier research loses its value. Research that pushes up the frontier will continue. The claim is that the place where the intelligence created there is captured as profit will shift from selling the model to designing the wiring.

The practical implication converges on a single point. What is scarce in your organization is no longer access to the latest model. The resource that will carry price next is the judgment required to wire intelligence into verifiable production—on top of which data, with whose validation in between, and into which business process. Those who invest people and design into that layer will be the ones who win in an age of surplus.
