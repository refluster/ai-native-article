In a world where execution has become almost infinitely cheap, what separates outcomes is not how fast you can run a given structure, but whether you can question that structure itself and redraw it.

At first glance, there are three unrelated stories here: Anatoli Kopadze’s observations about how people use AI, the five-step process Elon Musk repeats on the factory floor, and Databricks’ LTAP vision for the data stack. The subjects could hardly be more different—individual work habits, the manufacturing of rockets and EVs, and enterprise data architecture. But in fact, all three can be explained by the same principle. Each argues that the real point of divergence in results lies not in “optimization within the framework as it exists,” but in “dismantling the framework itself.”

## Before “running it faster,” change “how it runs”

What AI “used in loops” exposes is a problem of operating model. Kopadze’s claim is simple and sharp: AI has been in everyone’s hands for years, and yet most people who use it every day still rely on the slowest possible pattern by hand—“type a request → wait → revise → ask again.” The core of this point, which drew 9.5M views, is the observation that the axis of advantage has shifted from “access” to “how you run the loop.”

What matters here is that he is not saying, “write better prompts.” That would only be optimization inside the existing loop—a structure where a human enters instructions one by one, waits for the result, and fixes it manually. What Kopadze is pointing to is a level above that: reconfiguring the shape of the loop itself. The line “the problem is not tool performance but the operating model” clearly separates improving performance (optimization) from changing the model (structural dismantling). If people with access to the same model are producing sharply different results, the reading here is that the difference lies not in tool performance but in whether they changed the model.

## Put “question it, delete it” before optimization

“Elon Musk’s ‘algorithm’” formulates the same distinction as a matter of sequence. The five-step process Musk repeats at SpaceX and Tesla is valuable less for the steps themselves than for their order. Most people start with optimization, acceleration, and automation. Musk starts by putting “question every requirement” and “delete” first.

That order is the point. The core is distilled in Musk’s own words: “The most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist.” Optimization, acceleration, and automation only make sense if the thing being worked on is something that ought to exist in the first place. And yet many highly capable practitioners rush straight to making a given process run faster without ever testing that premise. What Musk’s process forces into view is a priority of structural dismantling: before you optimize anything, question the reason it exists at all, and if it is unnecessary, delete it. Kopadze’s “change the model” points in the same direction—before polishing the inside of the existing frame, interrogate the frame itself.

## Databricks erases the dichotomy itself

The third example, “what Databricks sees beyond the database,” demonstrates this principle at the largest scale. The LTAP (Lake Transactional/Analytical Processing) vision unveiled at Data + AI Summit aims to unify transactional processing (OLTP) and analytical processing (OLAP) on a single copy of the data.

Analyst Brad Shimmin (Futurum) frames this as a move to dismantle the “strict dichotomy” between transactions and analytics that has defined data management architecture for decades. What deserves attention is that Databricks did not choose optimization within each layer—“make OLTP faster” or “make OLAP faster.” Its aim is to remove, at the infrastructure level, a practical bottleneck: agents cannot write back into systems of record. In other words, rather than increasing the performance of two separate layers, it erased the boundary line that separated them in the first place. Here again, what was chosen was not optimization but the dismantling of a premise. And the fact that this was triggered by the arrival of a new kind of actor—agents that cannot merely read but must write back—will matter later.

## The shared principle: the bottleneck is not performance, but structure

Set these three facts side by side, and a single principle comes into view. In personal workflow, Kopadze; in engineering procedure, Musk; and in data architecture, Databricks all reject “making things faster inside the existing structure” (optimization) and place the source of results in “questioning and dismantling the structure, premises, and boundaries themselves.” Together, these cases suggest that the real bottleneck separating outcomes lies not on the side of performance, but on the side of structure.

Why is this principle coming to the foreground now? The key is that AI has made “execution” almost infinitely cheap. Once the cost of optimization, acceleration, and automation—that is, running a defined process faster—falls low enough, the bottleneck shifts from execution speed to “what should be executed in the first place,” or structural design. The cheaper execution becomes, the more the cost of running a flawed structure at high speed compounds. That is why the mistake Musk describes—“optimize a thing that should not exist”—becomes the most expensive mistake of the AI era. In Kopadze’s argument about AI, where everyone has access to the same tools, the difference likewise did not come from access or performance, but from a structural judgment: how the loop was redesigned. The moment execution becomes a common good, what becomes scarce is no longer the skill of “running things fast,” but the judgment to decide “what should be run, and which boundaries should be redrawn.” The scale of the objects these three are dealing with may be completely different, but their shape is the same not by coincidence, but because all three sit atop the same tectonic shift: the falling cost of execution.

## Forecasts and implications: from “making layers faster” to “erasing layers”

If this principle is correct, then future change will point in the same direction across three layers.

First, in AI as a personal tool, differences among users will likely converge not on “access” or even “prompt skill,” but on the ability to redesign workflow. The fact that Kopadze’s observation drew 9.5M views suggests how widely felt the discomfort is: the majority are still stuck in the slowest operating model. Conversely, that means the minority who have reconfigured the model will hold the advantage for now. This is a relatively high-confidence prediction—the fact that people with the same tools already produce such divergent results is itself supporting evidence.

Second, in engineering and organizational operations, the dividing line will be whether you can put “deletion” before automation. The more AI makes everything automatable, the more Musk’s order—question it, delete it, simplify it, then automate it—will matter. The temptation of a world where anything can be automated is precisely that it encourages the error of rapidly scaling processes that should not exist at all. The practical implication here is straightforward: before introducing a new AI tool, the first question should be whether the process itself can be eliminated.

Third, at the infrastructure and architecture level, redesign is more likely to move in the direction of “integrating or deleting layers” than “making each layer faster.” Once AI agents become actors that not only read but also write, the premise that has separated analysis for human readers (OLAP) from processing for operational systems (OLTP) starts to collapse. Databricks’ LTAP is the first major move in that direction, and the same kind of “dismantling of dichotomies” will likely spread into other foundational domains—monitoring and control, development and operations, search and generation.

Finally, this principle should be stated in a falsifiable form. If outcomes were determined not by “dismantling structure” but by “the precision of optimization,” then the majority with access to the highest-performance tools should be producing the highest outcomes. But reality points in the opposite direction: results diverge dramatically even among people with the same tools. The takeaway for readers is one thing: when you acquire a new kind of speed, the first question should not be “how do I run this faster?” but “should this process—should this boundary—even exist in the first place?” Optimization has meaning only after that question can be answered “yes.”
