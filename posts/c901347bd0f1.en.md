The winner will be whoever has the single smartest model—that assumption is quietly collapsing beneath three developments that arrived in rapid succession this summer. One is the argument that the best answer to model routing is not a general-purpose monolith but "task-specific" design. Another is OpenAI’s GPT-5.6, which makes "outcomes per dollar," rather than absolute performance, the main axis of competition. And then there is the post claiming that agentic AI will become a "digital employee" and grow into a $155 billion market by 2030. At first glance, these topics seem to belong to different layers of the conversation. In fact, they can all be explained by the same principle: the source of value is shifting from the intelligence of the model itself to the "design" that allocates heterogeneous computing resources across tasks.

## The Dismantling of the Single Model: The Design of “Task-Specific Routing”

The starting point is L2’s "The Real Future of Model Routing Is 'Task-Specific'," which laid out the argument from Jerry Liu, co-founder of LlamaIndex. In his long-form essay published on July 15, 2026, "The Best Model Routing is Task Specific," he argued that the optimal solution in routing is not a general-purpose monolith but task-specific design. What matters is that this is not just speculation—it is already backed by implementation. OpenRouter’s "Fusion" and Cognition’s "Devin Fusion," both launched in roughly the last 1.5 months, share the same design choice: rather than depending on a single frontier model, they combine or switch among multiple models depending on the task.

What is happening here is the dismantling of the frontier model as the single "box" it used to be treated as. The old developer question was: which model is the smartest? What Fusion embodies is a rewritten version of that question: which capability should be assigned to this task, and how? The model is no longer the destination. It has become a component to be allocated. A new layer—the router—is emerging as the place where value is created.

What deserves attention here is that "combining" and "switching" are two distinct modes of allocation that only appear superficially similar. Combining means synthesizing outputs from multiple models to construct a single answer. Switching means identifying the type of task and routing it to the one model best suited to it. The former improves the quality floor; the latter optimizes cost and speed. Which one to use—and where to combine versus where to switch—is itself now a design judgment, not a question of intelligence. It is a question that did not exist in the age of the single model.

## The Axis of Competition Is Moving: From “Intelligence” to “Outcomes per Dollar”

That same shift is now being declared by OpenAI itself at the very top of the supply side. What L2’s "OpenAI Launches General Availability of 'GPT-5.6'" captured was a three-tier structure consisting of the flagship "Sol," the everyday-work model "Terra," and the lowest-cost "Luna." And the core of OpenAI’s argument is not absolute performance as such, but "outcomes per token" and "outcomes per dollar." Sol is said to outperform competing frontier models while using fewer tokens and costing less.

The three-tier architecture is itself a mechanism for allocation by use case. Instead of processing everything with Sol, the product is built on the premise that tasks will be distributed to Terra or Luna depending on their weight. GPT-5.6 also introduced a new `ultra` setting that runs multiple agents in parallel by default. If routing is the "spatial" allocation of capability, then tiered models and outcomes per dollar are the allocation of cost, while parallel agents are the allocation of time. The logic of allocation that OpenRouter was assembling downstream, on the user side, has been preemptively built by OpenAI into the upstream design of the product itself. These two L2 pieces, though independently sourced, point in the same direction.

## When Storytelling Overtakes Implementation: The Borrowed Future of the “Digital Employee”

At first glance, the third L2 piece, "Agentic AI Is Becoming a 'Digital Employee'," looks like a summary of the same trend. In a post published on July 8, 2026, The Kobeissi Letter said that AI’s center of gravity had shifted from "chatbots that answer questions" to "a software layer connected to enterprise data and applications," and concluded that AI is "evolving from chatbots into digital employees." The market, it said, will exceed $155 billion by 2030.

But what this L2 piece coolly points out is that the figure is circulating as a "Paid partnership," with neither source nor methodological assumptions disclosed. It needs to be read not as an independent forecast, but as a claim made in a promotional context. That marks a decisive difference from the first two cases. Routing and GPT-5.6 both came with concrete mechanisms of allocation—which model is used, at what cost, and how models are combined. By contrast, the "$155 billion digital employee" claim is a story about gross market size with no mechanism attached. The clue for distinguishing narrative from substance is whether the claim comes with task decomposition and cost structure—or whether it merely presents a market-size number.

## The Shared Principle: Value Is Moving to the “Allocation Architecture”

Taken together, these three facts suggest a single shift. The unit of progress is moving from "a smarter model" to "the design that assigns heterogeneous capabilities to tasks." Task-specific routing is the spatial allocation of capability. Tiered models and outcomes per dollar are the allocation of cost. Parallel agents are the allocation of time. The three are simply different faces of the same allocation problem. That is precisely why players in very different positions—suppliers like OpenAI and users like OpenRouter and Cognition—are converging on the same design philosophy without having coordinated it.

This principle also explains why it is happening now. The closer the absolute performance of a single model gets to saturation, the more impact on cost per outcome comes not from another marginal point of intelligence, but from better design in how existing capabilities are assigned. If the same level of intelligence can be delivered at half the cost, that difference matters more to a business than a few points on a benchmark. That is why suppliers are emphasizing "outcomes per dollar," while users are building routers. The place where value is created is leaking outward—from inside the model to the space between models: routers, tiers, and orchestration. And the role of the third L2 piece is to illuminate the falsification point of this principle. Even as steady, unglamorous engineering progress is being made in the allocation layer, the narrative of the "digital employee" is inflating in advance, on borrowed expectations alone. Progress in implementation and expansion in narrative are not two sides of the same phenomenon; they are two lines that can diverge.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, several changes are highly likely to continue. First, the main arena of product differentiation will shift away from benchmark rankings of frontier models in isolation and toward the surrounding allocation layer—routing accuracy, outcomes per dollar, and the efficiency of parallel execution. Since both suppliers and users are independently pointing in the same direction, this should be read not as one company’s strategy but as a structural pressure. Second, market-size numbers of the "digital employee" variety will continue to drift further away from implementation unless they are accompanied by concrete mechanisms of allocation.

This claim can be written in a testable form. If the principle is right, major product announcements in the second half of 2026 will be framed not around "how much smarter a single model has become" but around "how capabilities, cost, and time are allocated." Conversely, if the industry returns to a race for the absolute performance of monolithic models and allocation design recedes to a peripheral topic, then this hypothesis should be rejected.

The practical implication is clear. For those adopting AI, the key question is no longer "Which model should we choose?" but "How should we design the allocation?" The point is not to buy the single smartest model, but to build a mechanism that allocates capability and cost according to the task—and to keep a careful distance from numbers that promise "digital employees" without any such mechanism behind them. The handoff in the starring role, from the model shelf to the architecture blueprint, has already begun.
