Technology does not move a society when its capability crosses a threshold. It moves one when the burden acquires an addressee.

In Pennsylvania, data centres became a first-order issue in the governor's race, and nationwide opposition jumped from 42% to 63% in eight months. Over the same period, Codex usage logs showed agentic AI penetration splitting by orders of magnitude — 99.8% inside OpenAI, 63.3% across organisation accounts, 16.5% among individuals. And a16z, testing the indicators that supposedly proved AI demand was cooling, found that none of them showed cooling at all. Three different subjects, three different methods. Yet the same principle explains all of them: what moved in these eight months was not the capability of the technology but the assignment of who receives the bill.

## The electricity bill as an addressee

What "Data centre opposition became a first-order issue in a governor's race" records is less the rise in opposition than the route it took. In the Emerson College survey, US voters who said they opposed data centre construction in or near their own area rose from 42% last December to 63% by the end of July — 21 points in eight months. Muhlenberg College's March survey found 64% of the state's adults describing data centres as a "crisis" or a "problem."

The decisive detail is that this politicisation did not run through the environmental movement. As the piece states plainly, the fuse was the electricity bill. The environmental load of data centres has been argued over for years, but it was a diffuse cost that landed on nobody's invoice, and diffuse costs do not move politics. The moment it appeared as a monthly line item priced in electricity, the burden acquired an addressee.

And a cost with an addressee hurts by exactly the same amount regardless of party. That is why the backlash formed across party lines and recombined the existing political coalitions, as the piece reports. Ideology does not discount an invoice. What changed here was not the reality of data centres — it was that the incidence of the burden shifted from an abstract "environment" to a concrete "household," and began to be metered every month.

## The delegation gap measures who has already paid the up-front cost, not who is more capable

"Agentic AI moves from consultation to delegation," covering arXiv:2606.26959v1, reports Codex's share of output tokens as of 11 June 2026: 99.8% inside OpenAI, 63.3% for organisation accounts, 16.5% for individual accounts. Same model, same price, same date, and a gap that wide. So the variable explaining it cannot be model capability, and it cannot be access — access is precisely the element that varies least across the three groups.

Exactly one variable fits: who has already finished paying the fixed cost of delegation. Decomposing work into delegable units, building the verification path, absorbing the cost of rolling back a failure — this is an up-front payment incurred once per organisation rather than once per task, and amortised thereafter. Inside OpenAI it has been paid organisation-wide. Organisation accounts have paid part of it. Individual accounts re-pay it case by case, which is why they stall at 16.5%.

The paper's conclusion — that conventional adoption metrics such as active user counts are losing their information content — follows directly from this structure. A headcount measures access, and access no longer differentiates anyone. A headcount cannot, in principle, see who has paid the up-front cost. In the paper's own terms, the transition is not about "which tool you use" but about "how you assemble the work" — and reassembling work carries a cost with a name on it.

## The evidence for a "slowdown" was reading the wrong ledger

The three pieces of conventional wisdom that a16z's Moses Sternstein tested in "AI infrastructure demand is not slowing" share a single error: they misidentified where the burden would land.

The claim that "AI took entry-level jobs" predicted the invoice would arrive addressed to new graduates. But the tech new-graduate hiring ratio has held at roughly 5% since 2019. Seven years, and the addressee never changed. The place the invoice actually arrived was H100 rent: twelve-month contract rates up about 40% against last November. The same demand produced nothing at its predicted destination and a 40% rise at a different one.

The backlog reads the same way. Data centre machinery backlogs have held steady at just under six months. Read as "demand is flat," that becomes evidence of a slowdown — but a queue is an invoice denominated in time, and stable volume can mean constrained supply rather than steady demand. In fact price (H100 rent) moved while volume (backlog) did not. Sternstein's conclusion — that most of the supposed slowdown signals reflect either supply constraints or structures that predate AI — reads precisely as a diagnosis of a misaddressed bill.

## The shared principle — a diffuse cost is not observed, and an unobserved cost changes no behaviour

These three facts suggest the following: **the social impact of a technology is not a function of its capability but a function of how the burden is assigned.** More precisely, an impact becomes observable and changes behaviour only when the cost is metered and lands on an identifiable payer.

The inductive path runs like this. The environmental load of data centres sat politically inert for years while its scale and capability were unchanged; what changed was that the burden acquired an addressee in the form of a household's monthly bill, and immediately afterwards came a 21-point rise in opposition and a cross-party realignment (fact one). In Codex, with capability, price and access all identical, a sixfold gap opened on the single question of whether an organisation had finished paying the fixed cost of delegation (fact two). And in a16z's tests, one and the same demand left no trace whatsoever over seven years at the destination where no invoice arrived (the new-graduate hiring ratio) and showed up as a 40% rise where one did (H100 rent) (fact three). In none of the three is capability the variable. In all three, what moved was the incidence.

A conclusion about measurement follows. An indicator carries information only when it is denominated in a unit that somebody actually pays. Active user counts lose their information content because they display nobody's burden. Backlogs say nothing about a slowdown because they are a quantity where the price is the informative variable. The national opposition rate moved because it was backed by a unit of payment called the electricity bill.

The claim is falsifiable. If a case can be shown where a burden was clearly metered and landed on an identifiable payer and yet behaviour did not change, the principle is wrong. It is equally wrong if a diffuse, unaddressed cost can be shown to have actually moved political, market or organisational behaviour. To test the first, the direct experiment already exists: in any state that introduces a separate rate class shifting the burden onto operators, does residential opposition fall?

## What follows, and what to do with it

First, the siting of data centres is likely to be settled as a question of rate design rather than of technology or environment. Since the addressee is the electricity bill, the resolution will take the form of readdressing it — a separate rate class for operators, direct assignment of interconnection costs, a cap on demand-side exposure. Given that the politicisation in Pennsylvania is already cross-party, the odds are high that this readdressing becomes a state-level legislative agenda after November 2026. Read the other way, it is also a prediction that no amount of accumulated environmental argument will carry political force until it lands on somebody's invoice.

Second, measurement of agentic AI adoption will shift from account counts to a delegation ratio — something like the share of output tokens executed by delegation, where burden and outcome are displayed in the same unit. Once the paper has explicitly established that the conventional metric is losing information, that shift is a matter of time. Confidence here is moderate, and the reason lies in the incentives of whoever does the measuring: headcounts flatter vendors and delegation ratios do not. So expect the shift to start from buyers and researchers demanding it, not from vendor disclosure.

Third, the debate about AI's effect on labour will keep spinning as long as it insists on observing that effect at the employment-ratio address. Re-reading a metric that has not moved from 5% in seven years, quarter after quarter, will not produce new information. The effect is showing up in compute rents and queues, because for now that is where the invoice arrives.

For a reader, the practical implication collapses into one question: **which line, in whose ledger, does the burden of the technology you are evaluating land on?** If you cannot name the line, then however powerful the technology, nothing will move for the time being — and the moment it does land, things will move far faster than you predicted. Twenty-one points in eight months is what that speed looks like.
