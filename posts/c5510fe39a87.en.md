## Executive Summary

Pinal Dave, a SQL Server performance-tuning consultant who writes as SQLAuthority, argues that the loud debate over AI and entry-level jobs is watching only the bottom of the career ladder while something quieter happens at the top: existing senior engineers are being deskilled by the same tool that erased the junior rung beneath them. His central claim is a distinction — earlier tools (the compiler, the IDE, Stack Overflow) removed the *typing*, leaving judgment to the human, while generative AI removes the *reasoning* itself, cutting off the exact activity where seniority used to be built.

## The Junior Work Was the School, and We Closed It

Dave traces how he actually became good at his craft — not the polished interview story, but the real one: "You got good by being wrong in public, over and over, on things small enough that nobody got hurt." He shipped something that looked fine but wasn't; someone older showed him why, and the embarrassment "welds a lesson permanently into your bones."

He compresses this into one line: "Seniority is not a rank. It is the accumulated memory of every way you have personally made a mess." The boring first draft, the report nobody wanted to build, the tedious middle — none of it was filler. "That was school, and we just closed the school and kept the diplomas."

## Approving Without Reading: A Reflex That Atrophies

Dave then turns to what a senior person's day looks like now: "The machine drafts. They approve. The machine drafts again. They approve again." At first the approvals stay sharp, because the judgment behind them was forged by actually building things. But over time the comments shrink — four paragraphs become two lines, then "looks good to me" — and because nothing visibly breaks, nobody notices the drift.

His concrete example: a team asks the machine for a SQL query, runs it, sees plausible rows, and ships it — without anyone opening the execution plan, "the server's own honest note explaining how it plans to go get your data." The plan may have decided to scan a table forty thousand times, invisible on a small test set and catastrophic on a Monday morning in production. "Two years ago that team had someone who would have opened the plan out of pure reflex. That person is still on the team. They just have not opened one in a while, and reflexes are use it or lose it, same as everything else your body knows how to do."

## "Haven't We Done This Before?" — Typing vs. Reasoning

Dave takes the obvious counterargument seriously: we panicked about the compiler, the IDE, and copy-pasting from the internet too, and the sky stayed up — so why is this different? His answer: "Every earlier tool removed the typing. This one removes the reasoning." The compiler spared the labor of writing machine code, but the human still decided what the program should do and why. Stack Overflow handed over a snippet, but the human still had to judge whether it applied to their own mess. Generative AI, by contrast, "hands you the finished decision, already argued, already formatted, sounding certain" — and reasoning, he writes, "was the exact place where all the learning used to live."

## A Cut Supply Line, and One Weekly Countermeasure

Putting the two halves together, Dave describes a supply line cut at both ends: the bottom rung is gone, so no new seniors are being manufactured, while the people currently holding the title are being quietly deskilled by the same tool that removed that rung. "The existing stock has a quiet expiration date nobody printed on the box." He predicts the reckoning will be delayed but not avoided: "Ten years from now, somebody is going to stand in a very expensive meeting and ask where all the people who can actually tell went. And the honest answer is going to be that we stopped making them in 2025 and nobody filed a report about it, because nothing broke that quarter." As he puts it, "Every quarter it does not break, the decision looks smarter. That is what makes it so hard to argue against."

His own countermeasures: do one thing the hard way, on purpose, every week; make the AI defend its answer by explaining why the alternatives are worse, rather than just supplying a conclusion; keep a running "wrong list" of every confidently incorrect AI answer and how it was caught; hand juniors the very work that now feels boring, since that work was always the training; and notice the "four-second test" — the moment you look at something and feel nothing, which he calls the closest thing to an early-warning system available. No policy or company budget, he stresses, is coming to fix this on its own — "the maintenance is yours."

## Reader Comments Reinforce the Same Dynamic

The comment thread adds testimony from the other side of this dynamic. Reader Harjit Samra writes: "Maybe by the time companies realise the seniors can't fix things the AI will be good enough not to need them to oversee their work. I'm betting the costs of the AI tools will be 5-10x the cost they are now, and companies will have no choice but to pay it."

Another reader, Beri, describes a status meeting where a non-technical project manager judged a senior software engineer's exceptional, early-delivered work as "poor performance," reasoning that "with the available AI he should have produced this in a few days not 4 weeks." That manager fired the engineer and passed the "poor performance" characterization on to future employers, causing what Beri describes as serious financial hardship for someone Beri knew personally and professionally. Dave's only reply: "Thanks for sharing the details." The anecdote gives Dave's structural argument a concrete edge — when the yardstick for AI-era productivity is itself distorted, the mechanisms meant to protect senior judgment turn out to be fragile.
