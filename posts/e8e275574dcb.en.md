## Executive Summary

Josh Elman, a general partner at a16z who has led product for RealPlayer, LinkedIn, Twitter, and Facebook, argues in a September 14, 2026 essay, "Product Management is Still All About Telling Stories," that AI has inverted the order of the product development loop itself. What used to run idea → spec → costing/scoping → build now runs idea → prototype with AI → design → ship and learn. But while the cost of prototyping has collapsed to near zero, he argues the cost of judgment — figuring out what to build — hasn't changed at all, and the core of product management still comes down to telling a story users can understand.

## From Spec to Story: Answering Reid Hoffman's Question, 16 Years Later

Elman started his career as an engineer at RealNetworks and went on to run product and engineering for RealPlayer, a consumer product with hundreds of millions of users at the time. Wanting to become "a real product manager," he was in business school at Berkeley when he interviewed at LinkedIn with Reid Hoffman, who asked him a question he says he's never forgotten: "What is the artifact that a product manager produces?" Engineers have code; business development has signed contracts; designers have the visuals — what does a PM leave behind?

At the time, Elman answered: the spec — the blueprint that defines requirements and lets every team build from it. The answer got him the job (he dropped out of business school to join), but he now calls it "the wrong answer." While at LinkedIn, working to reimagine a jobs platform woven into a social graph, he wrote a 120-page spec defining the entire experience — and came away concluding that the spec was never the most important artifact. The story was. A spec describes what a system must do; product management, he writes, is about telling the story of the people who will use the product and why it will matter in their lives.

## The Loop Inverts: From Spec-and-Scope to Build-and-Play

Elman describes the old "Waterfall Era" loop as idea → write a spec → cost and scope it → everything else → then build — a set of rituals invented to protect scarce engineering time from bad decisions, one that allowed only about six to eight turns of the loop per year.

AI has cut the cost of making a prototype by, in his words, "a different order of magnitude," and that has reordered the same loop: first build the idea quickly with AI to see how it feels, then design it (both visual/UX and engineering), then ship and learn. "It completely inverts: from spec-and-scope, to build-and-play," he writes. The spec is no longer the deliverable up front.

He's careful not to overcorrect, though. "Demos are almost free now. Working products are not," he warns — the distance from a prototype to something real still takes time to cross, and "just ship it" remains bad advice. He reframes the PM's central question from "Does it fit in the schedule?" to "Does it fit in the product?" — because when everyone has agents that can code, deciding what to build is no longer a resourcing debate but "an impact debate. 'This or that,' not 'this or nothing.'" The flip side of that speed, he cautions, is the temptation to cram everything in — what he calls "AI slop" for product.

## What Isn't Changing: Purpose, Core Actions, and Cycle

Elman argues AI hasn't touched the underlying discipline of product vision, which he says isn't a mission statement but "the end-to-end reason for the product to exist for users," built from three elements: Purpose (why someone picks up the product), Core Actions (what they actually do with it), and Cycle (how often those actions are expected to recur).

He illustrates this with LinkedIn, whose purpose was "to find and be found": for many users, the core action was simply responding when someone reached out — and the cycle for that action was as infrequent as "once or twice a year." Understanding that low cycle frequency mattered, he says, because it meant the network needed enough people willing to be found, so LinkedIn spent its early effort keeping profiles accurate rather than pushing daily engagement. He urges founders to look past vanity metrics like DAU/MAU ratio, signups, waitlist size, or ARR, and instead track direct traffic — people who came to the product of their own volition — and count only those who complete the core action.

## Onboarding as the Biggest Storytelling Moment — and the "Blank Prompt Box" Problem

Elman calls onboarding "the single most important moment you have to tell your story to a customer," and divides users into "eagers" (already sold), "fly-bys" (who'll bail regardless), and a "fuzzy middle" who are curious and genuinely convertible — the group products should be designed around.

He argues AI products have made onboarding harder, not easier: the blank prompt box that many AI products open with may be, in his words, "the worst onboarding screen ever designed" — a magic box that can do anything, leaving the user unsure what to ask for. His prescription is to teach capabilities concept by concept and get the user to one valuable use case quickly, ideally using their own data — and to judge onboarding not by completion rate but by whether users return the next day or week and take a core action.

He grounds this in a story from joining Twitter in late 2009. Twitter was in the news constantly and drew millions of new signups, but they never came back — because, he writes, "no one could tell you what Twitter was." New users were offered "find your friends" or "follow 20 random people," then landed on an empty compose box with nothing to say. Twitter spent a couple of years rebuilding onboarding into what became the "Learn Flow" — teaching, screen by screen, what a tweet is, then having the user build a timeline by following people and watching it populate — which, he says, "moved retention more than anything else we shipped that year."

## Conclusion: Shipping Got Faster, Judgment Got Heavier

Elman closes by arguing that in an AI world, shipping is less of a bottleneck than it used to be, while figuring out the right product — and who it's really for — matters as much as ever, "if not more." His advice: use AI to move faster on prototypes, but "don't speed up your judgment. Don't give up your judgment." Balancing creativity against the flood of new data available, he writes, remains the hardest part of the job.
