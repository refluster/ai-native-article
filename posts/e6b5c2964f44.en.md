On September 11, OpenAI told developers to strip down the AGENTS.md files and skill descriptions they write for coding agents. On September 2, Anthropic revealed that it secures commerce-agent safety not through prompting but through the harness surrounding the model. That same window, more than 100 companies including OpenAI and Anthropic sent governments a joint letter demanding a "surge in defenses" against AI-driven cyberattacks. Three seemingly unrelated events — but they resolve into one principle: the center of gravity for control is moving away from the text fed into a model and toward the structure that surrounds it.

## Why Bloated Instructions Turn Counterproductive

According to an OpenAI Developers post signed by Eric Provencher (September 11, 2026), the instructions written for coding agents — skill descriptions, AGENTS.md, task prompts — have kept accumulating bulk in the style suited to older, weaker models. For GPT-6 Astra, that same bulk now actively hurts performance. The post draws on concrete good and bad examples surfaced while revising the skill-creator skill, and calls for a shift from instructions meant to be "read" to instructions meant to be "pulled" only when needed. The takeaway is singular: the smarter the model, the shorter and more scoped its instructions need to become.

### Capability Reduces Dependence on the Text Itself

This is not merely a matter of writing style. It marks a shift from pushing every possible judgment input into a single upfront block of prompt text, to a pull-based scheme where a file is retrieved only once it's actually needed. The design question of where information lives is moving from the body of the prompt to an addressable structure outside it.

## Why Commerce Agents Put Safety Outside the Prompt

Anthropic's "Anatomy of Effective Commerce Agents," published September 2, distills a year of building Claude-based agents across retail, travel, and telecom. The finding: an effective commerce agent is neither a swarm of domain-specific subagents nor an intent router, but a single agent loop equipped with skills and tools — one common skeleton. Latency and cost are handled through prompt caching, with hit rates of 90–99%; safety is secured not by how carefully the prompt is worded, but by harness-side mechanisms — server-issued ID verification and staged approval flows.

### Separating the Caching Layer From the Harness Layer

What's happening here is a division of labor. Cost and speed problems are delegated to a caching infrastructure; safety problems are delegated to the design of the execution environment itself. Neither is solved by refining how politely or cleverly the model is asked — both are solved by building structure external to the model. The commerce-agent report shows practitioners already living out this shift.

## What the "Surge in Defenses" Letter Reveals About the Lag in Structure

Major tech companies — OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon — joined more than 100 companies including Broadcom, Capital One, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, General Motors, IBM, Mastercard, Oracle, Robinhood, Shopify, and Visa in a joint letter calling on governments and industry for a society-wide surge in defenses against AI-driven cyberattacks. The letter asks governments to accelerate "trusted access" programs that would give defenders early access to powerful models. Yet the U.S. cyber defense agency CISA has had roughly a third of its staff cut over the past year — a plain gap between the letter's urgency and the state of the defensive apparatus itself.

### The Institutional "Structure" Hasn't Caught Up

What this letter asks for is not better safety training for individual models. It asks for a societal-level structure: an institutional guarantee that defenders, too, get access to capable models. Yet the very agency meant to operate that structure is being cut in the opposite direction. That an industry of this scale has to speak with one voice to demand structural buildout is itself evidence that model-level safety alone is no longer considered sufficient.

## The Common Principle

All three cases can be read as data points on what happens once AI capability crosses a certain threshold. The GPT-6 Astra case shows that as model capability rises, encoding control "inside the text" becomes inefficient — and can even become actively harmful. The commerce-agent case shows practitioners already moving both safety and cost outside the prompt, into the harness and the caching infrastructure. The 100-company letter suggests this shift is not confined to individual product design — it now reaches the institutional level of a society's defensive posture.

Taken together, these facts suggest that the locus of effective AI governance is systematically migrating from the "words" given to a model toward the "structure" that surrounds it — harness, infrastructure, institutions. Put differently, much of what prompt engineering used to carry gets replaced, once capability crosses a threshold, by architecture design and institutional design. But this migration is not symmetric: model capability can leap forward in a short span, while harness, infrastructure, and institutional buildout require finite resources — staffing and budget. CISA's staff cuts are exactly this asymmetry made concrete: capability surging ahead while the structural side fails to keep pace.

This claim is falsifiable. The proposition that "control's effectiveness keeps eroding as long as investment in structural buildout fails to keep pace with capability growth" would be falsified if, say, a government "trusted access" program materializes within the next six months and CISA's staffing recovers. If buildout does not advance, the same migration pattern should recur across domains well beyond commerce and developer tooling.

## Forecast and Implications

First, corporate instruction design will likely keep getting "thinner" (high confidence). Given that vendors themselves are now explicitly recommending this, as in the GPT-6 Astra case, the center of gravity in prompt engineering as a discipline will keep shifting from the craft of writing text to the craft of designing skill and tool architecture.

Second, harness-level safety mechanisms — server-issued ID verification, staged approval flows, and the like — have a moderate probability of spreading as standard vocabulary into domains beyond commerce: finance, healthcare, internal enterprise agents. Once Anthropic has published a year's worth of hard-won lessons, the incentive for other domains to imitate the same design pattern is strong.

Third, government-led "trusted access" programs are likely to lag behind the pace industry is demanding. CISA losing roughly a third of its staff in a single year shows that institutional structure does not get rebuilt overnight; expect the private sector's voluntary coordination — like this very joint letter — to keep substituting, for a while, for the structural defense that institutions are supposed to provide.

The practical takeaway for readers is simple. When judging how trustworthy an agent or automated system is, weight "how cleverly is the prompt written" less, and weight "how robust is the harness, caching infrastructure, approval flow, and institutional backing around it" more. The center of gravity for control has already moved. If our evaluation habits haven't caught up, we will keep looking in the wrong place.
