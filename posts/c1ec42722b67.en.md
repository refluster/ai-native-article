## Executive Summary

Job postings using the title "FDE" (Forward Deployed Engineer) have surged, but because FDE is not a licensed profession or an industry-standard job category with a strict shared definition, companies can attach the label to conventional contract-development or implementation-support roles and nothing in the posting itself stops them. Product-development specialist Shin (@shin_sasaki19, CPO office at OpenWork Inc.) argues, in an analysis posted on X, that the deciding factor is not whether the engineer sits on-site at the client but whether they actually own the full loop — problem discovery → design → implementation → production deployment → adoption and outcome measurement → generalization → product improvement — and warns that focusing on location alone causes genuine FDEs to be missed too.

## FDE Is Not "On-Site Contract Engineering With a New Name"

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, and on the surface — "an engineer placed at the client's front line" — it looks almost identical to a traditional on-site contract engineer (常駐SE, or SES-style staffing). The difference, Shin writes, is what the engineer is actually entrusted with and how far that work connects downstream.

He points to the actual language of OpenAI's and Palantir's own postings as evidence. OpenAI's FDE listing describes owning problem discovery, technical scoping, design, implementation, and production rollout end to end, with success measured by production usage, measurable business impact, and feedback that changes the product or model. Palantir's FDSE listing similarly describes small teams that understand hard client problems, decide the architecture, work with large-scale data, write custom applications when needed, and own the work from concept through deployment.

In other words, a genuine FDE is not simply "an engineer who sits close to the customer." It is someone who starts from a client's ambiguous problem, builds the production system themselves, confirms the actual outcome, and turns that learning into features or patterns reusable for the next client — Shin's own framing. He renders this as a single loop:

> Problem discovery → design → implementation → production deployment → adoption & outcome measurement → generalization → product improvement

Whether this loop is actually turning is, in his view, the first thing to check. An engineer who sits at a client site but keeps implementing specifications handed to them, with the learning staying locked inside that one client, is doing bespoke development regardless of title. Conversely, someone who owns this loop is close to a genuine FDE even if their employment arrangement is on-site staffing.

## Why Fake FDE Postings Keep Appearing

Shin traces the root cause to how well the word "FDE" fits what companies currently want, regardless of whether the role matches. Generative AI does not create value just because a company has contracted a model or an API — it requires data connections, business-process design, evaluation, security, and operations to be built out in full. The people who do that "last mile" of work are genuinely needed. But calling the role "FDE" makes it sound more cutting-edge and more autonomous than "AI implementation engineer," "contract development," or "adoption consulting" — so, in his words, the title gets imported ahead of the actual role.

Shin is explicit that this is not a claim that on-site staffing or implementation consulting is low-value work. Safely deploying an existing system and keeping it stable in a client's environment is important, highly skilled work in its own right. The problem, he argues, is a mismatch between what the job actually is and what the candidate was led to expect: someone joins believing they can "build products starting from client problems," only to be assigned to a fixed project and work through tickets on the client's instructions. That gap, he notes, reshapes not just day-to-day satisfaction but the career someone can build afterward — a difference he calls anything but small.

## Checkpoints for Reading the Posting and the Interview

Shin lists the following as things to look for when reading a job posting:

- **No stated path back into the product.** A posting that only says "feed customer feedback into development," without naming which product, shared platform, or team receives it, is worth being cautious about.
- **The job starts from "receiving requirements."** A genuine FDE steps in before requirements are settled, while a role that receives finished specifications and only produces an implementation plan is closer to contract development.
- **Heavy use of "supporting," "bridging," "driving," with unclear ownership of building.** A genuine posting names concrete deliverables — production-quality code, system design, deployment, evaluation, operations.
- **Success metrics limited to deadlines, hours, or utilization.** OpenAI's posting states its success metrics as production usage and measurable business impact; an organization that evaluates purely on utilization, billable hours, or tickets closed makes it rational to keep someone stationed long-term rather than build and generalize quickly.
- **The connection to the product team is described only in spirit.** "We collaborate closely" is not enough — there needs to be a concrete path: which repository the FDE can send pull requests to, who reviews them, and which meeting feeds the roadmap.
For interviews, Shin argues the most reliable approach is not asking about abstract processes but walking through the most recent engagement chronologically. He lists the following questions: what the FDE decided and built on the most recent engagement, from the initial ambiguous problem through to production; what share of the code the FDE personally writes, and who reviews their pull requests and holds deploy authority; whether success is measured by anything beyond deadlines — usage rate, business KPIs, evaluation results, reuse rate; whether a discovery from a client site has ever changed the core product roadmap; whether an outcome built for one client was ever turned into a feature or template usable for another; who owns incident response and maintenance after a production rollout, and when and under what conditions the FDE rotates off; and how the last three months split across problem discovery, coding, project management, and support.

If limited to a single question, Shin says he would ask: "By the time you're on your tenth client, what's faster or easier than it was on your first?" An answer naming shared infrastructure, connectors, evaluation data, or templates signals that learning is actually accumulating. An answer of "every client is completely different" may signal a structure where someone is selling their time from zero each time.

## Judging by Three Verbs: Build, Own, Compound

Shin closes with a three-verb framework for judging whether a role is genuinely FDE-shaped:

- **Build** — building a production-quality system yourself.
- **Own** — owning the work from problem discovery through post-deployment outcomes.
- **Compound** — turning field learning into reusable features or patterns.
Without Build, the role is closer to implementation support or PM; without Own, it's closer to contract development done on instruction; without Compound, however skilled the individual, the work drifts toward client-by-client systems integration. All three have to connect as a team's work for the compounding effect that defines FDE to appear, he writes — though a company early in its life may not yet have a shared product, and can still qualify if it is deliberately finding commonalities across engagements so the next client goes faster. Conversely, even a company with a well-known product of its own should not assume safety if its FDEs are cut off from core development and left holding only per-client configuration and maintenance.

Shin summarizes the distinction this way: an FDE is a role where getting closer to the customer speeds up the product's own learning; a fake FDE is one that loads the FDE with the burdens of ambiguous problems, travel, and production responsibility while withholding design discretion, influence over the product, and any reusable asset. His conclusion: the next time you read an FDE posting, ask less about how deeply the role embeds with the client and more about which code and which metrics carry that learning forward to the next one.
