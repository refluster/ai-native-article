A single manager overseeing 13 to 20 people, a hyperscaler dropping its data-centre NDAs, a CFO waiting on the monthly close. The three stories look unrelated. They are explained by one principle: decision authority is set less by title or contract than by what you can see, at what resolution and how recently, and by how much of the unseen part you disclose first.

## Three facts: visibility moved first

### One manager widened what he can see

According to "Output more than doubled in six months, with the manager still one person: how a LayerX EM built 'AI companion' management, and its limits", the Engineering Manager of LayerX's Ai Workforce division chose not to add managers or split into sub-teams. Instead he built a system to watch a team of 13 to 20 people together with AI.

What matters is where the line was drawn. The AI is not asked to automate judgment; it extends the range of observation and prompts conversations. Its output stays a hypothesis to be checked with the person concerned. After six months of operation, the stated weakness was that "the resolution is still coarse." The limit of management is described as a limit of observation, not of headcount or authority.

### Secrecy given up first

According to "AWS ends data-centre NDAs and commits $1B to communities in five years: from secrecy to disclosure first, and the missing AI-facility figures", AWS announced on 2 October 2026 that it was dropping NDAs with municipalities involved in data-centre plans, together with "Built Together", over $1 billion across five years for education, workforce development and energy-cost relief. The background is that more than 100 US localities are weighing moratoria.

What moved here is the order of information more than the amount. A community that cannot see what will be built has nothing to judge by, and judgment tilts toward refusal. So AWS moved to showing first. Yet, as that article itself points out, the environmental metrics are company-reported and AI-specific facilities are not broken out. The disclosure still has a gap. Credibility, it says, will be decided by annual disclosure and third-party verification.

### Decisions that cannot wait for a month-end number

According to "A real-time decision base for the CFO who waits on the monthly close: the four areas of a16z's 'finance suite' overhaul", Andreessen Horowitz partners Seema Amble and Angela Strange argued in an essay dated 15 April 2020 that CFOs, who support the CEO's decisions in an economic crisis, were left behind on tooling despite growing data volume. ERP returned backward-looking numbers more than two weeks after month-end, tools sat in isolation, and manual transcription and reconciliation never ended. They framed this as an opening for new companies and sorted the opportunity into four areas.

This is a problem of freshness and connection. The CFO has the role of deciding, while the numbers on the desk are over two weeks old. When role and observation diverge, the gap becomes a market for new tools.

## The common principle: authority follows observation

Laid side by side, the three share a structure.

First, the problem is located not in who decides but in what is visible. The LayerX manager widened observation instead of adding people. The CFO's problem was not the power to decide but seeing only late numbers. AWS's problem was that communities could not see the plans. These facts suggest that decision authority is in practice subordinate to the range, freshness and resolution of observation.

Second, those who widened observation state what remains unseen. In the LayerX case, AI output stays a hypothesis for the person to confirm, and coarse resolution is admitted as the open problem. In the AWS case, the weakness the article names is that AI-specific facility figures are not separated out. In both, the line between what is seen and what is not shapes trust. Wider observation alone does not create authority; stating the unseen part first is what lets others take the observation as grounds for a decision.

Third, the order cannot be reversed. Granting authority first and tidying observation later leaves the decider waiting on late numbers, as with the CFO. Committing $1 billion while keeping the NDAs might not have changed the fact that over 100 localities are weighing moratoria. That AWS dropped the NDAs together with the pledge can be read as a judgment that money alone would not earn trust.

From this we state the principle: **decision authority is in practice determined by the resolution and freshness of observation and by how far its limits are disclosed first; titles, funds and contracts sit on top as secondary factors.** It can be refuted. If many cases show acceptance won on authority or money alone despite coarse, late observation, the principle weakens.

## What follows

If the principle holds, several shifts become likely.

First, AI's role is likely to move from substituting for judgment to extending observation. Returning output as a hypothesis, as in the LayerX design, widens what is seen while keeping responsibility where it was. We rate this likely, because the practitioner placed the remaining problem in resolution, not in judgment quality. It is one case, however, and whether other teams find the same shape is untested.

Second, more infrastructure and large-investment firms may move to disclose first. With more than 100 localities weighing moratoria, the entry point to consent is the information asymmetry itself. But narrow disclosure can breed distrust; the missing AI-facility figures are the example. Without annual disclosure and third-party verification, disclosure remains a declaration. We rate this moderate.

Third, in domains like finance, where someone decides but the visible numbers lag, room remains for companies that build observation tools. The a16z essay sorting four areas of opportunity is one expression of that view. It is a 2020 piece, though, and today's situation cannot be confirmed from these sources.

Three practical takeaways follow. Write down which numbers the decision-maker in your organisation can actually see, over what range, and how late. When introducing AI or a new system, decide what it makes visible before deciding what it is allowed to judge. And when reporting results, state the part not yet visible in the same passage as the good numbers. Before adding authority, build observation and the disclosure of its limits. That is the most reliable order these three cases support.
