Between the second and fourth weeks of October 2026, three unrelated stories appeared. Gartner predicted that 70% of enterprises will abandon agentic AI built through vendor forward-deployed engineering by 2028. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella proposed designing around frontier models as an insider risk. And Samsung and TSMC posted record numbers on AI chip demand on the same day. They can be explained by one principle. The more abundant the supply of intelligence becomes, the scarcer something else gets: not the intelligence itself, but the design of authority over it.

## Three facts: the stated reason for failure was not technology

The first fact is Gartner's prediction. On 29 September 2026 it said 70% of enterprises will abandon agentic AI built through vendor forward-deployed engineering (FDE) by 2028. According to the L2 explanation "Gartner's FDE forecast: the basis for '70% abandoned by 2028' is contract structure, not technology", the reasons are escalating cost and the inability to develop the system in-house. Gartner frames FDE failure as a structural problem that precedes technology, and asks buyers to settle scope, governance, ownership and exit at three stages: before the contract, during delivery, and at termination.

The four items named are all questions about who gets to decide. Scope is where the line of delegation sits. Governance is who can stop it. Ownership is who can modify it. Exit is who can walk away. None of them asks whether the thing could be built.

The second fact is the design stance Nadella set out in an X article on 10 October 2026. According to the L2 explanation "'The most trustworthy system' is the one that has to trust the model least", he assumes no malice, but starts from the premise that any powerful actor with access to critical systems can err or be compromised. He therefore separates the supply of intelligence from authority over it and puts control outside the model. The article lays out seven principles of observability, and concludes that the system that needs to trust the model least is the most trustworthy.

Again the issue is not how clever the model is. The cleverer it is, the more its range of authority has to be bounded from outside.

The third fact is numerical. According to the L2 explanation "Samsung operating profit 107.4 trillion won, TSMC quarterly revenue NT$1.49 trillion", Samsung Electronics estimated Q3 2026 operating profit at 107.4 trillion won (US$80.2 billion), up 782.5% year on year, and on the same day TSMC reported September revenue of NT$511.86 billion, up 54.6%. Two layers of the AI supply chain, memory and foundry, set records in the same week. Yet Samsung shares fell 2.42% that day. The market has already started asking whether the demand will last.

## The principle that runs through all three: an authority vacuum, not oversupply, decides value

The three stories differ in field and in speaker: a contract, a design, a semiconductor. But they share a structure. Each takes it for granted that intelligence, or its raw material, is available in volume, and each locates the trouble after acquisition, in the control of what was acquired.

In Gartner's case, vendors succeed in bringing intelligence in and building with it. The stumbling points are that the enterprise cannot grow the system itself afterward, and cannot leave when costs balloon. In Nadella's case supply is a given, and the design effort goes into placing authority outside the model. In the chip case the supply chain is selling at record levels, but the share-price reaction reflects that nobody has yet shown why the demand will persist.

These facts suggest the following principle. As intelligence becomes a procurable resource, the locus of value and responsibility shifts from what you hold to who can decide what about it. Supply-side earnings and demand-side staying power are separate problems, and the latter is set by authority design: ownership, exit, observability.

The principle can be stated so that it can fail. If authority design determines value, deployments that fixed scope, ownership and exit in the contract should be abandoned less often than those that did not. If deployments of equal technical level but different contract structure show no gap in abandonment rates, the principle is wrong.

## The relation to the third fact: this part is inference

Only the third fact lacks a stated cause. The Samsung and TSMC figures show that demand is strong now; they contain no ground for its persistence. What is firm is that the market is "starting to question durability", and no more.

With that said, one inference. If Gartner's prediction holds and 70% of FDE-built agents are abandoned, demand for the compute those agents used would shrink, at least in part. A failure of authority design on the buyer side may therefore feed back into the durability of supplier revenue. This chain appears in none of the three sources. It is my inference, and all the three facts support is that they appeared side by side at the same time.

## Likely objections, and their limits

The strongest objection is that contract-structure failure is a matter of individual negotiations and has nothing to do with an abundant supply of intelligence. True, Gartner's piece does not discuss oversupply. All this analysis claims is that the stated reasons for abandonment were cost and the inability to self-develop, and that both concern control after deployment.

Another objection is that Nadella's proposal is a statement of his company's product strategy, not evidence of industry practice. That too is fair. Whether the seven principles are adopted in real deployments cannot be told from the source article, so this analysis treats it as one explicit statement of a design philosophy, not as a description of practice.

Even with those two limits granted, it means something that the three facts landed in the same period. The supply side set records, a major demand-side player put control at the center of its design, and a research firm predicted abandonment. Each, from a different position, points attention at the same place: where authority sits after deployment.

## Outlook and implications

First, in enterprise agent deployments the axis of competition will move from comparing performance to comparing contract terms. The ground is that Gartner places the reasons for abandonment in cost and the inability to self-develop, not in technology. I judge it likely that buyers will increasingly demand scope, governance, ownership and exit before signing. One checkpoint: whether exit clauses become a standard item in major vendors' proposals by the end of 2027.

Second, designs that place control outside the model will be cited more often in regulatory and audit requirements. The ground is that a senior executive of a major vendor has publicly put forward a design that does not trust the model. This is one person's statement, however, and it does not establish an industry standard. I put the probability at moderate.

Third, doubts about the durability of chip demand will not be settled by quarterly figures, because the figures show strength of demand but not the reasons it will continue. The view taken here is that durability depends on whether what was deployed stays in the enterprise's own hands.

For the reader, one practical implication. When adopting AI, settle in writing who can stop it, who can modify it and who can leave before settling what it can do. Authority not settled at build time comes back as cost at run time.
