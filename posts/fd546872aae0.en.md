## Executive Summary

In an October 10, 2026 post on X titled "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era," Microsoft Chairman and CEO Satya Nadella proposes treating frontier models as insider risks. The core move is to separate the supply of intelligence from authority over it, placing the controls that govern what a model can access and do outside the model itself. He closes with the claim that the most trustworthy Super Intelligence system will not be the one with the model we trust most, but the one that enables us to trust the model the least.

## The premise: we could trace code, but we cannot trace models

Nadella starts by contrasting today's systems with traditional software. When traditional software was deployed across the economy, we had the tools and capability to trace behaviors to a specific code path. That kind of mechanistic understanding eludes us in today's Super Intelligence systems.

- We cannot attribute model behaviors and outputs to specific inputs of training data or configurations of model weights.
- Yet these complex agentic systems are being deployed with access to our most sensitive data and the ability to take mission-critical actions on our behalf.
The conclusion he draws concerns responsibility: "We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf." A model provider's assurances do not relieve us of that responsibility. Treating Super Intelligence as nested black boxes, and simply accepting or rejecting its recommendations, answers and actions, is not an option.

## The proposal: separate supply from authority

Nadella's one-sentence summary: "we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it."

- Set aside the hard problem of alignment and start with an engineering approach to containment and governance.
- Surround non-deterministic models with strong, deterministic system design, human controls, and reliable operating procedures.
- Establish industry standards where existing ones are insufficient.
The framing device is to treat frontier closed and open weight models like insider risks. The reason is not an assumption of malice: any sufficiently capable actor with access to important systems can make mistakes or be compromised, and the architecture of containment and control must account for that.

Enterprises already know how to handle powerful actors, he says: "This isn't new!" Practices refined over decades (establish identity, limit privileges, log activity, create containment boundaries) are now being applied to Super Intelligence inside the enterprise.

## Why transparency alone is not enough

Nadella calls chain-of-thought (CoT) transparency "a non-negotiable," and says "Neuralese" cannot be a justification for opaque model reasoning. But it is not sufficient or dependable, because "we don't yet know how to make model outputs themselves consistently faithful or transparent."

Can models verify each other instead? His answer cuts both ways:

- "You can and you should use models to adversarially test and verify each other."
- But you can then end up with an opaque model inside an opaque orchestration layer, watched by another opaque model: nested black boxes.
This is where the decisive principle appears. The controls that govern what a model can access and what actions it can take must sit outside the model. This builds on an information security principle dating back to the 1970s: a program must not be able to bypass or tamper with the mechanisms that enforce its permissions. In practice:

- Separate the model from the harness that orchestrates its work.
- Separate the model from the action space that defines what it can do.
- Externalize controls and safeguards.
## Seven principles built around observability

Nadella lists seven principles for designing these systems.

- **Model diversity:** No one model should become the sole dependency for an important outcome or be responsible for verifying its own work.
- **Observe everything:** Every meaningful model action must leave tamper-proof, human-readable evidence. "If it can't be observed, it can't be trusted!" It must be possible to reproduce how an outcome was achieved without relying on the model to attest to it.
- **Verifiability:** Continuously test the entire system, including failures, attacks, edge cases and system changes, not just successful tasks.
- **Independent controls:** Organizations should be able to independently determine what a model can access and what actions it can take.
- **Independent auditability:** Validation must be independent of the intelligence being validated. No single model should control both a system's behavior and the evidence required to determine whether that behavior is aligned with the original intent.
- **Containment:** Assume a model is compromised and contain it from the start. Like an emergency brake, an authorized person should always be able to pause or shut down a model mid-task. More advanced models will require more advanced containment technologies that need to be standardized.
- **Incident disclosure:** When these systems fail or are compromised, affected parties need timely disclosure, along with mechanisms to share what went wrong, which controls failed, and how to prevent a recurrence, industrywide. This should include implementation details that change the behavior of agents at runtime.
## Assessment: what is new and what is old

**What the text establishes.** Most of the components are existing security practice: least privilege, audit logs, separation of duties and emergency stops have long been applied to people inside organizations, and Nadella says so himself. What is new is the change of subject. The model is designed around not as a partner earning trust but as a capable insider whose trustworthiness is not assumed.

**What follows from it.** First, greater model capability is not a reason to relax controls; the more capable the actor, the larger the consequences of an error or compromise. Second, the locus of quality assurance shifts. One model scoring another's output does not satisfy "independent auditability"; evidence of verification must live outside the intelligence being verified. Third, vendor assurances become supporting information only, with responsibility remaining with the deploying organization.

**Caveats.** The source is a single post of about three minutes' reading. It gives no concrete specifications for implementing each principle, and while it calls for establishing industry standards, it does not name which ones. It is best read as a statement of design philosophy; translating it into a particular harness and action space remains work for each organization.

## Conclusion

Nadella's argument amounts to relocating trust. Rather than placing it in the quality of the model, place it in deterministic design around the model, externalized controls, and tamper-proof evidence. His closing sentence sums it up: "The most trustworthy Super Intelligence system will not be the one with the model we trust most. It will be the one that enables us to trust the model the least."
