A $155 billion market by 2030. An internship sought by someone who had already reached the summit of product leadership. And open-source AI that dominates in processed token volume while surrendering a majority of spending. At first glance, these are three unrelated topics. But in fact, they can all be explained by the same principle: the metrics of “hierarchy” that have long been used to measure organizations and markets no longer indicate where value is actually being created.

## “Size” No Longer Tells You Where Value Lives

“Agentic AI Is Becoming a ‘Digital Employee’” was an article unpacking a post published by The Kobeissi Letter on July 8, 2026. At the center of it was the claim that the enterprise AI market would exceed $155 billion by 2030. But that figure was being circulated as a “Paid partnership,” with neither its source nor its assumptions disclosed. The flashiest metric used to describe the “size” of the market had been detached from the basis for calculating it—scale alone no longer substantiates anything.

The same disconnect appears in sharper relief in “Why the Rise of Open-Source AI Hasn’t Yet Hurt Anthropic.” On July 7, 2026, Decagon CEO Jesse Zhang laid out a “two-layer structure” based on real data from Vercel and OpenRouter. In processed token volume, open-source models such as DeepSeek are in first place. Yet in spending, Anthropic still accounts for a majority. If you measure by “volume,” open source is the main player. If you measure by “value (spending),” the frontier is still on top. Token volume, the most intuitive metric of scale, does not indicate where money is actually being spent.

### There Is a Direction to the Betrayal of Metrics

What these two cases share is not a mere margin of error. The visible, easy-to-count metrics (market-size headlines, token-processing volume) are systematically diverging from where value actually resides (substantiation, spending). And the divergence has a direction. The easier something is to count—and the bigger it looks—the farther it is from the center of value. Splashy numbers are increasingly becoming mechanisms for drawing attention toward the less valuable side of the equation.

## Careers Are No Longer Built on the Assumption of “Moving Up”

The same inversion is happening on the human side. “A Former Slack Chief Product Officer Is Looking for a ‘PM Internship’” examined an article posted on June 29, 2026 by btrax CEO Brandon K. Hill. A person who had served as Chief Product Officer—the summit of the product ladder—is now seriously looking for a PM internship. The article portrays this as part of a quietly spreading movement in Silicon Valley: “career down.”

What is breaking down here is the assumption itself that “organizational hierarchy is something you climb in one direction.” The top of the ranking represented by a title like CPO no longer determines what kind of work that person chooses next. Someone who has already reached the summit is deliberately seeking an entry-level role. The height of the hierarchy and the place where that person actually wants to create value have come apart. Here again, the metric of hierarchy (title) is betraying where value truly resides.

## “Layers” Have Already Emerged as the Unit of Value

While hierarchy is failing to track value, “layers” are beginning to come into focus. The metaphor of the “digital employee” does not mean that AI has simply climbed one rung higher on the ladder of intelligence as a smarter chatbot. It means that a new functional layer has been inserted into the organization as a software layer connected to enterprise data and applications. The observation that the focal point has shifted from a “chatbot that answers” to a “software layer that connects” is, precisely, a move away from understanding value through hierarchy (how smart it is) and toward understanding it through layers (what function it performs).

The “two-layer structure” of the model economy makes this even more concrete as a division of labor. Frontier models handle “discovery”; open-source models handle “production.” Jesse Zhang’s view—that this division of labor could become a stable feature of the AI economy for the time being—means that value is created according to different logics in different layers. In the discovery layer, a small number of expensive queries generate disproportionately high value. That is why spending is concentrated in the frontier. In the production layer, value comes from scale, low cost, and repetition. That is why token volume is concentrated in open source. It looks contradictory only if you try to measure all “AI usage” with a single ruler. Once you separate it into two layers, there is no contradiction at all.

## The Shared Principle: Value Resides Not in “Hierarchy” but in “Layers”

These three facts all point to the same underlying principle. Namely, **the unit of work and value is being reorganized from vertical “hierarchy” to function-based “layers.”**

Hierarchy means position on a single scale: the size of a market, the rank of a title, the amount of processing volume. Layers mean divisions by function: “discovery” or “production,” “design” or “execution.” In all three cases, the metrics of hierarchy ($155 billion, CPO, token volume) failed to explain where value lived. Instead, value was explained by distinctions of layer—substantiated spending, the form of work a person actually chose, and the division of labor between frontier and open source.

Why is this reorganization happening? When agentic AI enters an organization as a “digital employee”—that is, as a software layer connected to enterprise data and applications—its value is determined by what that layer does, not by how “big” it is. The same logic explains why a division of labor in which the frontier handles discovery and open source handles production could become a stable structure for the near term. Once each layer has a different role, a single hierarchical ruler loses meaning altogether. This principle is falsifiable. If the value of each layer ultimately collapses back into a hierarchy of scale, then the leader in token volume should eventually also become the leader in spending, people with CPO experience should return to the top of the hierarchy, and unsupported market-size numbers should turn out to be accurate. So far, observation shows that none of these things is happening.

## Forecast and Implications: Ask “Which Layer?” Before You Ask About Scale

If this principle is correct, what will become more visible from here is the widening gap between hierarchical metrics and value metrics. The “headline numbers” of market size (TAM), titles, and usage volume will increasingly become misleading as starting points for investment and hiring decisions. The probability is high. Since all three cases show the same pattern at the same time across different domains—the AI market, careers, and the model economy—it is more natural to read this not as a coincidence within one field but as a cross-cutting structural change.

The practical implications are clear. First, never take metrics of scale at face value; always verify the backing behind them—spending, methodology, real data. A number like $155 billion should not be treated as decision-useful until its source is disclosed. Second, allocate people and resources not by “higher or lower in the hierarchy” but by “functional layer.” Ask first not who is senior, but in which layer—discovery or production, design or execution—they create value. In organizations where people and agents work together, this question is especially urgent. The frontier layer responsible for judgment and discovery should be staffed with humans and top-tier models, while the production layer responsible for execution and scale should be built around open source and automation. Designing organizations so that ownership and evaluation metrics differ by layer will be the next rational organizational form.

This can be tested concretely. If this structural change is real, then within the next 1–2 years: (1) the gap should widen further between market-size headlines that cite no source and the spending or revenue that is actually disclosed; (2) the vocabulary of the two layers—“discovery/production”—should spread beyond AI into other domains, such as talent allocation and organizational design, as a framework for division of labor; and (3) cases in which top-tier talent intentionally chooses lower-ranked roles should begin to be discussed not as exceptions but as design choices. All of these are countable signals. If they do not appear, the principle proposed in this article should be rejected.

It is precisely when you are drawn in by the size of a headline that you should stop and ask again: which layer is this, and what kind of value does it represent?
