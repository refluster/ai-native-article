What we observe is not the thing itself, but a proxy metric that once correlated with it. The backlog for data-center machinery has plateaued and remained stable at just under 6 months. The share of new graduate hiring at tech companies has stayed at roughly 5% since 2019. Codex’s share of output tokens differs by orders of magnitude: 99.8% inside OpenAI, 63.3% for organizational accounts, and 16.5% for individual accounts. These look like three separate stories. In fact, they can be explained by the same principle: even after the bottleneck has moved, the metric continues pointing at the old bottleneck.

## What signaled a “slowdown” was not demand, but supply

Of the three conventional narratives examined in “AI infrastructure demand is not slowing,” the one whose structure is clearest is backlog. Moses Sternstein of a16z New Media used U.S. Census Bureau data to show that the backlog for data-center machinery has stabilized at just under 6 months. Read straightforwardly, the fact that backlog is no longer growing looks like a sign that demand has peaked.

But another number presented in the same piece breaks that reading. According to Silicon Data, the 12-month contract rental price for H100 rose by about 40% year over year versus last November. If demand were cooling, there would be no reason for the rental price of in-use assets to jump by four-tenths.

The contradiction appears when we misidentify what the metric called backlog is actually measuring. Backlog is not “the amount ordered,” but “the amount not yet processed”—nothing more than the difference between order volume and the supply side’s processing capacity. When supply is pinned to the ceiling, a stable backlog does not mean stable demand; it means stable supply capacity itself. The metric is behaving correctly. Only the thing it points to has been swapped out.

### Metrics continue to be used even after the correlation has broken

The same article also rejects the conventional claim that “AI took entry-level jobs.” The share of new graduate hiring at tech companies has remained around 5% since 2019. What we are seeing is simply the continuation of a structure that predates AI; you cannot infer causality from the level of the ratio alone. This is structurally identical to the first example: because a given number has long been associated with a particular meaning, it keeps getting cited even after that meaning has expired.

## When adoption rates lose informational value

The same pattern appears even more explicitly on the measurement side of adoption in “Agentic AI Is Shifting From ‘Consultation’ to ‘Delegation.’” The paper "The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex" compared real-world Codex usage logs across three groups: individuals, organizations, and internal OpenAI use. As of 2026年6月11日, Codex accounted for 99.8% of output tokens inside OpenAI, 63.3% for organizational accounts, and 16.5% for individual accounts.

What matters is not the size of the gap, but the axis along which the gap appears. These three groups are using the same product, at the same moment, with access to the same functionality—yet the difference between them is an order-of-magnitude spread. If you measured by active user count, all three groups would simply be classified as “using it,” and the distinction would disappear. That is why the authors conclude that conventional adoption metrics are losing informational value in the age of agentic AI.

The paper argues that this transition is not about “which tool you use,” but about “how work is structured.” Once the structure of work becomes the variable, any metric that counts only whether the tool is used keeps measuring a dimension in which the variable does not exist. It has the same structure as backlog measuring supply.

## The shift in hiring criteria is the HR version of the same principle

In “Why Netflix Hires ‘Systems Thinkers’ Rather Than ‘Experts,’” CPTO Elizabeth Stone says that the hiring skill she values most in the age of AI is systems thinking, and that Netflix is hiring for cross-domain integrative ability rather than narrow specialization. She also points to making AI literacy a company-wide baseline and operationalizing excellence as a system.

The through-line of that article is simple: the cheaper generation becomes, the scarcer judgment about how to wire things together becomes relative to the ability to produce. This says the same thing as the previous two sections, but from the side of evaluation criteria rather than metrics. For a long time, specialization functioned as a proxy metric for production capacity. Once generation costs fall and production capacity is no longer scarce, specialization loses the scarcity it once stood in for. Netflix changed what it measures not because it improved the measuring instrument, but because what is scarce has moved.

## Stating the common principle

These three facts suggest the following principle: **a metric is chosen as a proxy for scarcity at a time when a given variable is scarce. Even after scarcity moves elsewhere, the metric remains where it was first anchored.** And because the metric keeps moving, its expiry advances silently. A broken metric does not stop measuring. It keeps accurately measuring the previous bottleneck.

This principle explains why misreading happens not where data are scarce, but where data are abundant. Backlog, new graduate hiring ratio, and active user count are not missing data. On the contrary, they are well maintained, backed by long time series, and easy to compare—which is precisely why they get cited. That very quality conceals the fact that the referent of the metric has shifted. In all three examples, the wrong conclusion is drawn not from a lack of numbers, but from an abundance of them.

The implication is that the reliability of a metric is determined not by precision, but by the freshness of the linkage. A metric remains valid only as long as the scarcity it stands in for is still located there. That means a metric needs not only a value, but also an explicit description of what scarcity it was chosen to proxy. A metric that leaves that linkage unwritten cannot detect its own expiry. In all three cases, the misreading would not have happened if the linkage had been explicit: that backlog is a function of supply, that the hiring ratio has been unchanged since 2019, and that the mere presence or absence of usage does not distinguish differences in how work is structured. None of these are things you can know just by looking at the number. All of them become legible once you ask what the number is a proxy for.

This claim is falsifiable. If, in domains where scarcity has moved, an existing proxy metric can still correctly rank the new bottleneck, then the principle is wrong. Specifically, if active user count can capture, by orders of magnitude, the difference in degree of delegation between organizations and individuals, then the conclusion of the Codex paper does not hold.

## Forecasts and implications

First, metric expiry will accelerate from here. Falling generation costs are a force that relocates scarcity, and the faster that relocation happens, the shorter the useful life of any proxy metric becomes. Traditionally, metric design could remain fixed for years. But as the three Codex groups show order-of-magnitude differences at the same point in time, expiry now happens asynchronously across groups. Running a single adoption metric across the whole company collapses department-specific bottlenecks into one number. Confidence is high here: all three examples show contradictions in the same cross-sectional moment.

Second, the focus of measurement will shift from “volume” to “configuration.” What the Codex paper shows is that the variable is not amount of use, but how work is structured. What Netflix shows is that what is scarce is not production capacity, but judgment about how to wire things together. These are two manifestations—one on the measurement side, one on the HR side—of the same shift. The metrics that will matter going forward, then, are not those that capture how much of something was done, but those that capture which judgments are allocated to whom. That said, confidence here should remain moderate: the three facts show the direction of movement, but not yet what the successor metrics will actually be.

Third, the practical implication is this: rather than revising the metric itself, revise the description of the linkage attached to it. Updating values can be automated, but updating the answer to “what is this number a proxy for?” cannot—and if left unattended, only the number survives. The smallest procedure readers can try in their own organizations today is to choose three key metrics and, for each one, write a single line answering: “Is the scarcity this proxies for still located there?” If you cannot write that line for a metric, it is a candidate for expiry—and as these three examples show, an expired metric does not look broken.
