When AI shifts from a “feature” to a “foundation,” profit and control sink to the lowest layers, forcing the layers built above it to reorganize. Y Combinator’s investment themes, Anthropic’s massive fundraising, and changes in development-team workflows may look like separate events at first glance. But in fact, all three can be explained by a single underlying principle: a tectonic shift in which the center of gravity of value is moving from the “upper layers”—applications, services, and human job functions—to the “lower layers” of compute, chips, and foundation models.

## From “Feature” to “Foundation” — A Declared Tectonic Shift

The most explicit statement of this transition came from Y Combinator. According to L2, “Y Combinator ‘Requests for Startups,’ Summer 2026 edition,” the latest version opens with the declaration: “AI has stopped being a feature and started being the foundation.” What matters is that this declaration does not appear as an abstract forecast about the future, but as a concrete allocation of recruiting themes. The focus is placed on software for agents, inference chips optimized for agent processing, the semiconductor supply chain, and even the replacement of the service industry itself.

What is happening here is a “descent” in investment targets. In the past, startups aimed for clever features layered on top of an existing stack. But in an era when the foundation itself is up for grabs, recruiting themes descend to the lowest layers—chips and supply chains—that had previously been treated as given, as mere infrastructure. The fact that YC presented this allocation under the signatures of its partners means that the actors with the sharpest sensitivity to capital have reinterpreted where value resides, shifting it from the upper layers to the lower ones.

## Where Massive Capital Is Heading: Compute as the Base Layer

Anthropic provides scale-based confirmation of that reinterpretation. In L2, “Anthropic raises $65 billion in Series H,” the company is reported to have raised $3.5 billion in its Series H round, reaching a post-money valuation of $61.5 billion. More important than the amount itself, however, is what that capital is for. The company says it will use the funds to develop next-generation AI systems, expand **compute capacity**, and deepen AI safety and research.

In other words, the enormous capital flowing into frontier AI companies is not being directed toward flashy applications, but toward the lowest layer: computational infrastructure. This points in exactly the same direction as the “descent toward the foundation” that YC has signaled. One appears as an allocation of investment themes; the other as a concentration of capital into a single company. But in both cases, money is flowing not to what sits on top, but to what supports everything underneath. Frontier models, and the compute that runs them, are becoming the gravitational center that pulls value upward.

## In the Upper Layers, Human Roles Themselves Are Being Reconfigured

If value is sinking into the lower layers, what happens in the upper ones? One of the clearest signs comes from a setting much closer to home: L2, “X post: ‘shin_sasaki19 on AI and software development.’” The post argues that AI is changing software development workflows and forcing teams to rethink parts of the traditional SDLC (software development lifecycle). The discussion touches on developer productivity, automation, and the changing role of engineers in AI-assisted development.

What is being observed here is important. When the foundation is replaced by AI, the human jobs that used to operate above it—engineering work, long seen as one of the highest value-added forms of labor in the upper layers—become subject to restructuring. YC’s decision to make “the replacement of the service industry itself” a recruiting theme, and the reexamination of engineers’ roles in development teams, are not separate phenomena. They are continuous with one another. As the foundation descends into commoditized computing resources, the human work that had been layered on top is either automated away or reorganized into the role of “orchestrating the foundation.”

## The Common Principle — Foundationalization Is Gravity That Pulls Value Downward

So what do these three facts suggest? YC’s downward shift in investment themes, Anthropic’s capital flowing into compute, and the reshaping of engineers’ roles in development teams. The observed facts may be scattered, but one common dynamic is at work: **when AI moves from a “feature” to a “foundation,” economic value and control sink irreversibly from the upper layers of the stack to the lower ones**.

Why does this happen? In the era of features, value lived in the differentiated upper layers. The base was a common given available to everyone, and competition was decided by “what you build on top of it.” But once the foundation itself becomes the object of competition, the logic flips. Whoever controls the foundation sets the terms of access, and the layers built above it are drained of profit in proportion to their dependence on that foundation. That is why capital flows into compute (Anthropic), investment themes descend to chips and supply chains (YC), and human labor in the upper layers gets absorbed into the foundation itself (the restructuring of the SDLC).

The key to this line of reasoning is that these are not “independent events in separate domains,” but “observations at different altitudes within the same gravitational field.” Investors, frontier companies, and engineers on the ground—these three actors differ in position and perspective, but they are all being pulled in the same direction. What is pulling them is the gravity of AI becoming the foundation.

## Forecast and Implications — When Will “Features on Top of the Foundation” Be Swallowed by the Foundation?

If this principle is correct, then the changes ahead can be predicted with considerable confidence. First, profit, talent, and capital will continue to concentrate in the lower layers. Value will skew toward the small number of players that control compute and foundation models, while the application layer above them will be able to differentiate itself only through “access” to the foundation and “what it can assemble on top of it.” The fact that YC has already made chips and supply chains central recruiting themes is a leading indicator that this concentration has already begun.

Second, upper-layer jobs will shift from “people who make things” to “people who orchestrate things.” The reexamination of engineers’ roles (L2 from shin_sasaki19) is not an isolated phenomenon, but the leading edge of the same flow as the replacement of service work (L2 from YC). The probability is fairly high—though the more likely outcome is not that “human labor disappears,” but that it is reorganized into roles that “combine, supervise, and verify the foundation.” The smarter the foundation becomes, the scarcer—and more valuable—humans who can correctly orchestrate it and take responsibility for it may actually become.

For readers, the practical implication comes down to one thing: determine whether your work or product is “a single feature sitting on top of the foundation” or “part of the foundation itself.” If it is the former, then you need to factor in the possibility that the feature will eventually be absorbed into the foundation, and invest more heavily in upper-layer differentiators that cannot easily be absorbed—proprietary data, trusted relationships in the field, and superior orchestration. If it is the latter, then you should prepare to receive the capital and talent flowing downward. In the age of AI as foundation, the real question is no longer “What can AI do?” but “At what altitude in this stack do I stand?”
