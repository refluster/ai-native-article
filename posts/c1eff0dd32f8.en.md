What is constraining AI progress now is no longer how smart the models are. Physical supply, society’s speed of absorption, and the capacity of institutions to accommodate change—the replenishment rate of resources outside the technology itself—now determine the ceiling on growth. That is the hypothesis of this article. Google is paying SpaceX $920 million a month in pursuit of roughly 110,000 GPUs, Sam Altman says an “employment apocalypse” is unlikely, and Grok, even as it grows its user base, is running into slowing growth and regulatory risk. At first glance these three facts seem unrelated. In fact, they can be explained by the same principle. The rate-limiting step has moved from inside the model to outside it.

## Even Giants Can’t Supply Themselves—Compute as a Physical Constraint

The fact reported in “Google pays SpaceX $920 million a month—a new phase in the AI compute scramble” is itself a turning point. From October 2026 through June 2029, Google will pay SpaceX $920 million per month for access to compute resources including roughly 110,000 NVIDIA GPUs. The deal was disclosed in an SEC filing on June 5, 2026, one week before SpaceX’s Nasdaq listing.

What matters here is who bought from whom. Even Google, widely seen as one of the world’s largest holders of compute capacity, is procuring “bridge capacity” from outside. A company that has continued expanding its own data centers still cannot keep up and must rely on another company’s supply. The binding constraint here is neither model performance nor the number of researchers. It is the replenishment speed of physical resources: silicon, power, and data centers. Demand is structurally outpacing the speed at which internal infrastructure can be added. In that single fact is a condensed truth: compute has ceased to be primarily a capability problem and has become a supply problem.

## The Labor Market Is a Rate-Limiting Buffer

The second fact is unfolding on the human side. According to “Sam Altman: AI is unlikely to bring about an ‘employment apocalypse’ in the near future,” Altman argues that while AI will change many jobs, people will adapt and new forms of employment will emerge. Reuters frames this not as a sudden collapse in employment, but as a more gradual reconfiguration of roles.

The question to ask here is why the collapse would be “gradual.” If progress in capability is exponential, why doesn’t the labor market reflect that immediately? The answer is that human and organizational adaptation is a buffered process with a speed limit. Integrating new tools into day-to-day work, redefining roles, and reallocating people all happen on a different timeline from technical progress itself. The labor market does not absorb AI capability as-is; it absorbs it at its own pace. In other words, that “gradualness” is not grounds for optimism. It is evidence that the replenishment rate of social capacity has itself become the rate-limiting factor. Altman’s observation aligns, from the reverse side, with this article’s hypothesis that the ceiling is now determined not by capability but by absorption.

## Even Frontier Products Hit Institutional Ceilings

The third example comes from what ought to be the fastest-growing products of all. “Grok AI briefing as of May 2026: user scale, slowing growth, and serious regulatory risk” shows that xAI’s conversational assistant Grok, while steadily expanding its user base, is already confronting two ceilings: slowing growth and serious regulatory risk.

Even products with frontier-level capabilities do not scale infinitely or frictionlessly in adoption. Growth slows, and regulation—a force outside the technology itself—draws the upper bound. Here too, what is rate-limiting is not model intelligence. It is variables outside the algorithm: how much absorption capacity the market still has, and how far institutions are willing to allow things to go. A product leading on capability slows down for reasons other than capability. That asymmetry is the clearest sign that the rate-limiting step has moved outward.

## The Common Principle: Growth Is Limited by the Resource Replenished Most Slowly

Set the three facts side by side and a commonality emerges. In Google’s deal, the ceiling was physical supply. In Altman’s observation, it was social absorption. In Grok’s deceleration, it was the market and institutions. In every case, the frontier of capability—what AI can do—is no longer the binding constraint. What has become binding is the replenishment rate of nontechnical resources outside the algorithm: silicon and energy, human and organizational adaptation, regulation and market capacity.

These facts suggest that Liebig’s law of the minimum is at work in the AI economy. Just as plant growth is determined by the scarcest nutrient, the speed of AI deployment is determined by the external resource replenished most slowly. Capability grows quickly, but physical, social, and institutional capacity can be replenished only linearly, or at best in stepwise increments. So the moment the two have to mesh, the latter becomes the rate-limiting factor for the whole system.

Inductively, the argument is this: three independent observations all run into ceilings not “inside capability” but “outside capability.” The odds that this sample lines up by accident are low. If so, this is likely not a set of isolated incidents but a structure running through the current phase of the AI boom. The rate-limiting step has shifted from the inside of the model—parameter counts and inference accuracy—to the outside of the model—supply chains, organizations, and law. What the narrative of “exponential AI” obscures is the fact that what actually constrains that exponent is intensely non-exponential: physics and institutions.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, several changes follow.

First, the scramble for compute will continue, and companies will push further toward vertical integration in energy and semiconductors. Confidence in this is high. Google’s deal has already established a precedent, and there is no sign that the structure of demand outstripping supply is about to disappear. Competitive advantage will shift from “who has the best model” to “who controls scarce external resources.”

Second, the reorganization of labor will proceed gradually, as Altman suggests, but its distribution will be uneven. Confidence here is moderate. Even if there is no economy-wide collapse, the gap will widen between organizations that can absorb change quickly and those that cannot. Gradualness is an average story; at the level of individual roles, abrupt compression can still happen.

Third, institutions will become the de facto ceiling on frontier products. As Grok’s deceleration suggests, frontier products from here on will increasingly hit limits imposed not by technology but by institutions. Skill in regulatory response will shape growth as much as capability, if not more.

For readers who run small AI-native teams, the implication is clear. Your bottleneck probably is not model capability either. It is your own absorption rate: how quickly your organization can digest AI output. If so, the thing worth investing in is not chasing ever-smarter models, but expanding the slowest-replenishing resource under your control—human adaptation, operational processes, and the speed of decision-making. Match your pace of building not to capability, but to absorption. In an era when the rate-limiting step has moved outward, that is the cheapest and most reliable move available.
