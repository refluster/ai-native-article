The costliest errors in judgment around AI do not happen where the parties involved are looking most carefully, but where no one is measuring. There are three facts that seem unrelated at first glance. In Anthropic research connecting usage logs with self-reports from about 9,700 people, the more heavily people delegated work to AI, the less pessimistic they were about their jobs the following year—in fact, they were more optimistic (“AI Use Has a ‘Schedule’”). The U.S. government’s export controls on Claude Fable 5 exposed not the danger of the model in isolation, but the absence of an industry-wide severity standard for jailbreaks (“The Export Controls and Re-Deployment of Claude Fable 5”). Carbon emissions at Google and Amazon rose 25% and 16% year over year, and most of that increase came from Scope 3 emissions that neither company directly controls (“The ‘Real Cost’ of AI”). In fact, all three can be explained by the same principle—the gap always lives on the side no one is measuring.

## Perception Quietly Overtakes Measurement

The two layers of data connected in “AI Use Has a ‘Schedule’” are themselves a textbook example of divergence. One is observed exposure in the form of Claude usage logs; the other is a self-reported survey answered by about 9,700 people. What the article calls its biggest finding—the “automation paradox,” in which users with higher rates of delegation are more optimistic about employment impacts in the following year—shows that perception can swell outside the bounds of direct measurement. The article goes further, stating explicitly that self-reported perceptions of AI capability “consistently exceed” actual exposure as captured by usage logs.

What matters here is not which layer is correct, but that the mismatch between the two has a pattern. People describe their use of AI as greater, and more controllable, than what the logs record. Optimism is amplified only in the realm outside measurement—that of subjective perception. The reason the article was able to identify AI use as having a “schedule”—a rhythm structured by time of day, day of week, and occupation—is precisely that it treated logs, not perception, as the analytical anchor. If organizations measure future views of employment through self-report alone, they will end up with a brighter picture than reality warrants. The correlation in which higher delegation rates are associated with greater optimism is, seen from the other side, also a warning: the most exposed users may be the least accurate in estimating their own exposure. Only by running direct measurement through logs alongside subjective accounts can we tell whether that optimism reflects reality or merely the experience of feeling in control.

## Risk Cannot Be Measured from Inside the Model

“The Export Controls and Re-Deployment of Claude Fable 5” shows the same divergence in a regulatory context. On 2026年6月12日, the U.S. government imposed export controls on Fable 5 and Mythos 5, and Anthropic suspended access for all users. The controls were lifted on 6月30日, and on 7月1日 Fable 5 resumed global availability. The trigger was a circumvention report by Amazon researchers, but Anthropic’s verification found that the same circumvention could be reproduced across many existing models, including those from other companies, and did not reveal any attack capability unique to the Mythos level.

In other words, the perceived risk—“this model is uniquely dangerous”—did not hold up once it was re-measured. The risk lay not inside the model, but along a measurement axis no one had established: a cross-model baseline. The article names the real issue directly—not the danger of any one model, but the absence of an industry-wide standard for measuring jailbreak severity. As long as attention remains fixed on individual models, risk will inevitably be misidentified. The reality can only be measured from outside the model.

## The Real Cost Lies Outside the Ledger

The numbers presented in “The ‘Real Cost’ of AI” have the same structure. In sustainability reports published by Google and Amazon, both companies reported rising carbon emissions against the backdrop of AI expansion: Google’s total emissions were up 25% year over year, and Amazon’s were up 16%. But most of the increase came not from Scope 1 and 2, which the companies directly manage, but from Scope 3, which they do not directly control—especially data center construction and the procurement of GPUs and memory.

The net-zero commitments companies put forward are a story about their own operations, where they measure and control most carefully. But the real cost of AI had been building up outside that ledger, in the supply chain that is not directly attributed to them. That is why the article concludes that net-zero has become “even harder” for both companies to achieve. The gap between declared goals and measured emissions opens precisely in the territory that is not directly measured or assigned.

## Shared Principle: Error Concentrates in Unmeasured Territory

On the surface, these three facts look like completely different issues: employment psychology, AI security, and environmental accounting. But the structure they reveal is one and the same. In every case, the parties involved can tell a coherent story about the place they measure most carefully—users’ own perceptions, the individual model scrutinized by regulators, the company operations firms directly control. The gap opens only in the unmeasured territory outside that field of view. Perception expands outside usage logs, danger resides in the absence of a cross-model baseline, and cost accumulates in Scope 3, outside the accounting perimeter.

These cases suggest that AI’s true risks and costs are not scattered around by chance, but systematically concentrated in the places stakeholders exclude from measurement. The reason is simple. In unmeasured territory, no rebuttal arrives. Only in places beyond disproof can perception, declarations, and pledges continue expanding without friction from reality. That is why the gap is maximized not at the well-watched center, but at the neglected edge.

This asymmetry has practical consequences. The wider the measurement net, the smaller the gap—but for the parties involved, expanding measurement always comes with pain. To trust usage logs over self-reports is to deny one’s own sense of reality; to build a cross-model baseline is to share a common yardstick of risk with competitors; to count Scope 3 is to make the difference between pledges and reality visible with one’s own hands. In all three cases, measurement lagged not because the phenomena were technically impossible to measure, but because measuring them would produce inconvenient numbers. Unmeasured territory, then, is not a product of ignorance. It is a product of preference.

This principle is falsifiable. If, in the future, errors in AI-related judgment turn out to occur most often in the central domains stakeholders measure with precision, then this argument collapses. But what these three independent cases show is the opposite: an asymmetry in which error is biased toward unmeasured territory. The essence of governance is to shift what gets measured—from the center where the story is told to the margins where the divergence actually lives.

## Forecast and Implications: Those Who Can Move the Target of Measurement Will Win

If this principle is correct, then the next battleground will be “how to measure” itself. First, AI risk assessment will move from individual models to cross-model baselines. Since the Fable 5 episode exposed the absence of severity standards, it is highly likely that industry-wide jailbreak severity metrics will move toward standardization—because regulators are already in motion, and because we now have empirical proof that the existing way of measuring risk misidentified where the danger lay. Second, disclosure of AI’s environmental cost will shift its center of gravity toward Scope 3. Now that the numbers show net-zero accounting focused only on internal operations diverges from actual emissions, accounting for indirect emissions—including data center construction and semiconductor procurement—will sooner or later become a central point of contention in disclosure.

Third, inside organizations, evaluation systems that trust observed logs over self-reports will spread. If subjective perception consistently exceeds direct measurement, then both AI utilization and productivity impact are more accurately measured through usage traces than through surveys.

For practitioners, the implication can be reduced to one point. When estimating AI’s impact, risk, and cost, do not take “the reality being narrated”—the reality conveyed through perception, headlines, and pledges—at face value. The better question is: what territory is this judgment leaving out of measurement? Your own usage logs, cross-model comparisons, indirect costs and supply-chain costs—the reality that will shape the next decision lives precisely in the places receiving the least attention. Those who can move the target of measurement from the center of the story to the unmeasured margins will be the first to grasp the true reality of AI.
