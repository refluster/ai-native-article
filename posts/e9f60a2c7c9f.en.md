The easing of job-loss fear, the GPU "rack spread", OpenAI's annualized revenue: three numbers meant to measure AI demand, and each changes its conclusion depending on how it is measured. They are not three separate stories. One principle explains all of them: **under high uncertainty, value gathers not in the number itself but in the position that defines and measures the outcome.** Below we read the three L2 explanations in turn and then extract that principle.

## None of the three numbers is settled

### Job-loss fear receded, but the reading of demand merely moved

According to "雇用不安が退き、ラック・スプレッドが残る" (a16z's "Charts of the Week" and how to read AI demand), a16z's Moses Sternstein noted in the October 30, 2026 edition that fear of AI-driven job loss has retreated from its May peak and that postings are rising, mainly in technical roles. What matters is that anxiety did not vanish; it shifted to a different question, whether capital expenditure can be recovered.

The evidence usually cited for that worry is the gap between token prices and GPU rental rates, the rack spread. But the same piece reports Sternstein's argument that the metric rests on a small, skewed sample and cannot be read as a definitive demand signal. Fear simply switched from one indicator to another, and the new indicator is no firmer than the old.

### $70 billion or $50 billion: part of the gap is how revenue is counted

"OpenAIの年換算売上「700億ドル」か「500億ドル」か" (OpenAI's annualized revenue: $70 billion or $50 billion, and the FT report that shook the AI capex story) is starker still. The Financial Times reported that OpenAI would fall about $20 billion short of $70 billion in annualized revenue at the end of 2026, and tech stocks dropped. Bloomberg, citing anonymous sources, countered that the figure would reach $70 billion or more, calling the lower number "merely a forecast".

As that article notes, part of the discrepancy may come from differences in how revenue is calculated. For one company and one period, a $20 billion gap can open up. One side moved the market; the other rests on anonymous sourcing. How certain a number is and how much power it has over the market are moving independently.

### Long-running agents move to the measuring side

The third piece, "長時間稼働エージェントがリレーレースを制す" (long-running agents win the relay race: a16z's "AI-native Red Ventures"), looks unrelated at first. a16z's David Haber argues that AI agents have reached the point where they can carry customer journeys lasting weeks or months rather than single tasks. He says models like Red Ventures, which own the whole journey and are paid for results, are structurally threatened, and sketches their AI-native successor.

The key is the phrase "paid for results". Only a party that holds both the definition and the measurement of an outcome can sign an outcome-based contract. The protagonist of this piece is not the one being measured but the one occupying the measuring position.

## The shared principle: when you cannot measure, value gathers with the definer

### Why: what makes the three look alike

Set side by side, the three facts share a pattern. First, none of the numbers a decision would rest on is settled: the rack spread depends on a small, skewed sample, annualized revenue swings with the counting method, and easing job fear did not resolve how to read demand. Second, unsettled numbers are nonetheless moving decisions and prices; the tech-stock drop after the FT report is the clear case. Third, within that uncertainty, only the Red Ventures lineage builds the uncertainty into the business itself, by defining the outcome and charging for it.

These facts suggest the following. **When the way of measuring outcomes is split, value moves from "the party who guesses the right number" to "the position that decides which number counts as the outcome and answers for it."** A contest to guess the number has no stable winner, because upstream choices of sample or counting method change the result. A party that holds the definition can fix it in the market through contracts and prices.

### So what: three phenomena the principle explains

The principle accounts for all three. The rack spread is discussed as a demand signal yet remains inconclusive because nobody has taken responsibility for defining it. Reports on OpenAI's revenue diverge because the counting method is not public. And the meaning of long-running agents carrying a whole customer journey is that the executing side holds the definition of the outcome.

The principle is also falsifiable. If a single, third-party-standardized metric spreads and the variance in counting methods disappears, the advantage of the defining position should fade. And if plain platform sales, which are not outcome-based, keep out-earning models that hold the definition, the principle is weakened. Of the three go-to-market forms Haber names (platform sales, services sales, full stack), which one can hold the definition is the axis of testing.

### Holding the definition does not guarantee the number is right

To avoid a misreading: the claim that value gathers where the definition is held is not a claim that the definition is correct. Depending on the method, the same OpenAI revenue can look like $70 billion or $50 billion. Definitions can be arbitrary, which is exactly why those who hold them owe an explanation. What the three L2s share is that a split number shakes the market, and the only parties able to absorb that shaking are those who state their definition and open it so that a third party can recompute it. Holding a definition and opening it are two sides of the same position.

## Predictions and implications

### Prediction 1: fights over the definition of a metric will come before fights over the number

Moderate confidence. The basis is that all three cases ended up at the counting method or the sample, not at the number itself. When the next earnings report or news story splits a number, the question is likely to shift from "how much" to "what was included".

### Prediction 2: outcome-based agent businesses will differentiate on definitional transparency

Lower confidence. Haber's ownership of the whole journey concentrates the definition of the outcome on the executing side. But whether customers trust that definition cannot be judged from these three L2s. The defensible inference stops at this: operators who can publish their outcome definitions are better placed.

### Practical takeaways

Three points for the reader. First, when you see an AI-demand number, check the sample size and the counting method before the conclusion. Second, if you deploy agents yourself, write down what counts as the outcome before execution; without a definition, you hand your judgment to outside reporting when numbers split. Third, treat having a mechanism to measure outcomes as an asset, not an implementation cost. Just as job-loss anxiety eased only to reattach itself to another metric, uncertainty returns in a new form when no measurement mechanism exists.

What the three L2s showed was less the strength of AI demand than the fact that who measures it, and how, shapes its value. Numbers move. What does not move is the value of the position that holds their definition.
