Where, exactly, do humans still remain in the loop of making AI? If you look there, you can read where the industry’s true bottleneck lies. At first glance, there are three unrelated facts. Meta reassigned about 6,500 engineers and PMs into a three-month-old AI unit, without giving them a choice, and had them manually generate training data. Anthropic has disclosed that Claude itself already writes more than 80% of the code merged into production. And the company’s new model, Claude Fable 5, leaves even the judgment of whether a query may be dangerous not to human review but to automated routing by a classifier. In fact, all three can be explained by the same principle: humans are gradually exiting the loops of AI production and governance process by process, and the steps where humans remain most visibly are the ones that reveal the bottlenecks of the moment.

## Meta’s Bet — “Data” as the Process Left to Humans Until the End

What “Meta’s AI unit that ‘conscripted’ 6,500 employees” depicts is the reality that even a giant company still has to pour massive human labor into the single process of generating training data. Meta reassigned about 6,500 engineers and PMs into its “Applied AI” unit just three months after the group’s launch, without allowing them any real choice, and had them generate puzzles and coding problems for AI training. Internal backlash was intense: one company stream was hijacked by insults, and employees reportedly referred to themselves as “conscripted soldiers” and to the work as “literally gulag.”

What is decisive here is the fact that even after buying Scale AI for $14.3 billion, Meta still has not escaped its dependence on human-derived training data. Even after swallowing the largest player in data procurement whole, the final step of producing high-quality training signals was effectively handed back to human labor on the scale of 6,500 people. In other words, Meta’s actions themselves prove that “data generation” is currently the most labor-intensive process in AI development—or, put differently, the least automated one. The bottleneck is not capital but the data itself, infused with human judgment.

## Anthropic’s Internal Data — The “Writing Code” Process Has Already Collapsed

The exact opposite picture appears in Anthropic Institute’s internal data, introduced in “AI has begun building AI.” In their article “When AI builds itself,” Marina Favaro and Jack Clark presented direct evidence that AI is accelerating AI development itself. In the second quarter of 2026, the company’s code output per engineer reached 8 times its 2024 level, and Claude already writes more than 80% of the code merged into production.

What matters here is that the process humans have exited is “writing code,” a step once considered the core of engineering. In contrast to data generation, where Meta still pours in human labor, in code generation the human role has already receded into review and direction-setting. The authors themselves are careful to note that AI has not yet reached “recursive self-improvement,” in which it fully designs its own successor autonomously, and that such an outcome is not necessarily inevitable. But at the same time, they warn that this state may arrive sooner than many organizations are prepared for. The important point is not whether that threshold has been crossed. It is the observation that the same motion—humans leaving a given process—has here already advanced furthest in the process of “writing code.”

## Fable 5’s Safety Mechanism — Even the Process of “Governance” Is Being Delegated

The third fact shows that this exit is not limited to production processes. According to “Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5,” Anthropic publicly released the new Mythos-class model Claude Fable 5, and offered Claude Mythos 5—built on the same foundation but with some safety mechanisms removed—to cyber defenders and critical infrastructure operators on a limited basis.

The core of its design is a classifier-based system that does not simply reject queries in potentially dangerous domains, but automatically falls back to the more constrained Claude Opus 4.8. Anthropic says that this fallback does not occur in more than 95% of all Fable sessions. What is happening here is that the governance process of deciding “which queries are dangerous”—a role that would traditionally have been handled by human policy judgment or review—is being delegated to another AI, in the form of a classifier. Pricing was set at $10 per 1 million input tokens and $50 output, less than half the price of Mythos Preview. Even safety judgments are being automated—and mass-produced cheaply. Humans are quietly exiting not only production, but governance as well.

## The Shared Principle — The Map of Human Labor Is the Map of Bottlenecks

Put these three facts side by side and a single motion becomes visible. Data generation (Meta), code generation (Anthropic’s internal data), safety judgment (Fable 5): the loop that creates and operates AI consists of multiple processes, and humans are exiting those processes not uniformly but one by one. Humans have already receded from the process of writing code and from the process of judging safety, while the process of generating training data has still been pushed back onto human labor at the scale of 6,500 people.

Together, these facts suggest a simple principle: in the AI production loop, the process where human labor is concentrated is the bottleneck at that moment. Humans leave the processes where automation has advanced, and remain clustered in the ones that still resist full automation. That means observing “where humans are still left” is equivalent to observing “where the bottlenecks are.” The fact that Meta tried to solve the problem not with capital ($14.3 billion in acquisitions) but with labor (6,500 people) is the clearest evidence that the process of data quality still sits on the near side of the automation barrier. Conversely, the human exit from code and safety judgment means those bottlenecks are already beginning to dissolve. The map of human labor is, as such, the map of bottlenecks.

## Forecasts and Implications — The Competition Over the “Last Human Process”

If we accept this principle, we can predict the main battleground of the next phase with fairly high confidence. If the bottleneck in writing code is dissolving, as Anthropic’s internal data suggests, then the major remaining bottleneck will concentrate in “the process of generating high-quality training data.” Meta’s bet—the acquisition of Scale AI and the conscription of 6,500 people—is nothing other than a bet built on the premise that this process will remain in human hands for the time being. If that premise is correct, whoever controls data generation will hold the next advantage. If synthetic data or self-generated data dissolves even this bottleneck, then Meta’s bet will fail, and the loop in which AI makes AI will move even further out of human hands.

The implication for practitioners is clear. You need to identify, precisely, which process in your organization still requires irreplaceable human labor. That process is both your current competitive advantage—a moat defended by people—and the next bottleneck likely to come under automation pressure. To read a labor-heavy process only as “our strength” is only half right. It is also the target that will attract the greatest investment in automation. Just as Meta’s 6,500 people proved that the bottleneck lies in the training-data process, facing squarely where people are still concentrated in your own organization is the cheapest way to predict which process will collapse next. Those who misread the order of exit will find their moat quietly filled in first.
