## Executive Summary

The developer of ARTEX, the open-source AI penetration-testing agent that CrowdStrike linked to breaches at South Korean banks, took the project's public GitHub page down on 8 October 2026 and said it will be converted to closed source, with no further public releases. But ARTEX has been downloadable since 26 July, so forks, mirrors and local copies may already exist. The source's verdict is that the move protects the developer's name more than it protects the next bank.

## What happened (facts as stated in the source)

- The developer, who goes by Autumn-27, wrote on GitHub, according to Reuters: "Given the misuse of the tool, the ARTEX project will no longer be updated and will be converted to closed source." And: "No further versions will be released to the public nor will maintenance support be provided."
- The takedown was on 8 October. ARTEX was published on 26 July under the AGPL-3.0 license, pitched as a legitimate automation layer for penetration testers.
- CrowdStrike said this week that a China-based individual, believed to be around 26 years old, combined ARTEX with Anthropic's Claude Code, several Chinese large language models including DeepSeek and Zhipu AI's GLM, and xAI's Grok to automate reconnaissance and intrusion across South Korean financial institutions.
- According to the source, at least nine banks and lenders have disclosed breaches or been named in local reporting since late September, among them Shinhan Bank, KB Kookmin Bank, Hana Bank and BNK Busan Bank. The two largest known exposures are Yegaram Savings Bank at roughly 40,000 customer records and Shinhan Bank at roughly 25,000.
- A related-article blurb embedded in the source describes the lenders that disclosed breaches since 29 September as Shinhan, KB Kookmin, Hana "and four other" banks, with roughly 65,000 records exposed. That is seven, not the "at least nine" of the main text, so the number of affected institutions is not settled.
- Per Infosecurity Magazine, CrowdStrike's researchers walked through exposed operator logs tied to the campaign. The attacker's tooling, prompts and intermediate outputs were in the open, which is partly how attribution came together.
## What the tool does

The source says ARTEX itself is not exotic. It strings together LLM calls to scan networks, find weaknesses and chain exploits, the kind of workflow a security team would otherwise do by hand. That is also what lets an attacker reuse engineering work someone else already did.

## What the takedown does and does not do

The source is blunt: the move closes the door after the car has left the garage. ARTEX has been downloadable for more than two months, so a penetration tester, a researcher or whoever hit Shinhan Bank could have cloned it. Pulling the page stops new users from discovering the project and signals that the developer does not want to be associated with the breach. It does nothing about existing forks, mirrors and local copies. "Open-source code doesn't have a recall function."

The source calls this an old problem and cites the long-standing split of offensive-tool users into defenders and attackers, whether the tool is a port scanner, an exploit framework or now an AI-driven penetration agent. Metasploit faced the same argument two decades ago and survived by becoming indispensable to defenders faster than to criminals. ARTEX never got that chance: it went from launch to implicated in a nine-bank breach in under three months, and is now closed before the defensive side caught up.

## The numbers behind it

- Timeline: published 26 July, pulled 8 October, more than two months in between.
- Scale: the two largest known exposures, about 40,000 and about 25,000 records, total roughly 65,000.
- Trend: CrowdStrike's 2026 Threat Hunting Report, cited by CRN Asia, found AI-driven intrusion activity up 89% year over year. The source says the tooling behind that growth is overwhelmingly open and easy to combine, and that DeepSeek and GLM remain freely accessible outside China.
## Conclusion: the source's assessment, and what follows from it

**The source's assessment.** The more useful question, it argues, is not whether Autumn-27 made the right call but what happens to the next ARTEX. Dual-use AI agents draw scrutiny fast once they are tied to a real breach, and going closed-source afterwards protects the developer's name more than the next bank. South Korea's Financial Services Commission has ordered a broader review of how banks defend against AI-assisted intrusions, according to Let's Data Science, and the source says that review matters more than ARTEX's GitHub status.

**What follows (this edition's judgment).** If a published tool cannot be withdrawn, the point of control moves to the moment of release or to the receiving side's defences. The source points to the latter, in the form of the regulator's review. It does not, however, report any change in misuse after the takedown or a count of forks, so there is no basis in it for calling the takedown pointless; what it supports is narrower: it has no effect on copies already distributed.

This edition rests on a single source article. The Reuters, Infosecurity Magazine, Korea JoongAng Daily, CRN Asia and Let's Data Science reports are cited second-hand through it, and no primary document was checked. The mismatch in the number of affected institutions between the main text and the related-article blurb also needs confirmation from primary reporting.
