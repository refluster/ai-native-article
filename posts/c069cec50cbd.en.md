What the AI market is pricing is no longer how smart the model is but the right to leave at will. Three events that look unrelated point to the same hypothesis.

Anthropic filed for its IPO on October 26, disclosing first-half 2026 revenue above $16 billion against a net loss of $80.7 billion. The US startup Reflection AI released its first open-weight model, "Beam," cutting into a public-model market that Chinese labs dominate. Gartner predicted that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will abandon agentic AI built through vendor forward-deployed engineering (FDE). Revenue, a model release and a failure forecast seem to have little in common, yet one principle explains all three: what is being traded is not the output but the option to keep receiving it, or to stop and take it with you.

## Most of the loss was the accounting of a right

The L2 piece "Anthropic files for one of the largest IPOs ever: first-half revenue above $16B, net loss of $80.7B" first reports scale. First-half revenue was 14 times the prior-year period. Demand for AI as output could hardly be stronger.

The breakdown of the loss in the same piece is where this argument starts. Of the $80.7 billion, $72.4 billion is a one-time accounting adjustment tied to financing agreements. Subtracting it leaves roughly $8.3 billion, so about nine-tenths of the loss comes not from the cost of running models but from re-measuring the rights embedded in contracts with capital providers.

What matters is that the adjustment attaches to financing agreements, contracts that give investors rights over the future. Re-measuring those rights alone moved a figure on the books more than four times first-half revenue. The same piece notes that the amount raised, the valuation and the offer price are still undetermined. Even for one of the largest listings ever, the price is open while the structure of rights is already fixed in the filing.

Revenue shows demand for output; the loss shows the pricing of rights. That the latter appears at several times the scale of the former suggests where the center of value sits.

## Beam is selling the existence of an alternative, not performance

What stands out in "Reflection AI releases its first open-weight model 'Beam' as the US cuts into a Chinese-dominated public-model market" is how little it argues about who performs better. Reflection AI claims performance equal to China's Z.ai GLM-5.2, but has not published technical details, so the claim is unverified.

Even so, the piece treats the release as meaningful for both industrial policy and enterprise cost structure. The reason lies in the status quo: Chinese labs hold the public-model market. For a US company, the scarcity of strong domestic options to land on after leaving a closed API is the background to the story. What Beam sells is less an unproven promise of parity than the fact that there is somewhere to go.

Whether the performance is actually equal becomes a secondary question. Enterprises weigh more than the quality of today's output: whether they can hold the weights and move if API prices rise, terms change or supply stops. An open-weight model creates bargaining power just by existing, even unused. It is valuable before anyone uses it, which makes it the value of an option, not of an output.

## FDE failures are explained as contracts that never defined the exit

"Gartner's FDE forecast: the reason for '70% abandoned by 2028' is contract structure, not technology" states the point most directly of the three. On September 29, 2026, Gartner predicted that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will abandon agentic AI built through vendor FDE. The reasons given are "escalating cost" and "being unable to evolve it in-house." Insufficient model accuracy is not among them.

Gartner frames the failure as structural rather than technical and urges buyers to settle scope, governance, ownership and exit from the start, across three stages: before the contract, during delivery and at termination. Of those four items, ownership and exit are themselves rights of withdrawal. "Unable to evolve it in-house" describes a buyer who has no ownership of the deliverable and no right to modify it without the vendor. "Escalating cost" can be read as what follows when there is no exit and so no room to negotiate price.

Even when the quality of the output is not the problem, a buyer without an exit gets squeezed on price or operations and ends up discarding the whole thing, or so the reasoning reads. Failing to write the right to withdraw into the contract at the start invites the most expensive form of withdrawal later: abandonment in full.

## The common principle: output commoditizes, and the right to exit becomes the axis of differentiation

Line the three facts up again. At Anthropic, the valuation of rights in financing agreements moved the income statement more than the size of revenue, which is output. At Reflection AI, the existence of an alternative outside a closed API mattered more than an unverified performance claim. In Gartner's forecast, the absence of ownership and exit, not technology, is given as the reason for failure. In each case the rights attached to the relationship through which output is received, not the relative quality of the output, determine where value and loss sit.

These facts suggest the following. Once output quality passes a threshold and converges across competitors, buyers and investors start pricing not the output but the right to dissolve, migrate or modify the relationship. Beam can matter on a claim of parity alone because it presumes the gap between outputs is closing. Gartner's prescription concentrates on contract terms rather than technical requirements for the same reason.

The principle can be refuted. If output quality keeps diverging sharply between competitors, buyers should flock to the best model regardless of exit, and the right to leave would carry no value. And if FDE projects without exit terms were abandoned at rates well below 70%, Gartner's structural explanation would be wrong.

## Implications: exit clauses become the headline of the specification

If the principle holds, three changes are likely over the next 12 to 24 months, listed from most to least probable.

First, ownership and exit terms will sit beside price as a main point of negotiation in agent-deployment contracts. The grounds: both of Gartner's reasons for abandonment trace back to a missing exit, and buyers who have seen the failure forecast published will ask for terms that avoid repeating it. I rate this likely.

Second, the stated reason for adopting open weights will shift from performance parity to procurement independence. A situation like Beam, where a model is discussed before its performance is verified, is already an early sign. But whether released weights actually meet enterprise operating standards is unproven, so my confidence is moderate.

Third, more large financing agreements will swing the income statement sharply at events such as a listing. Anthropic's $72.4 billion being a one-time adjustment shows that when the value of rights is fixed, profit that reflects the business and the loss on the books can diverge. Investors will need to read the contract structure before revenue. I can only call this moderate, since it needs other companies' disclosures to confirm.

One question travels into practice: if you stopped using the AI you use today, what could you take with you? Of the weights, data, prompts, evaluation sets and operating knowledge, which does the contract say are yours? If you cannot answer, then however good the output, your deployment is still on the abandoned side of that forecast.
