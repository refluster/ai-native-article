Between the end of October and the start of November 2026, three seemingly unrelated events followed one another: Amazon's and Intel's earnings, METR's capability assessment of Anthropic, and a sharp fall in the Nasdaq after the US midterms. **All three show capital being staked, ahead of the evidence, on a premise that cannot be verified: that AI will keep accelerating.** The evidence behind that premise sits with the parties who hold it, and when the premise is shaken, markets move on fear rather than on verification.

## Introduction: three numbers, three scenes

The first is earnings. According to "The weight of AI infrastructure spending reaches the market: Amazon's cash burn and Intel's PC demand warning," the Amazon and Intel results released after the US market close on 29 October 2026 showed the cost of the AI infrastructure race spilling into big tech's cash position. Giants that once carried little debt have become large borrowers.

The second is capability evaluation. According to "METR's internal assessment: Anthropic's model improvement has accelerated about 1.9x, but most of the decisive evidence is redacted," METR announced on 5 November that the gain Anthropic's models make on well-defined tasks has roughly doubled compared with the 19 months to October 2025. Most of the evidence METR judged most informative is redacted.

The third is the stock market. According to "The day the election result lit the fuse on fears of an AI infrastructure slowdown," right after Democrats took control of Congress on 5 November, the Nasdaq Composite closed down 5.52% at 24,315.00, its biggest drop in 19 months. It fell more than 10% from its recent peak and entered correction territory.

These can in fact be explained by one principle. The scale of investment, the pace of capability gains and the market's reaction all depend on the same premise: that AI capability keeps accelerating, and that infrastructure spending will therefore pay off.

## Analysis 1: money goes out before anyone knows whether it pays off

The Amazon and Intel article shows a time ordering: investment leads, payback follows. The description of big tech moving from "low debt" to "large borrower" means that spending has outgrown what internal cash can fund.

The same article notes growing wariness toward small, highly leveraged AI cloud companies. The more a company funds itself with borrowing, the deeper the damage if the premise fails. As borrowers multiply, the premise changes from a hypothesis one may believe into the basis for repayment. Once a hypothesis becomes the basis for repayment, doubting it becomes an expensive act for the market.

There is another side. According to the same article, a memory-chip shortage is depressing device demand, and Intel warned of weak PC demand. AI infrastructure spending is itself squeezing the supply of another market. The spending that serves the premise is eroding demand outside it.

## Analysis 2: the evidence behind the premise is held by those who hold the premise

The METR article shows this structure most sharply. The conclusion that capability gains accelerated by about 1.9x is a number from an independent evaluator. Yet its main driver is Anthropic's own heavy use of AI in its research, and most of the evidence METR judged most informative is redacted.

Two things happen at once. First, an evaluation exists. Second, most of what it rests on is invisible from outside. The evaluator's independence may be intact, but the amount of verifiable evidence is set by the party being evaluated. The conclusion circulates as a strong number, "roughly double," while readers cannot tell how thick the evidence behind it is.

If the acceleration is real, it works strongly in favour of AI companies' fundraising and capex justification, since "capability is accelerating" is the premise of the investment itself. The party with the most at stake in the premise holds most of the evidence for it.

## Analysis 3: when politics shakes the premise, markets move without verifying

The Nasdaq article shows the reaction when the premise is touched from outside. Reuters attributed the selling to fears of tighter regulation of AI data-centre construction. What was sold was not AI capability itself but the outlook that infrastructure expansion would continue.

Note that on that day neither the capability evaluation nor corporate earnings had changed. Only the political outlook around the premise changed. A 5.52% fall suggests the premise was held up by confidence rather than by verification. A premise supported by verification would have little reason to move this far on a single election result.

## The common principle: verification is slower than investment

Put side by side, the three facts reveal one structure.

The earnings showed investment running ahead of payback. METR's assessment showed that the evidence behind the premise sits with the parties who hold it and is hard to check from outside. The Nasdaq fall showed that the premise can be shaken by a shift in the political outlook. These facts suggest that capital around AI rests not on verified facts but on confidence in a premise that cannot be verified.

Why does this happen? Investment decisions run on quarterly and electoral cycles, while capability assessments emerge only after a model release and the work of an outside evaluator, and part of the evidence behind them is then redacted. When the speed of investment exceeds the speed of verification, the premise is priced in before it is verified.

So the principle can be restated: **capital is staked on unverifiable premises before verifiable evidence has accumulated, and the scope of disclosure by the party holding the evidence sets the ceiling on the premise's strength.** This is falsifiable. If the scope of disclosed evidence keeps widening and the market's reaction does not change, the principle is wrong.

## Forecast and implications

Three changes follow from this principle.

First, each shift in regulation or political outlook is likely to produce the same kind of sharp fall in markets like the Nasdaq. As long as the premise rests on confidence, news that touches it moves prices even without any change in substance. The ground is that this drop came on a day when neither the capability evaluation nor earnings had changed. Confidence in this: moderate.

Second, the more a company depends on borrowing, the greater the damage when the premise fails. With giants like Amazon already turning into large borrowers, and wariness toward small, highly leveraged AI cloud firms already rising, the next adjustment is likely to show up first in the thinly financed. Confidence: relatively high.

Third, transparency of evaluation becomes part of competitiveness. As the METR case shows, an evaluation with a strong conclusion and invisible grounds is weak support for a premise. Companies that can disclose evidence without redaction should be able to raise money on the same premise at a lower cost of confidence. Confidence: lower, but the direction is reasonable.

The practical takeaway for readers is simple. When you see a strong AI number, first check who can verify its grounds, and how far. The narrower the verifiable range, the more that number should be treated as a premise rather than a fact. Those who make investment or business plans need to estimate the loss if the premise fails separately from how likely the premise seems.
