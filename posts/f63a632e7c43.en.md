Three recent AI announcements look unrelated. a16z called H1 2026 tech an "everything cycle," Alibaba declared it will raise data-center capacity beyond 20 gigawatts by 2032, and Anthropic disclosed that Claude leads 26% of its own R&D work. Yet all three can be explained by one principle. **In the AI race, only inputs (power, compute, share of work) are stated as numbers, while outcomes go unmeasured, and that measurement asymmetry shapes the next competitive front and the next trust dispute.**

## Three facts, all about quantity

The first is from a16z's "State of Markets II" (H1 2026). It argues that tech accounts for roughly 76% of 2026 S&P 500 profit growth, making it an "everything cycle." Attention has moved to hardware, and GPU demand exceeds supply. The units here are share of profit and GPU supply and demand: quantities of money and chips.

The second is the target Alibaba CEO Eddie Wu announced at the Apsara conference on 22 September 2026: more than 20 GW of data-center capacity by 2032. He also moved mass production of the new "Zhenwu V900" AI chip forward two quarters, to Q1 2027. The article "Alibaba CEO Eddie Wu states a 20 GW data-center target" frames China's AI contest as shifting from model performance to power and chip supply capacity. Again the units are gigawatts and production dates: physical supply.

The third is the figure Anthropic published on 18 September 2026. As of August, Claude leads 26% of the company's R&D work, over 90% of staff collaborate with Claude on tasks, and about 30,000 AI agents are deployed across research and engineering. Anthropic says it disclosed the metric so the world can judge how close AI is to "recursive self-improvement," building its own successors autonomously.

Put side by side, the pattern is clear: profit share, gigawatts, share of work, number of agents. Each says how much was put in. None says what got better as a result.

## Analysis 1: a16z's own data shows the demand-side gap

The same a16z piece states the gap most plainly. AI adoption is broad but shallow, and only about 2% of companies report tracked metrics. GPU demand exceeds supply, yet only about one company in fifty measures and reports what it got for what it bought.

That is odd. If hardware demand is strong enough to outrun supply, buyers should be measuring outcomes closely. That they are not suggests purchasing is driven less by measured results than by fear of falling behind and by competitors' moves. Inside what a16z calls the "everything cycle," demand is plausibly sustained by a race to procure volume itself rather than by verified returns.

## Analysis 2: Alibaba chose a measurable constraint as its unit of competition

Alibaba's announcement reads as an example of choosing what can be measured as the unit of competition. A 20 GW target is easy for third parties to verify: follow construction and you can tell whether it was met. Pulling Zhenwu V900 production forward two quarters is likewise stated in a checkable form, a date.

If, as the article notes, the center of gravity is moving from model performance to power and chip capacity, that is more than "power matters now." Model performance is contested and tends to become a benchmark fight. Supply capacity can be counted, so competition moves to the side that can be counted. This is the flip side of Analysis 1: outcomes cannot be compared, so firms compete on volume.

## Analysis 3: Anthropic's disclosure is an attempt to fill the gap, and part of the same pattern

Anthropic's disclosure is an interesting exception and also part of the same picture. It published metrics in response to concern about recursive self-improvement. Anxiety about what is unmeasured prompted measurement and disclosure, which does fill part of the gap.

But look at what was disclosed. 26% is the share Claude leads, over 90% is the share of staff collaborating with Claude, and 30,000 is the number of agents deployed. These describe division of labor and volume of input; none directly shows that R&D got better or faster. In the terms of Analysis 1, this too is a "how much was used" metric.

Still, the disclosure matters because publishing a metric creates a form of accountability. Choosing what to measure and publish gives a baseline against which other metrics can later be demanded.

## The shared principle: measurement asymmetry shapes competition and trust

From these facts, Why then So What.

**Why.** Inputs can be counted by the party that procures, builds, or allocates them: power in gigawatts, chips by production date, work as a share, agents as a head count. Outcomes depend on the customer's workflow, the definition of quality, and the time horizon, and are hard to count from outside the organization. a16z's "about 2% report tracked metrics" shows the effort of measuring outcomes has not kept pace with the spread of adoption.

**So What.** These facts suggest that AI competition has a structure that puts measurable quantities first and defers unmeasurable outcomes. Two consequences follow. First, competition in inputs accelerates, because what can be measured can be made a target, pulled forward, and published; Alibaba's 20 GW and its pull-forward are the model case. Second, outcome measurement tends to be disclosed only under outside pressure. As Anthropic published metrics after concern about recursive self-improvement, measurement appears as a response to anxiety, not as an internal necessity.

The principle is falsifiable. If outcome measurement were keeping pace with adoption, the share of companies reporting tracked metrics should rise clearly from about 2% as adoption widens. If it does not, the principle is supported.

## Predictions and implications

If the principle holds, the next 12 to 24 months should look roughly like this.

**Prediction 1 (confidence: high).** Public supply-side targets will spread to other firms. Verifiable numbers like power capacity and chip production dates are easy to use as competitive declarations. The basis is that Alibaba's target takes exactly that form, and that both a16z and the article place the center of competition on supply capacity.

**Prediction 2 (confidence: medium).** Outcome-metric disclosure will start first at companies under regulatory or investor pressure. Anthropic's disclosure, made in response to concern, is the precedent. Conversely, where no pressure exists, outcome measurement will probably keep lagging.

**Prediction 3 (confidence: medium).** The gap between inputs and outcomes will become a central question in evaluation. As a16z notes, adoption is broad but shallow. If competition in volume runs ahead and outcomes do not follow, demand will eventually be repriced. I cannot give a timing, but the larger the measurement gap, the larger the repricing.

**For readers.** When you read an AI announcement, first check what the number is a unit of. Gigawatts, shares, and head counts are all measures of input. If no outcome figure appears, that is the question the announcement has not yet answered. If you are adopting AI yourself, the easiest way to join the roughly 2% is to pick one metric to track and report, and to define it by what the use produced, not by how much you used.
