## Executive Summary

LSEG macro analyst Erwan Jacob argues that AI is no longer just a technology theme but already a macroeconomic force. Capital spending by the top five US hyperscalers is projected to reach about US$720 billion in 2026, and it is already moving prices in semiconductors, electricity, and imported goods. The key point is that AI’s effect on prices is **sequential rather than simultaneous**: inflationary in the investment phase, disinflationary in the adoption phase. That is the dynamic now confronting central banks with difficult policy choices.

## The Scale of Capex — Not US$30 billion, but US$300 billion

The absolute size of investment is what defines the character of this cycle. In 2025, major AI companies including Amazon, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Oracle committed an estimated **US$300 billion** to AI infrastructure spanning semiconductors, data centers, power infrastructure, and specialized talent.

According to LSEG data, capital expenditures by the top five US hyperscalers are expected to reach about **US$720 billion** in 2026. That figure is being supported by a sharp expansion in AI-related revenue.

- Microsoft reported an annualized AI revenue run rate of **US$37 billion**, up **123% year over year**.
- AWS recorded its **fastest revenue growth in more than three years**, driven by accelerating AI-related workloads.
**Assessment:** The fact that this is capex backed by actual revenue is one reason the article does not use the word “bubble.” Still, as the article itself acknowledges later, expanding spending plans and sustained demand are not the same thing.

## The Inflation Channel — Goods That Had Kept Getting Cheaper Are Now Getting More Expensive

This wave of investment is already beginning to affect prices across multiple sectors. The figures cited in the article point not just to tighter supply-demand conditions, but to an outright change in direction.

- Prices for computer software and accessories rose **about 14% over the past 12 months**. That marks **a reversal of the continuous price declines that had persisted for decades**.
- Wholesale prices for electronic components rose **28%** over the same period.
The demand shock is also showing up in trade data. In the first quarter of 2026, US computer imports **more than doubled to about US$93 billion**, semiconductor imports rose **40%**, and imports of computer accessories were up **37% year over year**. The article reads this not as a reflection of temporary supply disruptions, but as evidence that AI infrastructure has become a major source of global demand for advanced technology products.

**Separating evidence from conclusion:** Up to this point, these are observed facts in prices and trade statistics. The claim that “AI has become a structural source of demand” is Jacob’s interpretation of those facts.

## Electricity — Not Just Digital Transformation, but a Physical Investment Cycle

Energy has emerged as another constraint. US electricity demand had been nearly flat for more than a decade, but that trend has reversed with the rapid expansion of AI data centers.

- US power generation rose **2.5% in 2024**, **2.4% in 2025**, and **3.0% year over year in March 2026**.
- Consumer electricity prices rose **4.6% year over year** over the same period.
From this, the article argues that the inflationary pressure from AI-related investment could spread beyond semiconductors into utilities and infrastructure more broadly. In other words, AI is not only a digital transformation but also a **physical investment cycle** that requires real expansion in generation and transmission capacity.

## The “Sequence” of Inflation and Disinflation — A Challenge for Central Banks

This is the analytical core of the article. Over the long term, AI could be structurally disinflationary. Automation, supply-chain optimization, better decision-making, and productivity gains could allow output to expand without a proportional increase in labor and capital costs.

But the transition is not immediate. Massive spending on semiconductors, data centers, and energy infrastructure may keep pushing up some prices **before** productivity gains spread widely enough to offset them. Jacob frames this relationship as **sequential rather than simultaneous** — inflationary in the investment phase, disinflationary in the broad adoption phase.

That dynamic poses a complex challenge for central banks. Policymakers have to weigh inflation generated by extremely strong capex against the possibility that AI will ultimately raise the economy’s potential growth rate. As a result, the article argues, rate decisions are likely to remain sensitive to incoming inflation data even as authorities increasingly recognize the structural nature of AI-related investment.

**Assessment:** The “sequential” framing does a good job of explaining why the inflation debate around AI often seems misaligned. The inflation camp and the disinflation camp may be pointing to different phases of the same cycle.

## South Korea — A Proxy for AI Investment in Public Markets

According to the article, South Korea is where this transformation is most visible. Because of its position in advanced semiconductor manufacturing, South Korea has become one of the most important **public-market proxies** for global AI-related investment.

- **HBM (high-bandwidth memory)**, essential for AI accelerators and large language models (LLMs), is one of the fastest-growing segments of the semiconductor industry. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix sit at the center of that supply chain.
- Before the recent pullback, the **KOSPI** had been among the best-performing major equity indexes in the world, driven in large part by gains in Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix.
- At one point, the index went through a **correction of more than 8%**. The article attributes this not to a deterioration in corporate fundamentals, but broadly to profit-taking after a strong rally and uncertainty around US monetary policy.
Industry fundamentals remain solid. Demand from hyperscale cloud operators, AI developers, and enterprise customers continues to outstrip supply, and advanced-memory makers are maintaining significant pricing power. The article describes this supply-demand imbalance as the defining feature of the current semiconductor cycle — and as **a contrast with previous upcycles driven by consumer electronics demand**.

At the same time, the risks are laid out clearly. The Bank of Korea has cut its policy rate to **2.50%** to support domestic demand, while remaining cautious on further easing because of persistent inflation risks. The Korean won has fallen to **multi-year lows**, reflecting higher-for-longer US rates, foreign investor repositioning, and periods of strong dollar demand. In addition, South Korea’s dependence on energy imports means external energy-price shocks could again put upward pressure on inflation. Even so, the article argues that while these are sources of short-term volatility, they do not materially alter the long-term investment case for Korean tech companies.

## Conclusion — An Investment Theme That Has Moved Beyond Software

Taken together, the article’s conclusion is that AI is evolving into a **global capex supercycle** on a scale comparable to past periods of industrial transformation. The investment theme now extends far beyond software, encompassing semiconductor manufacturing, cloud infrastructure, power generation, advanced manufacturing, and global supply chains.

Even if high valuations, tight monetary policy, and geopolitical uncertainty create periods of volatility, the structural drivers remain intact: demand for compute continues to exceed supply, corporate investment plans continue to expand, and countries with strategic positions in the semiconductor ecosystem — South Korea in particular — remain well placed to benefit from this investment cycle.
