## Executive Summary

On July 24, 2026, Anthropic unveiled “Claude Opus 5,” claiming a “new state of the art” on coding and knowledge-work evaluations. The company positions it as a model that delivers frontier intelligence close to Claude Fable 5 at half the price, and at the same price as the previous-generation Opus 4.8, while posting advantages across multiple metrics including Frontier-Bench, CursorBench, and ARC-AGI 3. But every figure presented comes either from Anthropic’s own testing or testimonials from early users, not from independent third-party verification—this article organizes those claims while preserving that distinction.

## The announcement’s core message—“everyday use” at an unchanged price

Opus 5 became available across all platforms on the day it was announced, and Anthropic positioned it as the new default model in Claude Max and the most powerful model in Claude Pro. The pitch has two pillars. The first is intelligence: Anthropic says it “approaches Claude Fable 5 frontier intelligence at half the price.” The second is cost: it says Opus 5 delivers “substantially improved performance” while keeping the same price as the previous-generation Opus 4.8. Operationally, the company says users can choose through an “effort” setting whether to maximize intelligence or conserve tokens for faster, cheaper operation. Anthropic also states its limits explicitly, acknowledging that on cybersecurity tasks it “still trails Mythos 5.” Phrases like “designed to be used every day” and “runs more efficiently than other models” set the tone for the announcement.

## Benchmark claims—what the numbers are actually saying

A defining feature of the metrics Anthropic presented is that they pair “performance” with “performance per cost.” The emphasis is not just on absolute scores, but on what the model can do at a given cost.

- **Frontier-Bench v0.1**: It outperformed every other model, and delivered more than 2x the performance of Opus 4.8 “while lowering cost per task.”
- **CursorBench 3.2**: At max effort, it came within 0.5% of Fable 5’s peak score at half the cost per task. Anthropic says that at the high, xhigh, and max settings, it outperforms every other model on a same-cost basis.
- **ARC-AGI 3** (solving novel problems): Its score was 3x that of the runner-up model.
- **Zapier AutomationBench** (whether it can complete business tasks end to end): At the same cost, its pass rate was about 1.5x that of the runner-up. Anthropic also says that even at the lowest effort setting, it passed more tasks than any other model.
- **OSWorld 2.0** (computer use): It exceeded Fable 5’s best result at “just over one-third the cost.”
In scientific research, Anthropic says Opus 5 outperformed Opus 4.8 on every life sciences evaluation. It gives specific figures: on organic chemistry—inferring molecular structure from spectroscopy data—it beat Opus 4.8 by 10.2 points on an internal benchmark, and on predicting the functional impact of protein sequence mutations it beat Opus 4.8 by 7.7 points. Anthropic also highlights improved visual output, attaching examples of generated artifacts such as a “wind tunnel” visualization of airflow around an aerodynamic shape and an interactive simplified diagram of a cell. It further says the model is “best and most cost-efficient” on related evaluations including GDPval-AA v2, HLE, and DeepSearchQA.

## “Verification and iteration”—examples of agentic behavior in practice

What Anthropic spends the most space on is not the scores themselves, but the model’s persistence in working through problems. In one Frontier-Bench task, Opus 5 was given a mechanical part drawing but no direct way to view it. Anthropic says the model wrote its own computer vision pipeline, extracted the shape from raw pixels, and reconstructed it as a 3D FreeCAD model—while competing models under the same conditions failed to solve it even after 5 attempts. In another example, involving a real bug in a well-known open-source package manager, Opus 5 fixed the root cause—an edge case that the community patch had missed. Anthropic says competing models fixed only the surface symptom and reported the issue as “resolved.” In yet another case, a market data feed for an exchange had previously proven impossible for earlier models to complete even when given a detailed plan; Anthropic says Opus 5, seeing that no live feed was available for validation, built its own test harness and completed the work in a single session.

Customer testimonials point in the same direction. Devin CEO Scott Wu said, “On FrontierCode 1.1, Opus 5 comes close to Fable-class performance at half the cost.” Cursor co-founder Sualeh Asif said, “Opus 5 delivers intelligence close to Fable 5 at Opus speed and cost. On CursorBench, it sits just below Fable 5.” Zapier CEO Wade Foster said, “It reached the top of AutomationBench without using more tokens... previous models did not pass, and Opus 5 reached 100%.” Another Lovable co-founder, Fabian Hedin, said that in “the hardest agentic coding” it improved 22% versus Opus 4.7, and added that run-to-run variability (variance) was much lower, making it more stable. The company’s CTO, Madabh Jha, called it “the biggest leap for the Opus family since 4.5.” Box CTO Ben Kus said it “outperformed Opus 4.8 by 8%, with 11% improvement in data analysis and 17% in due diligence.” Alfredo Andere, CEO of a genomics company, offered a different angle on that persistence, saying it “behaves more like a careful scientist than any model we’ve run to date, choosing statistical methods that rule out confounders and cross-checking its own results with independent methods.” Availability spans Claude API, Claude.ai, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork.

## An editorial reading—the source is vendor self-reporting

The key distinction to keep in mind is where the evidence comes from. Every figure above comes either from Anthropic’s own evaluation or from testimony by early-access customers. This is not independent third-party verification, and both the comparison set (Fable 5, Mythos 5, Opus 4.8) and the metrics (Frontier-Bench, CursorBench) were chosen by Anthropic. That means the claim of a “new state of the art” is most accurately read as a relative ranking on the field the company itself has presented. Even so, the fact that Anthropic explicitly names a weakness—it trails Mythos 5 in cyber—and consistently discusses cost alongside performance suggests that this announcement is not merely a flex about raw capability, but a deployment-oriented argument that “efficiency has improved at the same price.” For readers, the practical implication is straightforward. The focus of evaluation is shifting away from absolute capability alone and toward a more operational question: is it worth replacing Opus 4.8 at the same cost?
