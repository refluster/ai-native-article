Code review, AI-generated screens, and month-end close. Three pieces from different fields and different years look unrelated. The Claude Code team redesigned code review in an environment where Claude writes about 90% of the code. On 7 October 2026 OpenAI introduced Intelligent UI, in which GPT-6 generates an operable screen for each question. In April 2020 a16z published a diagnosis of CFOs held back by the late numbers of their ERP systems. In fact all three are explained by one principle: the work of "reading" moves from behind the decision to in front of it, and humans keep only the decision.

## First fact: review was not abolished; only the reading moved to machines

The core of "Moving review to machines to protect human time: the Claude Code team's code review design" is that review was not removed but decomposed. Even in an environment where Claude writes about 90% of the code, the team did not abolish review. It moved most of the reading to machines and kept human time and attention for the most important judgments.

The numbers show quality and volume moving together. Pull requests merged from June to September more than doubled, and the share reverted fell by more than 40%. Doubling throughput would normally be expected to cost quality; here both improved. Human reading did not get better. What humans read narrowed to the part that needs judgment after machine checks have passed.

What the article makes impossible to miss is that the starting question was replaced. It was no longer "did a human read it?" but "what can break, and which check is appropriate?" The first question asks about the form of the process; the second asks about the object of judgment. Keep the form, and a person is stood in front of the whole volume as a reader. Ask about the object, and the reading can be handed to machines while the person moves to designing the checks and making the final call.

## Second fact: instead of returning an answer, assemble the place where the decision is made

According to "GPT-6 and Intelligent UI: ChatGPT starts generating screens, not answers," GPT-6 generates, for each question, an operable screen containing buttons, forms and charts. It also adopted a mode that alternates thinking and answering.

Reading this as a cosmetic improvement misses the point. In a conventional chat the model answers in prose and the user interprets it and decides what to do next. The effort of interpretation sat with the user. Intelligent UI does part of that interpretation in advance: options become buttons, inputs become forms, numbers become charts. What remains for the user is the decision itself, whether to press or not, which value to enter.

Alternating thinking and answering points the same way. The machine thinks first and puts its intermediate work in front of the decision. The shape matches code review, where machines inspect first and people face the object that survives the inspection.

## Third fact: numbers that arrive two weeks after month-end put the reading behind the decision

The piece covered in "Giving CFOs who wait for month-end close a real-time decision foundation: the four areas of a16z's 'finance suite renewal'" was published on 15 April 2020 by Seema Amble and Angela Strange. In the middle of an economic crisis, the CFO who supports the CEO's decisions is left behind in tooling even as data volume grows. The ERP returns only backward-looking numbers, more than two weeks after month-end. Each tool is isolated, and manual transcription and reconciliation never end.

Here too the position of "reading" is the heart of the problem. Collecting, matching and arranging the numbers into a readable form is needed in front of the decision, yet in practice it sits behind, taking two weeks after month-end. At every decision the CFO acts on an old reading. The authors framed this as a new founding opportunity and organised it into four areas.

One more point. This piece dates from 2020, several years earlier than the other two. The same structural problem was named before AI was practical. The fix was written down, but the reader who would carry it out was still a person.

## The common principle: where "reading" sits decides an organisation's speed and the quality of its decisions

Line the three facts up and the same structure appears. In every setting the work divides into "reading" and "deciding". Reading means gathering state, matching it, and putting it in a form one can decide on. Deciding means choosing what to do in front of that form. Traditionally people did the reading as well, and it sat behind the decision or outside the place where the decision is made. In review, people read every diff; in close, numbers were arranged after month-end; in chat, users interpreted prose.

These facts suggest that the source of value is not "automation of work" but "relocation of reading." The Claude Code team moved the reading, not the writing, to machines. OpenAI moved the effort of reading the answer, not the prose of the answer, to the machine. The opportunity a16z described is to move the reading that sat behind month-end to the front, in real time. Three parties use different technologies and put their hands on the same place.

From this a falsifiable principle follows. It is not that "as machine capability rises, human work shrinks." **The more reading is moved in front of the decision and carried by machines, the more human work concentrates in the quality of decisions, and the more an organisation's speed is set by how fast it decides rather than how late it reads.** The opposite prediction can be stated too: if reading moves to machines but people still re-read everything, simultaneous gains in throughput and quality will not appear. The Claude Code team's figures, pull requests more than doubled and reverts down more than 40%, fit the former.

There are limits to note. The evidence here is confined to what the three articles report. The Claude Code figures are one team's result, with no guarantee that other organisations see the same gain. Intelligent UI was introduced on 7 October 2026 and is new; the extracted article offers no figures on how users' decisions change. The a16z piece is from 2020 and describes the tooling of that time. The principle is induced from three facts, not proven.

## Predictions and implications

If the principle is right, the following should follow.

First, processes that rest their quality on "a human read it" will break as the volume to read grows. I rate this likely. The ground is that in an environment where pull requests more than doubled, a practice of people reading everything is physically hard to sustain. Designs that ask "what can break, and which check is appropriate?" will spread beyond review, for example to contract or audit checking. That is an extrapolation into areas the three articles do not touch, so my confidence there is only moderate.

Second, what users see will move from prose toward operable screens. Intelligent UI is the first large example. The bet is whether screens raise the quality of decisions or merely make pressing easier. Confidence is moderate: if a screen narrows the options too far, relocating the reading could produce biased decisions.

Third, work bound to a cycle such as the month will come under pressure to go real-time. The delay a16z pointed out in 2020 is, if machines can take on the reading, a delay that can be removed structurally. Confidence is moderate to high. But in domains such as accounting, where accountability attaches to a person, the question of who vouches for the machine's reading remains.

The practical takeaway reduces to one step. Split your own work into "reading" and "deciding", and list which of the hours people spend reading could be moved ahead to machines. The first question to ask is not "who read it?" but "what can break, and which check would be enough?" If the only reason the reading cannot move is "the rule says a human checks," that rule is probably protecting the form rather than the decision.
