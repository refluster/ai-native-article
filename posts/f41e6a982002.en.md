Three recent events around Anthropic look unrelated: METR's assessment of how fast the models' capabilities are growing, an IPO filing that could become one of the largest ever, and a federal district court ruling that the Defense Department's "supply chain risk" designation of Anthropic was unlawful. They can in fact be explained by one principle. **An outside evaluator can only reach conclusions as strong as the evidence the party under evaluation has shown, and that range is, in practice, largely set by that party.**

## Introduction: three evaluations, three kinds of judge

Each of the three source articles covers a different kind of judge. First, an AI evaluation group assessing capability growth ("METR's internal assessment: Anthropic's model improvement accelerates about 1.9x, but most of the decisive evidence is redacted"). Second, the market assessing a company's value ("Anthropic files for one of the largest IPOs ever — first-half revenue above $16 billion, net loss of $80.7 billion"). Third, a court assessing the lawfulness of a government action ("Federal court rules the Pentagon's 'supply chain risk' designation of Anthropic unlawful").

The subjects differ: capability, finances, legal status. So do the judges: an evaluation group, investors, a judge. Yet the structure is shared. None of the evaluators sits inside the company; each judges through the evidence the party supplied, or did not supply. This piece examines inductively how that structure caps the strength of an evaluation's conclusion.

## Capability: the growth can be measured, but the grounds are redacted

On 5 November 2026 METR announced that the capability Anthropic's models gain on well-defined tasks is growing about twice as fast as over the 19 months to October 2025; the headline gives about 1.9x. The main driver cited is Anthropic's own heavy use of AI in its research — in other words, a self-referential acceleration in which AI speeds up AI research.

The other fact in the article matters just as much: most of the evidence METR judged most informative is redacted. The material the evaluator calls decisive is invisible to readers. Outsiders see one number, "about 2x," while the strongest part of its support has been withheld at the discretion of the party being evaluated.

Readers are left with two options: trust METR's judgment or don't. The means of checking the substance are cut off by the extent of the redaction. What bounds the certainty of the conclusion is not only the evaluator's skill but what the evaluator was shown, and how much of that readers are shown in turn.

## Finances: a 335-page filing, with the price still undecided

On 26 October Anthropic filed documents for an IPO that could be among the largest ever. According to the 335-page filing, first-half 2026 revenue exceeded $16 billion, 14 times the same period a year earlier, and the net loss was $80.7 billion. Of that, $72.4 billion is a one-time accounting adjustment tied to financing agreements. Subtracting it leaves a loss of roughly $8.3 billion outside the adjustment. The same net loss reads entirely differently at $80.7 billion and at about $8.3 billion.

The amount to be raised, the valuation and the offer price are not yet fixed. The listing is planned on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker "ANT." Here the evaluator is the market, which folds its judgment into a single number, a price. But before that price exists, what investors hold is 335 pages written by the company and the accounting categories inside them. Which losses are labelled "one-time" is a line the company draws at the entrance to the evaluation.

In capability evaluation, redaction shapes the evaluator's field of view; in financial evaluation, the choice of categories does. The mechanisms differ, but the dynamic is the same.

## Legal status: even a finding of unlawfulness does not end the dispute

US District Judge Rita Lin ruled on Thursday that the Defense Department's designation of Anthropic as a "supply chain risk" was unlawful, concluding that the government's action violated constitutional rights. Unlike the other two cases, here the evaluator, a court, directly judges the conduct of the party being evaluated, the government. This is a setting where the evaluator has power to compel disclosure.

Even so, the conclusion does not close. The government is expected to appeal, and a separate dispute over a designation under a different statute continues in the D.C. Circuit. The same act of designation is the subject of separate proceedings depending on the statute relied on. One ruling has been issued, yet under another framework the question remains open.

What this shows is that even an evaluator with strong powers has a reach limited to "the evidence and statutes presented in that case." The district court could find one designation unlawful; it did not decide whether a designation under another statute is lawful. The strength of an evaluation is set not only by the evaluator's authority but by the portion of the matter that was carved out for evaluation.

## The common principle: the ceiling on evaluation is the scope of disclosure

Lined up, the three cases show one structure.

First, the evaluator is outside the party in every case: METR evaluates Anthropic's internal assessment, the market reads a filing, a court hears a government action. Second, the strength of each conclusion depends not on the evaluator's competence alone but on the range of material put before it. Redaction, accounting categories and statute-by-statute carving are different forms of the same thing — the design of what can be seen. Third, the power to design that range sits, not completely but disproportionately, with the party concerned.

These facts suggest the following principle: **the certainty of an external evaluation is capped not by the evaluator's independence but by the amount of evidence the evaluator can verify.** An independent evaluator must still suspend judgment on what it cannot see, or take the party's word for it. "An independent third party assessed it" does not mean "the assessment is verifiable."

The principle invites an objection. Where the evaluator can compel disclosure, as a court can, the party under evaluation should not set the ceiling; the evaluator should. But as the third case shows, even a court with compulsory power has its reach cut to the scope of the case brought. The objection does not break the principle; it only refines it: who sets the ceiling varies with the evaluator's powers, but a ceiling exists.

## Implications and predictions

Three changes follow from this principle.

First, in the IPO review process, the treatment of the one-time accounting adjustment that makes up $72.4 billion of the $80.7 billion net loss is likely to become a central point of evaluation (high confidence). The ground is that most of the loss sits in one category, and how that category is read changes the financial evaluation substantially. Whether the content of the adjustment is explained in further disclosure will shape how firm the market's evaluation can be.

Second, AI evaluation groups like METR may come under pressure to offer procedures by which third parties can verify redacted evidence, for example re-evaluation by another evaluator bound by confidentiality (medium confidence). The ground: while the most informative evidence is invisible, every citation of "about 2x" spreads a claim that cannot be checked.

Third, the dispute over the Defense Department's designation will not end with a single ruling (high confidence). The government's expected appeal and the separate case in the D.C. Circuit already point that way.

The practical takeaway for readers is simple. When you cite an evaluation's conclusion, check at the same time what the evaluator was able to see. The extent of redaction, the accounting category and the reach of the statute are written small beside the conclusion, but they are the variables that decide how firm it is. If this principle is wrong, later correction rates should not differ between evaluations with narrow disclosure and those with broad disclosure. Whether they differ is observable.
