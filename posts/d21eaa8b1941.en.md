## Executive Summary

An Engineering Manager (mootoh) at LayerX's Ai Workforce division built his own tooling to oversee a team of 13 to 20 people alongside AI, under a policy of adding no managers and splitting into no sub-teams. The AI is assigned "extending the range of observation" and creating openings for conversation rather than automating judgment, and its output is treated only as hypotheses to confirm with the person concerned. After six months, the main problem in his words was that the resolution is still coarse ("解像度が(まだ)荒い").

## Background: output grew faster than information could be tracked

In a post dated October 1, 2026, marking one year since he joined, the author shares what he worked on over the past half year. The comparison for the Ai Workforce development department between the first half (2025-10 to 2026-03) and the second half (2026-04 to 09) is:

- PR merges: 1,982 to 5,326
- Tickets: 606 to 1,389
- Authors (including people outside the development department): 63 to 76
- Diff: 5,971 files changed, +669,250 / -132,515 to 14,357 files changed, +2,178,240 / -177,232
- Files: 4,038 to 7,383 (1.83x), then 7,383 to 16,816 (2.28x)
- ADRs: 11 added to 85 added
The author attributes the growth not simply to handing development to AI, but to building guardrails such as review skills and automated test generation, and to turning best practices into skills to strengthen the organization. Full-time staff grew from a handful a year ago to a dozen or more.

There was a side effect. As activity grew, the density of information he needed to follow rose, and "conventional information gathering made it hard to see the movement of teams and projects." The post starts from there.

## Policy: a flat organization, with AI assumed in the loop

From April to September 2026 the team ranged from 13 to 20 people, about half of whom had joined within the previous one to two months. Having read sources including Block's blog, the author set this policy for the half year:

- Flatten the hierarchy and move nimbly, so do not add managers, so do not split into sub-teams
- Projects are born daily and run on different timescales, so keep assignments flexible
The result is that the author alone, as manager, oversees all the engineers and projects. He therefore tried an operating model that assumes AI accompaniment: have it collect daily information, and grasp the situation by talking with an agent.

## The mechanism: an Obsidian vault, skills, and routines

His personal app has two purposes: to continuously collect people's daily activity and fill in what he cannot see, and to produce insights for conversations such as 1on1s. The composition:

- Data accumulates as .md files in a dedicated Obsidian vault, and exists only on his local machine
- It can be read from Claude Code and similar tools, so he can consult the AI about each person's present and future
- Action items from 1on1s are managed in a self-made local Todo app, a customized version of one by teammate uphy
- Collection from sources is done by skills and tools, and scheduled runs by Claude Code or Shepherd routines
- A web app serves as a viewer to reference during conversations: a dashboard (team "weather" to member overview), a member screen (overview, load, OKRs, issues), a daily activity record tab, and a 1on1 record tab
The aim is to turn collected information into support for individuals and the team: surface prompts to check in with someone based on changes in communication or activity (expressed as weather icons), show how daily activity connects to OKR attainment, see who has which capabilities and how much load when weighing assignments for new priorities, and let retrospectives be grounded in facts.

The part he found interesting in experiments was a section proposing "what to praise this week." Sometimes he already knew; sometimes he did not, and it can make a 1on1 quietly lively. One example given is a member who merged a feature flag removal and a reviewed item on the same day and got them into the 10/1 release.

## The line: what the AI does and what people do

The author organizes the division of labor in a table:

- Collecting and organizing information / Human: checking with the person
- Summarizing past activity / Human: giving the situation meaning
- Proposing candidate discussion points / Human: deciding what to cover in the 1on1
- Showing signs of load or change / Human: assignments and check-ins on well-being
- Extracting candidates for praise / Human: choosing the words to tell the person
(AI on the left, human on the right.)

A note adds: "What the AI presents is treated strictly as hypotheses to confirm with the person; it is never used to determine psychological state or load, nor used as-is for evaluation." The opening framing, extending observation rather than automating judgment, is this line itself.

## Lessons: missing data, and the irreplaceable value of being on the ground

After roughly six months, the problem was that the resolution is still coarse. The author names three concrete issues:

- Data gaps are unavoidable. Sensitive meetings, conversations and documents fall outside collection, and sometimes a scheduled collection fails unnoticed.
- He made 1on1s irregular, held when an agenda emerged from the data and analysis. When busy, a month could pass. A degree of rhythm tailored to each person is better, he says.
- From April the number of parallel projects grew considerably and his resolution on each became coarse. The system was weak at grasping the state of projects and the people on them, but more than that, "there are things you can only get by getting more deeply involved on the ground yourself." Meet logs of meetings he does not attend are outside the collection scope.
Assuming he increases his own activity, the mechanism-level reinforcement he names is stronger metrics and alerts, positioned as support for the team's observability.

## Implication: a personal tool taught product requirements

LayerX develops Ai Workforce (AiW), an AI platform product. By building an app for himself, the author says he came to feel in his bones why the things AiW is working on are necessary. Three points:

- Ingesting data safely: fine while only he uses it, but deploying within an organization means much to consider, such as ensuring only the right people have access
- A scheduled-execution platform: it runs on his work MacBook because it is a personal app, but a platform capable of proper scheduling should be always on
- Improving from usage: compare the agenda prepared before a 1on1 with what was actually discussed, and have the AI improve how the next agenda is chosen
These are part of what the team implemented in AiW this half and is implementing now.

## Assessment: what can and cannot be said

**What the post supports.** The output growth (PR merges 1,982 to 5,326, and so on) is shown in the post's own table. That a single manager oversaw 13 to 20 people for the half year is the author's own account.

**What the post does not claim causally.** The post does not link the output growth directly to this AI-accompanied management. The causes the author gives for the output growth are guardrails such as review skills and automated test generation, and turning best practices into skills, which are written as separate from the effect of his own app. It cannot be read as "AI management doubled output."

**The design implication shown.** Placing extension of observation with the AI and meaning-making and word choice with humans holds up even when data is missing and resolution is coarse, because the AI's output is a hypothesis to confirm, not a conclusion. At the same time, as the author himself admits, what is gained by being on the ground is not replaceable by this system. The more the one-manager structure is kept, the more that one person's own activity becomes the bottleneck.
