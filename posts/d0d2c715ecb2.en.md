In 2026, the bottleneck in the AI race quietly moved one step upstream from the models themselves. At first glance, three developments seem unrelated. Micron bundled joint memory design, supply, and investment into a single agreement with Anthropic. Meta reassigned about 6,500 employees, without giving them any meaningful choice, to generate AI training data. And at that same Meta, 30–50% of core engineers are being forcibly transferred into data-labeling organizations, as the definition of the role itself is being rewritten. In fact, all of these can be explained by the same principle: demand for frontier models has turned the “inputs” at the very top of the supply chain into scarce resources, and companies are redrawing their own boundaries in order to secure them. Semiconductor partnerships and HR excesses are simply different expressions of the same force.

## Enclosing Memory as an Input Through Design, Supply, and Capital

What L2’s “Micron-Anthropic Strategic Partnership” describes is not a simple parts procurement agreement. On June 22, 2026, Micron announced a partnership with Anthropic that bundled together four domains into a single contract: joint design of AI architecture, supply agreements for data-center product lines (HBM, DRAM, and SSD), adoption of Claude within Micron, and a strategic investment in Anthropic’s Series H fundraising round.

What matters here is the logic of the bundle. Rather than treating memory as a “product” to be purchased from the market whenever needed, the two companies are co-designing it from the development stage, locking in supply through long-term contracts, and binding themselves through capital as well. Memory—supposedly an interchangeable commodity component—has been elevated into a strategic asset to be enclosed. Demand for frontier models is no longer primarily about “which GPU to buy,” but increasingly about “how to design memory bandwidth, how to secure it, and how to support it with capital.” L2 itself positions this partnership as a case in which demand has begun to rewrite the structure of the supply chain itself.

## Turning Human Judgment Into the Input Called Training Data

What L2’s “Meta ‘Conscripted’ 6,500 Employees Into an AI Unit” depicts is the same enclosure logic applied to human beings. Meta reassigned around 6,500 engineers and PMs into its “Applied AI” unit—established only 3 months earlier—without leaving them room to opt out, and put them to work generating puzzles and coding problems for AI training. The key point is that this happened after Meta acquired Scale AI for $14.3 billion. Even after spending an enormous sum to acquire a specialized data-supply company, its appetite for high-quality, human-origin training data remained unsatisfied, to the point that it had to redirect some of its most expensive talent into that supply line.

The intensity of that appetite is underscored by the ferocity of the backlash on the ground. Internal broadcasts were hijacked with abuse, employees described themselves as “conscripted soldiers,” and called the work they were assigned “literally the Gulag.” When the labor of engineers hired to build products was repurposed into labor for producing inputs to feed the model, they experienced it as mobilization—as conscription. The same movement by which Micron turned memory into a strategic asset is, here, being extended to human cognitive labor.

### Inputification Rewrites Job Functions and Metrics

L2’s “Why Meta’s Engineering Culture Broke Down” (Gergely Orosz of The Pragmatic Engineer, June 16, 2026) documents how this repurposing deforms an organization from the inside. According to the essay, following Meta’s acquisition of Scale AI in June 2025, the company forcibly reassigned 30–50% of engineers in core teams into the ADO organization responsible for data labeling. On top of that, keystroke monitoring was made mandatory, token counts were introduced into performance evaluation, and a 10% companywide workforce reduction was announced even amid record profits. Orosz sees this as a structural shift in which engineering is being redefined from a “profit center” into a “cost center.”

These three changes—forcible reassignment, surveillance, and evaluation by token count—can be read as the inevitable consequences of turning labor into an input. Once labor stops producing products and instead produces raw material, management measures it by output volume (= token count), and the process itself is standardized through surveillance. And a process that produces raw material is, by definition, treated not as the center of value creation but as the center of cost. The downgrading of the role is not an emotional complaint; it is the accounting image of labor having been transformed into an input.

## The Shared Principle: Competition Has Descended From the Model Layer to the Input Layer

On the surface, these three facts look like stories from different genres: “a semiconductor partnership,” “HR run amok,” and “an essay about corporate culture.” But place them side by side, and they reveal the same movement: in order to secure the scarce inputs that power frontier models—memory semiconductors on one side, human cognitive labor on the other—companies are moving toward vertical integration (Micron-Anthropic) and internal mobilization plus surveillance (Meta).

Together, these facts suggest the following. The bottleneck that determines who wins or loses in AI has shifted away from model architecture and model performance themselves, and toward the supply chains for the inputs that support them. Models, at least for the time being, are heading toward commoditization. What is truly scarce are two things: the HBM bandwidth that can be fed into models, and the high-quality training data that only humans can produce. That is why, both externally and internally, we are seeing simultaneous moves to elevate inputs from “things that can be bought on the market” into “strategic assets that must be enclosed.” Micron’s bundled agreement extends the corporate boundary outward to absorb the upstream supply chain, while Meta’s conscription rewires labor inside the corporate boundary into a supply line for inputs. The directions are opposite, but the principle is one and the same: redraw the boundary in order to secure scarce inputs.

This principle is falsifiable. One could argue that Meta’s reorganization is simply about cost cutting or managerial drift, unrelated to the supply of inputs. However, the fact that Meta still needed to mobilize human labor even after acquiring Scale AI for $14.3 billion, and the fact that its evaluation metric shifted to token count—the “output volume of inputs”—both point to the securing of inputs as the true driving force.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, several developments are likely to continue with high probability. First, Micron-Anthropic-style contracts that bundle design, supply, and capital will spread beyond memory to other scarce inputs—power, cooling, and proprietary data. Now that the “template” has been established, this is the most probable next step. Second, the conversion of human labor into inputs will accelerate further, and token-count evaluation, surveillance, and cost-centerization will become more common at other companies as well. At the same time, the backlash this produces—the rebellion of the “conscripted soldiers”—and the resulting talent outflow will eventually surface as hidden costs that were initially invisible. Third, competitive moats will be determined less by model benchmark scores than by who secured scarce inputs first—HBM supply allotments, and proprietary pipelines of human judgment.

This forecast, too, is falsifiable. If the future AI race is once again determined by superiority in model architecture—for example, if training methods that do not require human-origin data become dominant—then the thesis that competition has moved to the input layer will prove wrong. What we should watch is the number of contracts that bundle supply and capital, the scale of human mobilization, and how many companies adopt token count as a core evaluation metric.

The practical implication for readers is clear. If you want to understand who will dominate AI, do not watch the model leaderboards; watch the input layer. Who controls semiconductor supply allotments, and who controls the supply line for training data? And for workers, the role of “engineer” is set to polarize into two tracks: those positioned close to the model, and those made to produce inputs for it. The line dividing those two paths will become one of the defining career fault lines of the years ahead.
