## Victory or Defeat in AI Adoption Will Be Determined Not by “Model Performance,” but by the Management Mechanism That Mass-Produces Implementation

## Introduction

An article aimed at CEOs argues that AI adoption should not be treated as a series of scattered experiments, but should instead be scaled like an “assembly line.” Another article reports that **16 non-engineer executives built a “management secretary” on their PCs in just 2 and a half hours**. Meanwhile, Anthropic has launched a research institute that treats the development site of frontier models itself as an object of observation, tracking economic impact, threats, and social implementation.

At first glance, these facts seem unrelated. In reality, they can be explained by the same principle. **The value of AI does not come from a smart model alone, but from a system of institutions, tools, and observation that converts it into a repeatable operational unit.**

## Analysis 1: Pilot Failures Happen Not Because of Weak Technology, but Because of the Absence of a “Mass-Production Mechanism”

The core point of the first article is straightforward. Companies can achieve “major productivity gains” with AI, but only if they **industrialize adoption itself, rather than simply increasing the number of individual PoCs**. The article frames AI deployment as an “assembly line,” presenting the idea of standardizing each step: use-case selection, data connection, governance, evaluation, and rollout. What matters here is that AI is being treated not as a one-off act of ingenuity, but as a **reproducible operation**.

Seen from this perspective, the reason AI adoption stalls inside companies also comes into sharper focus. In many organizations, one department experiments with a chatbot, another with meeting-summary generation, and yet another with sales support. But each effort ends as a **scattered pilot**. What the article suggests is that the cause of failure is not model accuracy itself, but the absence of **shared components for scaling horizontally across the organization**.

These facts suggest that the unit of AI monetization is not “one successful case,” but “the ability to replicate successful cases.” Even if one PoC succeeds, if evaluation metrics differ by department, if the method for obtaining data permissions varies each time, and if legal review must be handled case by case, then the cost of the second and third deployments does not fall. In other words, the management issue has shifted from “Can AI be used?” to **How do we lower the marginal cost of AI adoption?**

## Analysis 2: What Happened in 2 and a Half Hours Shows Progress Not in Skill Acquisition, but in the “Externalization of Cognition”

What is especially symbolic in the second article is the fact that **16 non-engineer executives were able, in just 2 and a half hours, to have their own personal “management secretary” on a PC**. This figure is not merely the outcome of a training session. In the past, if an executive wanted custom work-support software tailored to their own needs, it would have required a long process of requirements definition, outsourcing, prototyping, and revision. What made it possible in such a short time was not simply that AI had democratized programming itself, but rather that **vague knowledge and decision criteria could now be externalized on the spot and converted into something functional**.

According to the article, the conventional division-of-labor development structure—in which planning, design, and implementation proceed in sequence—is now being shaken. This is critically important. With AI in the loop, users can describe “what they want to do” in natural language, obtain a prototype immediately, and then redefine the requirements while looking at it. In other words, requirements definition and implementation feed back into one another, and the boundary between them becomes blurred.

This phenomenon means more than just faster software development. Because tacit knowledge that once existed only in the minds of decision-makers can now be prototyped at far lower cost than before, the organization’s information-processing structure itself begins to change.

These facts suggest that AI will not first spread as a “replacement for specialists,” but rather as an **interface that directly expands the working environment of decision-makers themselves**. The fact that 16 executives achieved results in a short time tells us not only that training costs are falling, but that the barrier to adoption has shifted from “the ability to write code” to “the ability to articulate one’s work in language.”

## Analysis 3: What the Anthropic Institute Is Observing Is Not Technology, but the “Measurement Infrastructure for Social Implementation”

The article on the Anthropic Institute is not adequately understood if read merely as news of a new AI research institution. Through the Institute, Anthropic is trying to track **economic impact, threats, social implementation, and even AI’s self-accelerating effects**, while also using internal data from frontier labs. The key point here is that Anthropic is investing not only in “building good models,” but in **a mechanism for continuously measuring how those models spread through society and what kinds of side effects or institutional challenges they create**.

This connects directly to enterprise AI adoption as well. The more an organization pushes toward company-wide rollout, the more the problem shifts away from model capability and toward auditing, risk detection, usage visibility, and impact assessment. Anthropic’s emphasis on research using internal data reflects its understanding that the essence of social implementation lies in **observing the changes that occur after deployment**.

For example, even if a given AI function reduces working hours, you cannot know which job categories benefited disproportionately, which tasks saw an increase in hallucinations, or under which authority structures abuse risk rose, unless you measure those outcomes continuously after deployment.

These facts suggest that competitive advantage in the AI era lies not only in training data or model size, but in **the ability to adjust institutions using observational data gathered from real-world operation**. In other words, not only the “power to build,” but also the “power to measure,” is becoming central to AI social implementation.

## Presenting the Common Principle

So what deep principle explains these three articles in a unified way? Inductively organized, it looks like this.

First, the CEO article showed that the unit of AI value creation is not the individual use case, but a **standard process that makes deployment repeatable**.

Second, the article about 16 executives in 2 and a half hours showed that AI reduces dependence on specialists and **turns the tacit knowledge of decision-makers into software instantly**.

Third, the Anthropic Institute article showed that the success or failure of social implementation depends on **the ability to measure post-deployment impact and revise institutions accordingly**.

Taken together, these facts suggest that the essence of AI lies not in “providing intelligence,” but in **transforming intelligence into an operational unit that can be replicated, connected, and supervised within an organization**. Put differently, the substance of the AI revolution is not an algorithmic revolution, but a **revolution in the organization’s information-processing architecture**.

Traditional IT improved efficiency by placing standardized operations onto systems. AI, by contrast, brings even previously vague and fluid judgment, dialogue, and exploration into the domain of processing. As a result, value comes less from the intelligence of the model alone than from a three-part set: 1) interfaces that rapidly formalize frontline knowledge, 2) standardized components that can be rolled out horizontally, and 3) an observational infrastructure that measures impact after deployment and enables correction.

That is the “Why.” Whether a company can generate results with AI depends less on whether it can access superior models than on **how quickly it can run the cycle of knowledge externalization → implementation → observation → redesign**.

And the “So What” is clear. In management going forward, AI strategy will mean not selecting tools, but **internalizing this cycle as a company-wide operation**.

## Forecasts and Implications

The future implied by this principle is fairly clear. First, what is highly likely to happen in the next 2〜3 years is that the main battleground for enterprise AI use will shift from “model selection” to “competition over implementation infrastructure.” The probability is high. As the articles already show, even executives have reached the point where they can get a work-support agent in **2 and a half hours**, and the room for differentiation based on core capability alone is shrinking rapidly. As a result, the differentiators will be foundational elements such as authentication, data connection, access control, audit logs, and evaluation metrics.

Second, in the medium term, companies that succeed in AI adoption are likely to move from “department-level optimization” to a “cross-functional AI operating model.” As the CEO article’s “assembly line” framing suggests, even if PoCs are produced at scale, company-wide gains will not be maximized without shared evaluation axes and reusable components. Conversely, companies that can share templated prompts, workflows, review procedures, and guardrails will see increasing returns in deployment speed.

Third, on the regulation and governance front, the Anthropic Institute approach—namely, **continuous observation based on internal data**—is likely to become standard. It will no longer be enough simply to say, “We have an AI use policy.” Organizations will be expected to build systems that track which departments are using AI, for which tasks, to what extent, and what benefits and incidents result. This is both a compliance response and a source of learning speed.

There are three practical implications.

The first is to change the KPI of the AI promotion office from the number of PoCs to **reuse rate, deployment speed, and post-deployment impact measurement rate**.

The second is not to leave usage promotion to the front lines, but for the executive team itself to use AI in its own work and verbalize its tacit knowledge. The example of the 16 executives is symbolic of this.

The third is to design observation at the same time as deployment. If you do not record who used what for what purpose, what improved, and where failures occurred, the assembly line does not learn.

In the end, the winner of AI social implementation will not necessarily be the company with the highest-performance model. The winner will be **the company that builds management operations capable of mass-producing, measuring, and correcting intelligence in an organized way**. The essence of the change now underway is not AI adoption. It is the implementation-ization of management itself.
