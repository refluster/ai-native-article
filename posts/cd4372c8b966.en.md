What separates outcomes is not how much you put in, but which single point you choose first. Enterprise AI budgets are now in place, and token costs are no longer the binding constraint. Alignment has propagated broadly from small amounts of data. And Elon Musk argues that “delete” should come before optimization. These three facts may seem unrelated at first glance, but they are actually explained by the same principle: a small number of correct choices generalize across the entire system.

## Where Quantity Constraints Have Fallen Away

What the “RBC CIO survey” shows first is that, in enterprise AI, the constraint of “quantity” has quietly fallen away. In a semiannual spending survey conducted by RBC Capital Markets of more than 100 technology leaders, more than half of respondents said that “AI is already in production,” while 35% expected to move into production within six months. Adoption is moving from proof of concept into live deployment.

What is easy to miss here is the treatment of token budgets. In the same survey, 90% said their budgets were “manageable.” In other words, the cost once seen as constraining generative AI adoption—that is, the amount of compute being put in—is no longer the dominant bottleneck. The fact that 100% of respondents had an AI/LLM budget, and that 91% had created a new budget for it, also shows that the quantitative investment decision has already been made.

What remains is a question of choice. By vendor, ChatGPT was the most frequently used at 57%, far ahead of Claude at 12%. In a world where budgets are in place and costs are manageable, what differentiates companies is no longer “how much they spend,” but the choice of “what they put into production, and which foundation they bet on.”

The shift itself—from “proof of concept to production”—is evidence of exactly that. At the proof-of-concept stage, companies can try many use cases lightly and broadly—you can get by by putting in quantity. But moving into production means narrowing down which of the tested candidates will sit at the core of the business, and that is fundamentally a matter of selection. The fact that 100% have budgeted and 91% have created new budgets means companies are no longer hesitating over whether to do this at all. The hesitation has moved entirely to “what, and on which foundation.” The concentration of 57% on ChatGPT shows that this choice is already converging early on a small number of platforms—that a small number of choices are beginning to set the standard for the ecosystem as a whole.

## Narrow Training, Broad Effect

“OpenAI’s Beneficial RL research” shows the same structure from inside the technology. In June 2026, OpenAI’s Alignment team reported experiments using reinforcement learning on small amounts of data aimed at “beneficial traits” such as honesty, corrigibility, and metacognitive transparency. The result was that alignment improvements generalized to 44 of 53 benchmarks not used in training, and moreover persisted even under adversarial prompts and fine-tuning. Particularly suggestive was the fact that training only in the health domain still improved evaluations in non-health domains.

What matters here, again, is that what worked was not the amount of training data being put in. Even in small quantities, if the trait chosen as the seed is the right one, the effect spreads across the entire system. A generalization rate of 44 out of 53 reaches a scope that could never be achieved by tackling each training target one by one; this is a number attained not through “coverage” but through “spillover.” Further, the fact that the improvement persisted even under the pressure of adversarial prompts and fine-tuning suggests that the generalization is not superficial memorization, but the acquisition of a deeper property.

This research also offers early evidence that “emergent misalignment,” in which narrow bad behavior produces broad inconsistency, can be induced in reverse—deliberately, in a desirable direction. In other words, generalization as an amplifier spreads either good or bad initial choices across the whole. The fact that training only in the health domain improved evaluations even in non-health domains means that the choice propagates across domain boundaries as well. Restated in the idiom of software development practice, it points to a shift in design philosophy: quality and safety can be ensured not by “inspecting every single location individually,” but by “selecting the right small number of properties upstream.” What determines whether an intervention succeeds is not scale, but which single point you choose.

## Sequence Determines Value

“Elon Musk’s ‘algorithm’” translates this principle into a decision-making procedure. The core of the five-step process Musk repeats at SpaceX and Tesla is that the value lies less in the steps themselves than in the “sequence.” Most people begin with optimization, acceleration, and automation, but Musk puts “question every requirement” and “delete” first.

The reason is condensed in Musk’s own words: “The most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist.” Optimization is an act of pouring in effort. But if you get the object of that effort wrong, then the quantity you poured in is wasted in its entirety. That is why the choice of “what to keep and what to remove” comes first in the sequence. Before you commit quantity, you fully determine the correct single point worth committing it to—that is precisely what Musk’s procedure teaches.

What is doing the work here is not the skill with which each step is executed, but the structure of the sequence. Even if you perform the same five tasks, putting optimization first fixes effort onto the wrong target, and even if you arrive at “delete” later, the loss cannot be recovered. Put deletion first, by contrast, and all subsequent acceleration and automation compound on top of the right target. In other words, the initial choice determines the efficiency of all subsequent effort in one stroke. This has the same structure as the previous two cases—a small upstream choice generalizes across the entire downstream system—restated in the language of organizational decision processes.

## A Shared Principle

Taken together, these three facts suggest the same thing. **What determines outcomes is not the amount of input, but the selection of a small number of correct points, and the way that selection generalizes across the entire system.** Put differently, leverage has moved from “scale” to “position.”

The inductive path is straightforward. In the RBC survey, the quantitative constraint of cost fell away, leaving only the choice of “what to put into production” as the source of difference. In the Beneficial RL research, it was not the amount of data, but the choice of the seed traits that generalized improvement across 44 benchmarks. In Musk’s procedure, it was not the quantity of optimization effort, but the choice of “delete,” and the ordering that put it first, that determined the value of the whole process. In all three cases, outcomes are determined not on the axis of “increase the quantity,” but on the axis of “choose the right single point.”

And there is one more common element running through all three: the existence of generalization as an amplifier. Because a narrow choice spreads across the whole, an error in the initial choice also propagates across the whole—optimizing something that should not exist, or emergent misalignment arising from narrow bad behavior, are simply its negative manifestations. For the same reason that the amplifier exists, correct choices move the whole cheaply, while incorrect choices become expensive.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, then several changes can be predicted with high confidence.

First, competitive advantage in enterprise AI will shift from budget size to the precision of use-case selection. If token budgets are “manageable” for 90% and investment has become table stakes, then what creates differentiation is not spending volume but “what was put into production.” The numbers in the RBC survey are themselves the basis for that view, and the confidence level is high.

Second, the way safety is built in will move forward in time—from large-scale patching after the fact to the selection of a small number of traits at the initial stage of training. What Beneficial RL showed is the existence of a path in which, if you choose the right seeds, small amounts can work broadly. The idea of alignment as something you “fix later, in bulk” will give way to the idea of “choosing the right traits first.”

The practical implication is clear. Before starting anything, ask: “Is this the right single point worth optimizing?” Institutionalize Musk’s sequence, and question the choice before increasing the quantity. In a world where generalization is powerful, the precision of the first move determines everything that follows.

This claim is falsifiable. If quantity is still dominant—if more data, more budget, and more effort still produce results linearly—then the principle in this article is wrong. But three independent pieces of evidence all point in the opposite direction.
