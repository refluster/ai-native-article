Fake "Forward Deployed Engineer" job postings, senior engineers reduced to rubber-stamping AI output, and AI-generated books now accounting for roughly 40% of self-published sales — three seemingly unrelated phenomena share one mechanism: a proxy signal (a title, a role, an artifact) quietly detaching from the process it was supposed to certify. In fact, all three can be explained by the same principle. When AI drives the cost of imitating a proxy toward zero while the cost of actually performing the underlying process stays flat, the proxy stops being evidence that the process happened.

## "FDE" alone proves nothing

An analysis by product-development specialist Shin (@shin_sasaki19), shared on X — call it "How to Spot a Fake FDE Job Posting" — points out that the title Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is inflating fast. FDE is neither a licensed occupation nor a role with an industry-wide, rigorously agreed definition, so relabeling an ordinary staffing or implementation-support contract as "FDE" costs almost nothing, and no job posting's wording alone can stop it.

The diagnostic the piece proposes is precise: the axis that matters isn't where you work (on-site with the client or not), but whether the person actually owns the full work loop — problem discovery → design → implementation → production rollout → adoption and outcome measurement → generalization → product improvement. Owning only a slice of that loop, say implementation alone or rollout support alone, doesn't earn the title. What's telling is that this diagnostic had to be invented at all: because the cost of copying the label collapsed, verifying it now requires the extra step of confirming ownership of the entire loop.

## An engineer who only approves never goes through the process that made them senior

SQL-performance specialist Pinal Dave's argument — call it "It's Not Just the Junior Chair That's Disappearing" — describes a structure hiding behind the debate over AI replacing junior hires: existing seniors are quietly de-skilling as their work shifts to merely approving AI-generated output. His central claim is a qualitative distinction: earlier tools eliminated *typing*; generative AI eliminates *reasoning* itself — and reasoning, repeated over years, was exactly the practice ground that built senior expertise in the first place.

The structure here mirrors the FDE case. A "senior" title was originally evidence of a process: years of working through problems, failing, and correcting course by one's own reasoning. Now, an engineer whose job has shifted to approving AI-generated code or drafts keeps the title while no longer passing through the process that first earned it. The cost of copying the title (tenure, a role name on a badge) is near zero; the cost of actually performing the process (reasoning it through yourself) hasn't fallen at all — which is exactly why the two pull apart.

## What the 40% bookslop number marks as a threshold

a16z's Moses Sternstein, in "Charts of the Week: Bookslop" (published August 7, 2026), shows a domain where this same gap has already scaled to move an entire market. The paper he cites — "Generative AI floods and dilutes the market for books," by researchers at Stony Brook, Columbia, Michigan, and MIT's Initiative on the Digital Economy — reports that books detected as containing AI-generated text now account for roughly 40% of observed sales.

A "book" was supposed to be evidence of a process: an author researching, structuring, and revising. AI has driven the cost of reproducing the artifact's surface form — the word count, the formatting, the shape of a book — toward zero, while the cost of actually doing the research and building the argument hasn't moved. The same issue juxtaposes a surge in defense-industry order backlogs, IT-services valuation multiples compressing to a third of their prior level, and demand shifting toward Moonshot's "Kimi K3" — each a separate market showing the same shape of reorganization: once supply cost collapses in a sector, the old evaluation metrics stop measuring substance. A roughly 40% figure reads as a level close to the point where a proxy loses collective credibility.

## The unifying principle

The three facts — the hollowing-out of the FDE title, the hollowing-out of the senior title, and the hollowing-out of the book as an artifact — look, on the surface, like separate stories about occupations, careers, and publishing. What they share is that each proxy (a title, a role, an artifact) originally functioned as evidence that a process had been performed. Performing that process costs time and cognitive effort, so until recently, acquiring the proxy without going through the process didn't pay off. That imbalance was precisely the reason the proxy could be trusted.

What AI changed isn't the cost of the process — it's the cost of imitating the proxy. Writing the copy for a job posting, holding onto a title, mass-producing text shaped like a book: AI has pushed all of these toward zero. Meanwhile, the cost of actually running the loop from problem discovery to production rollout, of reasoning through a problem yourself, of researching and structuring an argument, hasn't changed before or after AI. These facts suggest that whether a proxy keeps functioning as evidence of substance depends not on the nature of the indicator itself, but on whether the ratio between the cost of copying the proxy and the cost of performing the process holds.

What happens once that ratio breaks down? At first, proxies acquired without the underlying process enter the market as a minor share of noise. Evaluators — hiring managers, readers, reviewers — keep trusting the proxy as long as verifying it costs more than the expected loss from being wrong; call this "rational non-verification." But once the share of process-free proxies crosses some level, the expected reliability of the proxy category as a whole drops, and evaluators stop trusting the proxy itself and start demanding evidence of the process's trace — loop ownership, demonstrated reasoning, a documented research record. The roughly 40% bookslop figure, and the fact that the FDE market now needs a "work-loop ownership" diagnostic at all, are evidence that these domains have already crossed, or are close to crossing, that threshold.

## What comes next, and what it means for you

If this principle holds, the next development is a race to redesign proxies. The more likely path is that evaluators stop doubting the proxy category wholesale and instead start requiring proxies that embed a trace of the process. Hiring will lean less on a title like "FDE" and more on a portfolio proving the scope of the loop actually owned; publishing will lean less on the book format and more on a documented record of research and revision. This shift will be gradual, and it will show up fastest in domains where the cost of copying the proxy is already near zero — low-barrier, easily-imitated markets like hiring and publishing are the likeliest to cross the threshold first.

A second prediction is less certain but shouldn't be ignored. Senior engineers' de-skilling is more dangerous than the other two cases in one specific way: it's the hardest for the person involved to notice. A faked FDE title or an inflated book catalog can be detected by a third party; an engineer reduced to approving AI output has no easy way to notice their own reasoning capacity eroding. If this case does cross its threshold, the exposure won't come from a market re-rating the title — it will come the moment that engineer, after a long gap, tries to solve a hard problem with nothing but their own reasoning and finds they can't.

The practical takeaway for readers reduces to one habit: whenever you hold, or evaluate, a title or an artifact, ask what portion of the underlying process it is actually evidence of. A title that can't answer that question concretely is probably a proxy whose imitation cost has already collapsed toward zero — and sooner or later, it will stop being trusted on its own.
