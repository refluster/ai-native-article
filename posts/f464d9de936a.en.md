## Executive Summary

Joe Schmidt IV draws a clear line between the winners and losers among AI application companies based on whether they step onto the “Yellow Brick Road.” His core argument is that defensibility will not come from going after the horizontal, general-purpose workflows that OpenAI and Anthropic are pursuing with massive investment, but from owning the “system of work” in areas that require complex business processes, multi-step approvals, regulatory compliance, and integration with existing systems.

The question is not whether “the application layer is dead.” The real question is which parts of the application layer are likely to be absorbed by the labs, and which can build lasting differentiation through data accumulation, model operations, cost optimization, and governance.

## Defining the Yellow Brick Road — The Horizontal Territory the Labs Are Pushing Into With Capital, Models, and Distribution

Briefly: The article begins by rejecting the view that the AI application market is one monolithic category. It then defines “The Yellow Brick Road” as the areas where the labs are deploying “extraordinary resources,” and warns of the risks of entering those markets head-on.

- **Evidence**
- The author writes that founders and candidates repeatedly ask him, “is there any AI application layer left to build, or are OpenAI and Anthropic going to kill everything?”
- He defines it this way: “The Yellow Brick Road is our shorthand for the path the labs are walking, where they’re committing extraordinary resources.”
- He argues that code generation, writing, and image-creation are the kinds of problems where “every dollar spent on pre-training and post-training improves product quality,” making them well suited to the labs.
- As a dangerous archetype, he points to a product architecture that simply takes a “high performing model,” plugs in “off-the-shelf connectors (like G Drive, Slack, Salesforce, Notion, GitHub),” and adds an “agentic orchestration layer” on top.
- He says this overlaps directly with what the labs are building in “Cowork and Codex,” and notes that the labs “own the model,” giving them “better margins, control, and the ability to exert pricing power.”
- He further notes that the labs have “massive distribution arms and the biggest brand halo in AI.”
- He states it plainly: “If you’re an AI app company running that playbook with the same connectors, no sub-agents or configuration below it, and no distribution, you’re likely walking down the road to nowhere.”
- **Conclusion**
- Horizontal applications built by wiring general-purpose models to off-the-shelf connectors are structurally disadvantaged against the labs across four dimensions: product architecture, margins, pricing power, and distribution.
- As long as you compete there, your point of differentiation is likely to collapse into “the top layer of model capability,” meaning improvements from the labs become direct competitive pressure.
- It is not that “the application layer” is inherently doomed. What is fragile is the application layer that shares the labs’ design philosophy, uses the same surface-level connectors, and targets the same use cases.
## “The Rest of Oz” — The Defensive Line Created by Complex Work, Multi-Step Approval, and Legacy Integration

Briefly: The article’s alternative is to build not horizontal tools, but complex operational systems embedded in industries and job functions. The source of value is not the model alone, but the software, approval paths, rules, integrations, and operational reliability built around it.

- **Evidence**
- The author describes “The Rest of Oz” as “more complex, often vertical problems,” where the real value lies in “the scaffolding around it that makes the output trustworthy, compliant, and operational inside a specific industry.”
- He argues that OpenAI and Anthropic themselves are effectively signaling that “they can’t solve every problem with a generic AI coworker,” as shown by their announcement of “massive forward-deployed joint ventures” to configure and customize models for enterprise customers.
- He writes, “You don’t pour billions into those programs if you think the next model release is going to take care of it.”
- Companies in the Rest of Oz weave models into “a complex web of tools, automations, and integrations,” and as a result become “vertical by default.”
- The target work is “multi-step and multi-player work,” involving “sub-agents for role- and vertical-specific tasks,” context gathered from multiple systems, multiple human approvals, legacy systems, deterministic outcomes, and direct linkage to a specific business outcome.
- He says the size of the opportunity is also reflected in the fact that the labs are building their own “outsourced configuration shops,” and that “an entire upmarket class of reinforcement learning businesses” has emerged.
- **Conclusion**
- Defensible AI applications must go beyond one-off responses and horizontal assistance, and instead embed themselves in the execution layer of complex work itself.
- The domains least vulnerable to replacement through model improvement alone are those entangled with business procedures, approval accountability, consistency with existing systems, and auditability.
- The fact that the labs themselves are adopting a forward-deployed build model is treated as counter-evidence to the idea that “a generic AI coworker” is enough.
## The Four Elements of Defensibility — Data Learning, Model Operations, Cost Optimization, and Governance

Briefly: The article responds to the objection that Rest of Oz companies will eventually be absorbed by the labs by laying out four sources of defensibility. The common thread is “focus” — staying deeply attached to a specific customer set, workflow, and regulatory environment.

- **Evidence**
- **Data and learning flywheels**
- He argues that “unwritten industry norms, undocumented standards, the tribal knowledge that lives in practitioners’ heads” does not exist on the public web or in training data.
- He says there are two layers to the flywheel: one “across-customer one,” and another “within-customer one.”
- For example, a company that has internalized the shape of the problem through “a hundred legal redlines,” “a thousand insurance underwriting cycles,” or “ten thousand SDR campaigns” cannot be matched by a new entrant “spinning up a fresh agent for the first time.”
- In theory, a horizontal agent could build a similar learning base, but in practice, he argues, it cannot design the “workflow surfaces” needed to elicit the right knowledge as effectively as a vertical player can.
- He points to “Eval sets, labeled outputs, and edge-case taxonomies” as possible vertical-specific data flywheels.
- **Managing model variability and complexity**
- He says the labs themselves route internally across “different model classes” and “ensembles under the hood,” but cannot route “across vendors,” which means they cannot optimize customer-specific subtasks using competing models or open-source fine-tunes.
- Rest of Oz companies take responsibility for “re-running evals on upgrades, recalibrating prompts for the customer’s edge cases, rolling out without breaking production.”
- By contrast, he depicts the labs as saying, “sell you their next model and tell you to migrate,” leaving the burden of absorbing the migration to the customer.
- **Cost optimization**
- He states directly: “Running every query through Opus 4.7 is the fastest path to negative gross margins.”
- Strong companies route work by difficulty across “frontier models,” “mid-tier,” and “smaller custom or fine-tuned models.”
- Some, he says, use post-training to optimize their own models for narrow workflows and deliver them at “a fraction of the cost of a frontier API call.”
- He contrasts the two models by saying the labs price “the least intelligence available at $X,” while Rest of Oz companies sell “the lowest dollar cost for the specific level of intelligence the workflow actually requires.”
- **Governance**
- He identifies one high-value role as becoming the customer’s “control plane” for AI operations.
- That is where permissions, auditing, allowed actions, and actual actions all converge.
- Guardrails differ by industry: legal is governed by “FRCP and bar rules,” healthcare by “HIPAA,” finance by “SEC and FINRA,” and also “state insurance regulations.”
- He writes, “CIOs want to have a partner that contractually states they are handling compliance for the agents they are providing.”
- He sums it up this way: “All of these come back to the same thing: focus.” The target is “a vertical (insurance, legal, accounting) or a function done deeply (sales, customer support, finance).”
- He describes the labs’ structural constraint with the line, “you can be everywhere at once, or you can be great at one thing. Not both.”
- **Conclusion**
- The heart of the moat is not model ownership, but the bundle of private knowledge, evaluation infrastructure, exception taxonomies, migration operations, pricing design, and regulatory responsibility gained through repeated production deployment.
- What customers need is not “direct access to the strongest model,” but an operating layer that delivers consistent quality, price, and auditability at the workflow level.
- Because the labs’ strength is generality, they are structurally ill-suited to deeply embedding themselves in a specific workflow.
## The Practical Principles Seen in 11x and FurtherAI — The “Workflow Itself” Carries Intelligence

Briefly: The article uses Sales and Insurance to show concretely where vertical or company-specific players create differentiation. In both examples, the core is not the model alone, but task decomposition, the non-agentic parts of the system, guardrails, exception handling, human approval, and continuous learning.

- **Evidence**
- **11x / Prabhav Jain**
- He says the starting point was “helping companies generate more pipeline.”
- From there, the company looked at the activity end-to-end, decomposed it into tasks, and sorted out “Which tasks are agentic and which aren’t. Which require intricate domain insight and which don’t.”
- He lists the tasks 11x handles as “lead prospecting based on custom signals, lead enrichment, deep account research, context fetcher from CRM, channel-specific message writer, lead qualification agent, and email deliverability system.”
- These, he says, are “These aren’t tasks you can just one-shot and require deep engineering.”
- The author adds that “roughly half of any real workflow that is non-agentic carries no lab advantage.”
- In 11x’s operation, the system has to learn what a “good sales conversation” looks like by industry and persona.
- As an example of adaptation becoming a moat, he notes that people’s sense for spotting AI-generated email changes “every few months.”
- Even in that environment, the company claims, “our positive reply rates have gone up 4x in the last few months and we’ve generated hundreds of millions in pipeline for our customers.”
- As an example of complexity, he points to what seems like a simple problem — not accidentally doing outreach to an existing customer — and explains how data quality breaks down through subsidiaries, parent domain relationships, and stale matching fields in Salesforce.
- As a result, he writes, “the quality and freshness of our data is much higher than our customers, so by default, we anchor on our own.”
- On guardrails, he notes that the required assurances differ between a regulated financial services prospect and a mid-market SaaS customer, and can extend to who may be contacted, what can be written, what data may be touched, what can be said on a call, and how it must be recorded.
- That, he says, is why “FDEs and technical deployment strategists” are required.
- As a concrete example, he describes “consented outbound via voice to their large SMB customer base” for “a F1000 institution.” Initially, pickup rates were low, but after learning how to hook prospects in the first “10s,” the result was: “We now generate more sales opportunities for them in a day than their entire sales team for that segment in a month.”
- **FurtherAI / Aman Gour**
- In insurance operations, he argues, the assumption that “the model is the intelligence, and the workflow is just scaffolding around it” is backwards.
- He states it directly: “In insurance, a lot of the intelligence lives inside the workflow itself.”
- Even when the surface flow looks the same — submission, review, quote, bind — what actually matters is “which risks get escalated, which loss signals matter, which appetite rule wins when two of them conflict, when a human has to sign off, which external data gets pulled in, and how the final decision gets documented.”
- That logic is distributed across “SOPs, manager reviews, underwriting philosophy, carrier-specific appetite, and years of operational experience,” much of it undocumented.
- As a result, they use neither “a pure agent that reasons from scratch every time” nor “a rigid workflow that breaks the moment reality gets messy,” but instead build “agentic workflows.”
- In that structure, the workflow provides “repeatability, auditability, and cost control,” the agent handles variability and recovery when execution falls off the happy path, and humans handle the judgment calls that require accountability.
- In production, “every escalation becomes a signal, every exception is feedback and every human correction shows where the runbook was incomplete,” and the workflow becomes the carrier’s “operating memory.”
- The real moat, he says, is this: “The workflow you ship on day one is not the moat. The loop that production usage creates over time is.”
- **Conclusion**
- The 11x example shows that when you decompose work from the standpoint of outcomes, a large deterministic software layer remains where the labs have no inherent advantage.
- The FurtherAI example shows that operational intelligence is distributed not inside the model, but inside the workflow, including exceptions, approvals, and operational memory.
- What both examples share is that the real product is not the agent by itself, but the combination of agent, workflow, human-in-the-loop, and guardrails.
## Three Tests to Determine Whether Your Company Is on the “Oz Side”

Briefly: The article closes with a set of concrete questions for determining whether your company is on a path likely to be swallowed by the labs, or instead occupies a defensible position.

- **Evidence**
- **The tools-and-steps test**
- Ask how many steps the work requires, and how complex the supporting tools are.
- As an example, “horizontal AI search across Google Drive” is “one step against one tool with a forgiving outcome,” because if the result is wrong, the user can reread and ask again.
- By contrast, “a multi-step legal redline against three years of firm precedent” involves “dozens of steps across many tools,” must survive partner review, and may ultimately be contested in court.
- **The system test**
- Ask whether you are building the “system” by which the customer executes the work, or merely building a “tool” that sits on top of an existing system.
- A system owns “data capture, the governance, the records of what got done” end-to-end.
- A tool merely adds intelligence to an existing workflow.
- He adds the caveat: “High ACV is usually a signal of a system, since systems replace real headcount and get paid accordingly, but it isn’t a guarantee.”
- The decisive question, he says, is “if the customer would still need your tool if a lab shipped something that supposedly directly competes with you. If yes, you’re building a system. If no, you’re a tool.”
- **The hedge fund / P&L test**
- The labs are judged by benchmarks, while the Rest of Oz is judged by the customer’s “P&L.”
- Customers are not looking at “SWE-Bench or MMLU,” but whether the company “closed the deal, redlined the contract correctly, or bound the right policy.”
- Therefore, the best agent companies must be “winning on alpha measured in customer P&L, not in benchmark scores.”
- **Conclusion**
- The more a workflow involves not just many steps, but multiple tools, strict output requirements, approval accountability, and record retention, the more distance it creates from the labs’ horizontal products.
- If customers see your product as the place where work happens, it is a system; if they see it as a helper feature, it is a tool — and defensibility is concentrated in the former.
- As long as you sell on benchmark performance, your comparison set collapses into the same category as a Claude or Codex seat. Selling on customer P&L outcomes is the article’s condition for getting “off the road.”
