## Executive Summary

Gergely Orosz of The Pragmatic Engineer interviewed nearly 20 engineering leaders — CTOs, VPEs, and heads of engineering — who are on a career break or seriously considering one, publishing his findings on August 18, 2026. Six out of ten leaders he spoke with said they are on their way out, and the reasons cluster around three AI- and "founder mode"-driven pressures: the job itself getting worse, equity turning worthless, and the career risk of falling behind on AI-native skills. Orosz, with roughly two decades in the industry, writes that he has "not seen as many capable engineering leaders opting out or taking prolonged breaks as now."

## Reason 1: The job got worse — founder mode and "AI psychosis"

Orosz identifies unrealistic AI expectations from founders and CEOs as the leading cause. Concretely: CTOs are expected to "magically transform the company to be 'AI-native,'" ordered to cut engineering costs by 20–50%, and pushed to "do more with less" — shipping more with no backfills.

One CTO described what he called "AI psychosis" among founders and peers: "Managing 'AI psychosis' with founders and executive peers has become very difficult. For example, what do you do when a founder ships a 60,000-line pull request into the product, gleaming with joy at how much more productive they've become with AI? They won't see all the issues with that PR, and how do you bring up that they've created a massive amount of tech debt? Especially without looking like a 'Debbie Downer.'" Orosz calls this pattern "founder slop" — it muddies accountability for who is on call when founder-shipped code breaks. By contrast, Ramp, Stripe, and Notion are named as companies that "understand how to integrate AI into the engineering culture with a growth mindset without forsaking quality."

## Reason 2: Equity turns worthless — the preference-stack math

One CTO explained why a generous-looking grant meant nothing: "My company would have needed a massive exit for me to realize any upside. I had an equity grant that was 2% of the common shares. However, this equity was behind an already steep preference stack for investors, post Series A." Orosz walks through the arithmetic: a $10M seed round at a $50M valuation, followed by a $100M Series A at a $500M valuation — $110M raised in total. With a standard 1x preference, investors would take the first $110M off any sale. But this company's Series A investors negotiated a 2x preference, so $210M ($10M to the seed, $200M to the Series A) goes to investors before any common shareholder — including the CTO — sees a cent, meaning the company needs to sell for over $210M just to break even for him. Orosz further notes that a startup earning $10M a year and growing 30% year-over-year, after raising $110M, is often realistically worth only $30–50M (3–5x revenue) once growth slows — far short of what would be needed.

## Reason 3: Falling behind on "AI-native" skills is a career risk

A former engineering director at a large bank quit to accelerate his career, telling Orosz: "I was not getting the opportunity to 'close the loop' on hypotheses enough. [...] To stay relevant in the industry, I feel like I need to pull out into the 'fast lane.'" Charity Majors, co-founder and CTO of Honeycomb, put it bluntly on The Pragmatic Engineer podcast: "You've got to get AI on your resume. You just have to. If you don't, this is a huge career risk. If you're working somewhere where you're not getting these skills, I would do whatever I could to change that [including taking an IC role within the company]." Claire Vo — founder of ChatPRD, host of the "How I AI" podcast, and former Chief Product & Technology Officer at LaunchDarkly — argues most companies will never truly go AI-native because most VPEs lack the change-management skill for it: "The VPE role used to be primarily about deploying the dark arts to defend engineers from the roadmap, and now everyone thinks that's BS and leaders are under tremendous pressure to inflect velocity or GTFO... and everyone good has left for a lab."

## Reason 4: Smaller teams need fewer leaders

Karthik Hariharan, an engineering leader at DoorDash, observes: "A lot of folks qualified for [these roles] have consciously been stepping back into IC roles or joining bigger companies for stability and better compensation. Engineering teams are also smaller now. A VPE isn't needed until the team is large enough to require it." Anthropic's Head of Claude Platform, Katelyn Lesse, described why: "On an individual project, you often cannot have more than two people working on it. This is because each engineer is already running several agents. And so as an engineer, you're already fighting against your agents, which are stepping on each other's toes on implementation." Frontend-only and native-mobile-only teams are shrinking too — Orosz cites Bluesky, whose web, iOS, and Android apps were originally built at launch by a single engineer using React Native and Expo. Orosz adds that the broader trend of flattening org structures and cutting middle-management layers, which he traces back to Meta's cuts in 2023, has continued for three years running.
