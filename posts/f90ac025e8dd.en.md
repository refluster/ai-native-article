## Take the “Data Ingestion Layer,” Not Gross Margin, in Enterprise AI — Services-Led Growth Centered on the Forward Deployed Engineer

## Executive Summary

This a16z essay argues that for AI startups, services that include implementation and deployment support are not an “inefficiency” of the software business, but a strategic wedge for embedding deeply in the customer’s operating context, getting AI into production, and ultimately becoming a high-gross-margin product company. The key is not aiming for “~80% gross margin” from year one, but determining where data enters in the workflows AI is replacing, and which workflows your company will control.

## Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Workday as a Rebuttal to the Conventional Wisdom Favoring PLG

The essay positions itself against the widely shared assumption of the past decade that “product-led growth (PLG) is superior to implementation-heavy enterprise software,” arguing that this framework does not cleanly apply to enterprise AI adoption. There are successful PLG products that are easy to use on a self-serve basis, can be adopted without sales, and can be purchased by credit card—but when it comes to replacing complex business processes, there is another path to victory.

- **Evidence**
- As examples of successful PLG companies, it cites “Atlassian, Slack, Figma, Notion, and Dropbox (and more recently, ChatGPT and Cursor).”
- By contrast, it argues that during major platform transitions, “Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Workday” built dominant positions with models that required “significant implementation, services, and support.”
- On market capitalization, it states: “$254 billion for Salesforce, $194 billion for ServiceNow, and $63 billion for Workday.” It goes on to say, “Their combined value dwarfs that of the top PLG companies, and it’s not even close.”
- On AI companies, it notes that in complex products spanning “sales, support, and legal,” some have gone “from $0 to $5 million, $10 million, or beyond $20 million in ARR in their first two years.”
- **Conclusion**
- The essay’s premise is that so long as AI is being deployed into existing “core workflows,” lightweight, PLG-style adoption alone will struggle to drive replacement.
- What should be valued is not “low implementation friction,” but the implementation capability required to replace and entrench complex workflows—and on that basis, services-led growth is rational.
## AI Apps Carry a Heavier Implementation Burden Than Traditional SaaS, and Lower Gross Margin Should Be Treated as an Early Cost

The central argument of the article is that enterprise AI products require even more implementation work than traditional enterprise SaaS because they depend on deep integration and business context. For AI to deliver value, it must be securely connected to internal DBs, APIs, workflows, historical data, and business logic.

- **Evidence**
- It states explicitly: “Enterprise AI products have an even more pronounced implementation requirement, as they require deep integrations and context.”
- It describes the role of the services organization as “securely connecting the AI application to internal databases, APIs, and workflows, ensuring models have the context they need — historical records, business logic, and more — to deliver value.”
- As examples of what makes incumbent platforms valuable, it offers the following:
- “Salesforce is only valuable when configured with the right fields for tracking sales data”
- “ServiceNow only works when ITSM workflows are properly integrated and orchestrated”
- “Workday requires extensive HR data migration and configuration”
- On early gross margin levels, it notes: “At IPO, ServiceNow’s gross margin was 63.2%, and Workday’s was just 54.1% — far below the ideal ~80% for software.”
- On Salesforce, it says the company “reportedly burned over $52 million to generate $22 million in revenue before developing its partner ecosystem.”
- As examples of later improvement, it adds: “in 2024, ServiceNow had 79% gross margins; Workday was at 75%.”
- **Conclusion**
- In this reading, low early gross margins and high burn are not signs that the product is a commodity, but reflections of the implementation burden required to penetrate deeply into real business operations.
- Accordingly, investing in services is framed not as an “un-software-like” deviation, but as an upfront investment that later creates pricing power and ecosystem formation.
- In the essay’s logic, the KPI AI companies should optimize early is not gross margin percentage itself, but “growing total gross profit as fast as possible.”
## The Shift to “Software is the worker” Changes Implementation Support from Manual Help to Operating Design

The essay argues that in the age of AI agents, software is no longer merely assisting humans; it is becoming something that performs human work end to end. That shift means the challenge is no longer simply UI adoption, but redesigning roles and workflows themselves—and that is where the services organization comes in.

- **Evidence**
- It states: “Software is no longer aiding the worker — software is the worker.”
- It continues: “Software doesn’t need the same graphical user interface on top of a database to operate; it can autonomously complete tasks end-to-end.”
- But for complex work, “companies will need expert services to redesign job functions and processes around this new approach.”
- It describes the people who do this work as a “professional services employee, sometimes rebranded as a forward deployed engineer or an implementation/solutions specialist.”
- As a concrete example, it points to Decagon, writing: “The company has a sizable team of human ‘Agent Product Managers’ who work closely with customers to stand up AI support agents.”
- Those staff are described as “relatively technical” and able to “implement, integrate, and problem-solve so Decagon’s agents can resolve a wide range of customer support requests autonomously.”
- **Conclusion**
- The argument is that differentiation in AI applications will shift away from foundation model performance alone and toward how effectively those models are translated into a customer’s real workflows.
- The forward deployed engineer is therefore defined not as a supporting implementation resource, but as a core function for turning AI into a “real-world solution.”
- The knowledge accumulated in that role is not merely input for one-off custom projects, but a mechanism for discovering repeatable productization opportunities.
## The Goal Is Not the “System of Record,” but First Owning the “System of Work”

The article acknowledges that incumbent platforms are powerful because they own data as the company’s “source of truth,” but argues that AI companies should first aim to become the “system of work” where day-to-day activity actually happens. In this framing, value accrues to the company that controls where data is generated, ingested, and updated.

- **Evidence**
- On the value of incumbent systems, it says: “Incumbent systems of record are valuable because they own workflows that capture valuable data, which helps them become their company’s source of truth.”
- It sets the goal for AI companies as becoming the “system(s) of work that generate, capture, and store valuable company data.”
- It then says, “As they race to own the data ingestion layer, it’s shortsighted to be optimizing for 80% gross margin.”
- On what companies should optimize for, it states: “The only thing businesses should be optimizing for today is growing total gross profit as fast as possible.”
- It also notes that as the importance of the application layer rises, even model companies are moving the same way: “even model providers like OpenAI and Anthropic have been hiring forward deployed engineers and solutions engineers to win at the enterprise.”
- On OpenAI specifically, it says: “22 of the 311 open roles on OpenAI’s career page fall into these categories.”
- **Conclusion**
- In the article’s framework, durable advantage lies less in the model itself than in control over the data ingestion layer and operational workflows.
- That is why services-led implementation is not a revenue supplement, but a strategic step toward owning the “system of work” and, over time, moving closer to the “system of record.”
- The fact that OpenAI and Anthropic are also expanding these roles is presented as evidence that this implementation-led model is not unique to application companies, but a broader requirement of enterprise adoption.
## Design Principles for an Early Services Organization — Customer Selection, Selling at Cost, API Design, and Frontline Feedback

The essay lays out a fairly concrete set of operating principles for teams building a services organization for the first time. The core idea is to narrow the target customer and implementation scope, align sales and incentives, and accumulate implementation assets with future externalization and automation in mind.

- **Evidence**
- On customer selection, it advises: “Pick the right customers and start small in scope.” It also says: “Identify an ICP with at least some commonalities across systems and use cases to maximize learning.”
- On incentives, it says: “Forward deployed teams need to be closely aligned with account executives.”
It also recommends that companies “design incentive structures that allow services to be sold at cost. As ACVs grow, all that matters is success (and retention).”

- On technical design, it advises: “Build common software libraries and design APIs in a way that is easy to integrate with and understand.”
- On documentation, it says: “Outsourcing to ecosystem providers requires clear and consistent documentation.” It also advises teams to “document everything.”
- On automation, it notes: “Best-in-class companies are automating as much of the process as possible, including tasks like process mining, data pipeline automation, system integrations, and combing through API documentation.”
- On talent requirements, it says: “This kind of implementation work doesn’t require a PhD, but it does require hustle.”
It further describes the ideal people as having “high agency and insatiably curious” and “a healthy disregard for the status quo.”

- On product collaboration, it states: “Forward deployed teams — technical or not — are closest to the customer. It’s critical to have the right feedback loops and clean information sharing between the front lines and product.”
- On in-person engagement, it adds: “Being physically present can help as you sort through internal power dynamics and drive adoption of new tools.”
- **Conclusion**
- The essay’s position is that a services organization should be designed not merely as a delivery function, but as a learning infrastructure connecting sales, product, and a future partner ecosystem.
- The phrase “services to be sold at cost” makes explicit that the priority is not short-term services gross margin, but ACV expansion and retention.
- It also suggests that because AI can improve the efficiency of implementation work itself, companies may be able to scale complex deployment models faster than the Salesforce era did, and at lower minimum ACVs.
