## Executive Summary

A joint team of security experts from the Brookings Institution and Tsinghua University has proposed safeguards for military AI modeled on the rules that govern nuclear weapons — including a ban on letting AI autonomously decide to launch a nuclear strike and a new US-China military hotline for AI-related incidents. The proposal was published a week before US President Donald Trump meets Chinese President Xi Jinping, and neither government has yet endorsed it.

## The Core Proposal: Human Control on a Nuclear Scale

The recommendations come out of the US-China AI and National Security Dialogue, a track-two dialogue running since 2019. The authors are Melanie Sisson, a senior fellow at Brookings, and Tianjiao Jiang, an associate professor at Fudan University. According to Reuters, the proposals were published last week, and neither government has formally endorsed them.

The proposal rests on three pillars. First, AI should not be allowed to autonomously decide whether to use nuclear weapons. Second, AI should not be used to attack the other side's nuclear command-and-control systems. Third, humans should retain control over any consequential cyberattack against a rival's nuclear infrastructure.

The experts also call for a dedicated US-China military hotline to handle AI-related incidents, and for the two countries to agree on a shared definition of "meaningful human control" — a phrase widely used in arms-control debates that currently has no common definition.

## Building on the 2024 Biden-Xi Understanding

The proposal builds on an agreement reached in November 2024, when then-President Joe Biden and President Xi Jinping affirmed that humans, not AI, should retain control over decisions to use nuclear weapons. This new proposal attempts to translate that principle into concrete operating rules for military AI more broadly.

## Skepticism About the Hotline's Effectiveness

The hotline proposal, the plan's centerpiece, faces doubts about whether it would actually work. Carla Freeman of Johns Hopkins University told Reuters that no one in the Chinese system wants to talk to the US until top leadership has settled on a response — a process that "may take days if not longer" — meaning the hotline would only help if both sides actually picked up.

On the Chinese side, Shen Jian, China's ambassador for disarmament affairs, has said that military AI could raise the risk of miscalculation and escalation, and that China handles AI through its Foreign Ministry's arms-control bureaucracy. Reuters reported that Washington has no equivalent central coordinator — an asymmetry that complicates the bilateral institutional design the proposal envisions.

## Timing Against a Backdrop of Diplomacy and a Slowdown Debate

The timing of the publication appears deliberate. Trump is due to meet Xi in Washington on September 24, and the two governments are planning a fresh round of official AI talks, where the US intends to raise the issue of AI-directed cyberattacks.

The same week, US lab chiefs led by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for a slowdown in frontier AI development, a call China's commerce ministry dismissed as a bid for technological hegemony. The Brookings-Tsinghua proposal steers clear of that broader dispute over development speed, aiming instead at a narrower goal: keeping the most consequential military decisions in human hands.
