## Executive Summary

Moses Sternstein's "Charts of the Week: Bookslop," published by a16z on August 7, 2026, lines up four separate datasets that all trace how cheaper supply — or a structural surge in demand — rewrites a market. At its center is a paper by researchers from Stony Brook, Columbia, Michigan and the MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy, "Generative AI floods and dilutes the market for books," which reports that books carrying detectable AI text now capture roughly 40% of observed sales. The same issue places that alongside a jump in the defense industrial backlog, a collapse in IT services' forward multiples to about a third of their early-2025 level, and the conversion of hype into real usage for Moonshot's Kimi K3. Markets that got cheaper to supply and markets whose demand structurally expanded sit on the same page.

## "Bookslop" — AI-written books take about 40% of observed sales

The paper's claim is straightforward: AI-generated books — substantially or somewhat — are proliferating in the self-published categories, and they are selling.

- **Books with at least some detectable AI text are now capturing ~40% of observed sales.**
- **Books with substantial AI text hold at least a 10% share of the Top 25 across all genres**, reaching about 40% in the Sci-Fi/Adventure/Dystopian category.
Because the barriers to entry are low, the entries are high — and so AI-generated or AI-assisted books can top the charts in basically any genre. Sternstein compresses this into a phrase: "Flood the zone, win the zone."

These figures rest on a premise: that AI text detection actually works. Sternstein flags it himself — "Assuming that AI text detection actually works" — and in the comments Thad McIlroy attacks the paper's "mathiness," arguing the damage is "illusory (or, at least, a fraction of what is being claimed)." That the premise is contested belongs in the record with the same weight as the numbers.

## A 40x catalogue against 9x revenue — the arithmetic of dilution, and the counter-reading

The "dilutes" in the paper's title lives in the gap between catalogue growth and revenue growth.

- **Since 2023, self-published new books have grown the total catalogue nearly 40x.**
- **Actual revenues have only increased ~9x.**
- Non-AI books still earn the most per book, but the gap has closed substantially.
AI authorship, in other words, is taking a volume approach: lower-cost output scaled the self-published catalogue massively, but the hit rate is lower, so revenue grew far less than proportionally. The paper's conclusion is that revenue per book is falling across the board and human writers are being squeezed.

Sternstein offers a second reading of the same chart. By the authors' own calculations, self-published revenues still rose nearly 9x in three years — a large increase, even off a small base. If bookslop is flooding the market while also growing that market much faster than it would otherwise have grown, then a rising share of revenue moving to AI books is not self-evidently a bad outcome. One body of evidence — the divergence between catalogue and revenue — supports more than one conclusion.

## The defense industrial backlog — a demand inflection alongside cloud

Against the cloud revenue backlog widely read as an indicator of AI-driven demand, the piece sets the defense contractor backlog.

- Per Census data, **defense orders and shipments have both increased ~60% since 2020**, with the lion's share of that increase in the past 1.5 years.
- **"Unfilled defense orders" have jumped by ~$20B, even as the estimated backlog has dropped by ~20%.** The piece is explicit that this measure is an imperfect proxy: it is just orders divided by monthly shipments, and it does not distinguish a mortar round from a jet engine.
- **$5B of investment has substantially increased munitions capacity since 2022**, though target capacity remains higher still.
- **The manufacturing PMI has posted seven straight months of expansion**, with every indicator rising except prices paid.
The aggregate picture is that shipment throughput has accelerated even faster than demand. The drivers named are the ongoing conflicts with Iran and Russia, European countries allocating more to defense budgets, and a shift toward autonomy in key battlefield components that has created urgency to evolve the industrial base itself.

## IT services, repriced — not elimination but forced evolution

Once considered a likely beneficiary of AI implementation work, IT services is now read as a likely loser of IT budgets shifting away from services and toward AI.

- **Consensus multiples for IT services are now about a third of what they were at the beginning of 2025.** Accenture is named as the example.
The argument is that this is a case of AI forcing a function to evolve rather than eliminating it. The evidence is the cloud precedent: **it took 3–4 years from the collapse of cloud-driven growth expectations for IT services to recover.** On-prem services were disrupted by the shift off-prem, and the pain for consultants was real — until they adapted their skills and offerings and became the experts in lift-and-shift. Those who did not adapt continued to languish. The change was net-good, but it was not painless.

## Kimi K3 — hype converting into usage, and an ad budget moving to LinkedIn

Two weeks after Moonshot released Kimi K3, the piece uses SensorTower data to test whether attention converted into use.

- **App downloads nearly quintupled and daily active users jumped by ~40%.**
- Most of the impact landed outside the US. **Kimi's global revenue reach is already 6–7 times higher than in the US**; gross in-app purchase revenue went vertical in July and roughly doubled in a single week.
- **More than 60% of Moonshot's 2026 advertising spend is directed at LinkedIn**, with YouTube — where it spent big in 2025 — now an afterthought.
The piece declines to read the LinkedIn shift as proof of an enterprise play: it "doesn't necessarily mean that Moonshot is making an enterprise play, but it might mean that." It also notes that LinkedIn was already the favored advertising channel for AI apps generally, which limits how much of a Moonshot-specific signal the data can carry.

Running through all four sections is one structure: markets where AI pushed costs down directly (publishing, IT services) and a market where demand rose structurally for reasons other than AI (defense) are being reorganized at the same time. In the first, a flood of supply erodes unit economics; in the second, shipping capacity itself is the constraint.
