In business software, "an assistant coordinates the agents." In code review, "most of the reading moves to machines." In the model market, an open-weight model arrives claiming parity with an incumbent. These look unrelated, but one principle explains all three: the more interchangeable the party that does the work, the more the criterion for deciding which work goes to which executor, and through which check, becomes the unit of competition and of responsibility.

Here are the three facts. In its "AI Release Highlights Q3 2026" dated 26 October 2026, SAP said 45,000 cloud customers use its AI solutions (the L2 on SAP's Q3 release, "an assistant that binds agents together"). The Claude Code team, in an environment where Claude writes about 90% of the code, did not abolish review; it moved most of the reading to machines (the L2 on the Claude Code team's code-review design). Reflection AI released Beam, its first open-weight model, and claims performance on par with China's Z.ai GLM-5.2 (the L2 on Reflection AI's Beam).

## Business software: the unit of competition moves from "features" to "how things are bound together"

According to the L2 on SAP's Q3 release, the centre of the quarter is a structure in which a Joule assistant for each business domain oversees many specialist agents. SAP positions this as a step toward the "Autonomous Enterprise."

What matters here is not how clever any single specialist agent is. The customer touches the domain assistant, and the assistant decides which specialist to hand a request to. The specialists are treated as parts that can be added or swapped. What fixes the product's identity for the user is the layer that dispatches the parts, not the parts themselves.

The same article asks for caution, though. Every effect figure is either SAP's own "expected" value or a customer's self-report. The 45,000-customer footprint is not evidence that the binding is done well. Describing the structure (value is moving to the coordinating layer) and demonstrating that the layer produces results are separate claims.

## Code review: the question shifts from "who read it?" to "what can break?"

The L2 on the Claude Code team's review design gives the most concrete numbers on what happens when executors become replaceable. If Claude writes about 90% of the code, the author is already not a person. The team still kept review. It moved most of the reading to machines and kept human time and attention for the most important judgments.

The numbers: PRs merged from June to September more than doubled, and the share reverted fell by more than 40%. Throughput rose and rework fell.

What supported this was a change in the starting question. As that article frames it, the starting point was not "did a person read it?" but "what can break, and which check is appropriate?" The former asks about the executor, the party that read. The latter asks for a routing criterion that picks a check according to the nature of the change. Only the latter holds up whether the reader is a person or a machine. Because the team fixed a criterion that did not depend on the executor, it could hand the reading to machines without losing quality.

## Open models: the more suppliers there are, the more the selection criterion becomes the subject

The L2 on Reflection AI's Beam concerns the executor side: the model itself. Reflection AI, a US start-up backed by Nvidia and the US administration, announced its first open-weight model. It claims performance on par with GLM-5.2 but has not yet published technical details. The open-model market is held by Chinese players, and whether the US can field a credible domestic option is being tested on both industrial policy and enterprise cost structure.

An open-weight model can be placed in a company's own environment and swapped out. Which model to use stops being a contract with one supplier and becomes a choice made inside the company. The article names industrial policy and cost structure, not only performance, as the grounds for that choice. If the parity claim holds, models become parts that performance cannot separate, and what decides the choice is something other than performance. Here too, the unit of competition moves from the executor (the model) to the criterion for choosing it.

The article also shows a weakness. Parity is the company's claim and the technical detail is unpublished. A criterion is useless if there is no way to check the candidates against it.

## The common principle: Why → So What

Set side by side, the three facts share one structure.

First, executors are multiplying and becoming swappable. At SAP, specialist agents are bound together as parts; on the Claude Code team, the party writing about 90% of the code is no longer a person; among open models, more suppliers claim comparable performance.

Second, once the quality of the executor stops being the source of differentiation, the remaining question is where to send the work. At SAP the assistant plays that role. For the Claude Code team the question "what can break, and which check is appropriate?" does. In model selection, industrial policy and cost structure join performance in that role.

Third, the routing criterion is written in a form that does not depend on who the executor is. These facts suggest that the place where value sits is moving from "the party that does the work" to "the criterion that routes the work."

We state the principle so it can be refuted. If differences in executor performance remain decisive, that is, if the quality of the specialist agents or models being bound decides most of the outcome, the routing layer is only a thin relay with no added value. Conversely, if outcomes diverge widely with the same pool of executors purely because routing criteria differ, the principle is supported. The Claude Code team's case is one instance of the latter, but it is one team's result, and generalising it requires reproduction in other organisations.

## Implications and predictions

Three changes follow from this principle.

First, competition over the "binding layer" is likely to intensify. In business software, the difference between products will probably come less from the number or breadth of specialist agents and more from how a request is decomposed and where it is sent. The ground is that SAP's structure itself concentrates the customer touchpoint in the binding layer. But the effect so far rests on SAP's "expected" values and customer self-reports, so confidence is moderate.

Second, organisations that write down how they choose checks may raise throughput while holding quality. The ground is the Claude Code team's numbers (merged PRs more than doubled, reverts down more than 40%), with one condition: the criterion must be written in a form that does not depend on the executor. An organisation whose comfort rests on the fact that a person read the change will not receive this benefit once the executor becomes a machine.

Third, model selection will move closer to procurement and policy. When several candidates claim comparable performance, the choice is decided by criteria other than performance. For Beam, though, the technical detail remains unpublished and the parity claim cannot be checked. Confidence is low, and withholding judgment until the details appear is the reasonable course.

The practical takeaway for the reader is a single exercise: write down whether the criterion for handing work to someone in your organisation is stated in terms of "who did it" or in terms of "what can break" and "which check is needed." If it is the former, the criterion stops working the moment the executor is replaced.
