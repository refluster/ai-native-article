## Executive Summary

On July 9, 2026, Google announced that it will add a “How this ad was made” panel within My Ad Center for ads served on Search, YouTube, and Discover, allowing users to check whether an ad was created with AI. At the core is a two-tier mechanism: ads made with Google’s own generative AI tools receive disclosure labels automatically, while ads made with other tools require the advertiser to disclose that manually. The key point of the announcement is that, rather than leaving disclosure to voluntary good faith, Google has implemented it as a “standard product feature” by combining automatic detection on the tool side, advertiser disclosure obligations, and label display based on regional regulation.

## What the announcement says—who is doing what, and where it appears

The facts are straightforward, with little room for speculation.

- The announcement date is **July 9, 2026**, and the speaker is **Keerat Sharma**, VP & General Manager of Ads Privacy and Safety.
- What is being added is a section called “**How this ad was made**” within **My Ad Center**. It can be accessed from the three-dot menu or the info icon on an ad.
- It applies to ads on **Search, YouTube, and Discover**, and the rollout is global.
- In this panel, users can see, in Google’s words, whether “an ad was created or edited with AI.”
In other words, the primary location for disclosure is outside the ad itself—a panel that users actively open—with the information surfacing onto the ad’s face only where regulatory requirements call for it.

## Three layers of disclosure—automatic, manual, and conditional display

What Google has designed is not a single label, but three disclosure paths with different origins. The layers are divided by who bears responsibility for disclosure.

- **Automatic disclosure (automatic)**: If an advertiser creates an ad using Google’s generative AI tools, Google automatically adds the disclosure. Responsibility lies with the platform.
- **Manual disclosure (manual)**: If an advertiser uses generative AI through tools other than Google’s, the advertiser can declare that themselves. Responsibility lies with the advertiser.
- **Conditional on-ad display (on-ad labels)**: Depending on local requirements, labels may appear directly on the face of the ad. These are shown either automatically or through advertiser controls.
What this suggests as evidence is that Google has adopted a complementary structure for the single disclosure field of “whether AI was used”: where detection is possible, it is handled automatically; where it is not, the gap is filled by advertiser declaration. Automatic disclosure alone would miss ads made with third-party tools, while declaration alone would rely on goodwill. Running both in parallel narrows the gaps in disclosure.

## How this connects to SynthID and existing policy

This new panel is not a standalone feature, but one grafted onto existing provenance technology and ad policy.

- Google embeds “imperceptible signals,” such as **SynthID**, into outputs from its own generative AI tools. Automatic disclosure is built on those embedded signals.
- In policy lineage, there is the election-ad requirement introduced in **2023**. Election ads containing synthetic or altered content were already subject to mandatory disclosure. This new measure is positioned as an expansion of that election-ad-only disclosure regime to ads more broadly.
- Existing ad policies prohibiting misleading and deceptive ads remain in force regardless of how the content was created. Advertiser identity verification processes also remain in place.
## What it means to “design” disclosure

The conclusion to draw here is that AI disclosure is shifting from a simple declaration of “show it or don’t” into a systems problem that combines detection, declaration, and display.

Google has adopted a staged design in which the default location for disclosure is a panel that opens when users ask for it, with exposure on the ad surface itself limited to regions where regulation requires it. That is a choice balancing transparency against noise in the ad experience, and it reduces both operational cost and visual burden compared with “putting a label on every ad at all times.” At the same time, because automatic disclosure is limited to ads originating from Google’s own tools, the accuracy of disclosures for ads made with third-party tools depends on the sincerity of advertisers’ manual declarations. Signals like SynthID can only be embedded in a company’s own outputs, so the completeness of disclosure is ultimately capped by the platform’s ability to detect what falls within its reach.

For users, the practical meaning is simple. If they open “How this ad was made” from an ad’s three-dot menu, they will be able to check whether that ad was created or edited with AI—at least to the extent Google knows.
