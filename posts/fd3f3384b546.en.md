The more a capability becomes surplus, the more value shifts away from the capability itself and toward its scarce complement: the adjacent layer. a16z says that “for the first time in history, humans are cheaper than software,” Decagon’s data shows that spending does not fall even when tokens are abundant, and Apple sued OpenAI over trade secrets involving metal finishing. Unit labor costs, inference costs, model performance—at first glance, all three look like stories about things becoming “cheaper and more abundant.” In fact, they can be explained by the same principle. As a resource becomes abundant, value escapes from it and is drawn to the complementary good beside it that remains scarce.

## What “Too-Cheap Labor” Exposed Was Not Scarce Capability, but Scarce Instruction

“‘You just hired a million bad employees’—reading a16z’s essay on AI workforces as a management problem” flips the usual premise that AI replaces labor. The core of the a16z.news essay “You just hired a million bad employees.” lies in one sentence: “for the first time in history, humans are cheaper than software.” Agentic labor itself is no longer scarce or expensive. So where does the bottleneck move?

The essay’s answer is precise: wasted tokens are not fundamentally a capability problem, but an instruction-quality problem. Agent swarms and human organizations “fail in the same ways”—in other words, what is lacking is not intelligence, but the mechanism for measuring and fixing dysfunction. That is why the prescription is Eval (evaluation suites). Next to capability, now cheaply available, the only thing that remains scarce is how to evaluate and how to instruct. When the essay calls “AI transformation” the next great business opportunity, that is simply the reverse side of value sliding down from the model itself to the layer of evaluation and management around it.

## Tokens Are Plentiful, but Spending Concentrates in “Discovery”: A Two-Tier Structure

“Why the rise of open-source AI still hasn’t dented Anthropic—an economy splitting into ‘discovery’ and ‘production’” captures the same shift in economic data. The contradiction highlighted by Decagon CEO Jesse Zhang is straightforward. Even as mature deployments move toward lightweight, open-source models, total spending on frontier models has barely fallen.

Real-world data from Vercel and OpenRouter supports this two-tier structure. By token volume processed, open-source models such as DeepSeek are in the lead. But by dollars spent, Anthropic still accounts for more than half. Volume has moved to the cheap layer, but the money has not. In that essay’s reading, a division of labor has taken hold: frontier models handle “discovery,” while open-source models handle “production.”

### Money Escapes from Production Once It Becomes a Commodity

What is happening here is, precisely, the surplus-ification of capability. “Production” inference—routine, large-scale processing—has become commoditized and sunk into a lower-priced layer. The fact that open source leads in processed tokens is proof that capability has become abundant. And yet spending remains with the frontier because what is scarce is not token volume, but “discovery”—judgment in difficult terrain. Money escapes from cheapened production, while scarce judgment beside it continues to command a price.

## Once Models Converge, the Fight Moves to Metal Finishing

“Apple sues OpenAI—the trade-secret case born of hardware competition” may look, at first glance, like a pure intellectual-property story, unrelated to abundance in AI. But the structure is the same. On 2026-07-10, Apple sued OpenAI in the United States District Court for the Northern District of California. The dispute is not about which model is better. Apple claims that through the hiring of former Apple executives, OpenAI systematically acquired trade secrets including technical information on unreleased products and Apple’s proprietary metal-finishing techniques.

What matters here is that the battleground has shifted away from software and model performance and toward physical, scarce assets: hardware, talent, and manufacturing know-how. As OpenAI pushes ahead with development of its first hardware product, the movement of personnel has turned into an inter-company clash over intellectual property. Once models line up side by side, what decides the contest is outside the model—hard-to-copy metal finishing, and the people who know it. The very thing companies are defending in the most friction-heavy form possible, litigation, reveals what still remains scarce in this economy.

## A Shared Principle: The Scarce Complement to an Abundant Resource Sets the Price

Put these three facts side by side and their surface themes seem unrelated: labor management, a two-tier inference economy, and a hardware trade-secret lawsuit. But the direction of movement is the same in all three. In the a16z essay, next to cheapened labor, the evaluation layer—Eval and instruction—became scarce. In Decagon’s analysis, next to low-cost production at scale, spending concentrated in the frontier layer responsible for “discovery.” In Apple v. OpenAI, next to converging models, metal finishing and talent became the objects of competition.

Taken together, these facts suggest the following principle: **the more a resource becomes commoditized and abundant, the more value and competition move away from that resource itself and toward the scarce complementary goods adjacent to it**. When capability—models, intelligence, tokens—becomes surplus, what gets priced is not the capability itself, but the adjacent layer required to make that capability useful, so long as supply in that layer has not increased. Instruction and evaluation, judgment in hard cases, physical manufacturing know-how and talent—all of these are complements to “abundant intelligence.” What is operating here is, in effect, the inverse image of the familiar strategy “commoditize your complement.” Value settles instead into the adjacent layer that one does not commoditize oneself.

This principle is falsifiable. If spending on frontier models actually collapses as open-source alternatives mature, then Decagon’s view that “the two-tier structure may become a stable feature” will be wrong, and value will not have escaped into the adjacent layer. If Eval and the management layer do not become a business opportunity, and rising capability alone resolves dysfunction, then a16z’s reading is mistaken. If models converge and yet competition remains centered on model performance rather than moving into hardware and talent, then Apple’s suit is merely an exceptional anecdote. This principle is a claim with edge: it would collapse if any one of those things happened.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, several shifts are likely to continue for the time being. First, it will become harder for model performance alone to determine winners, and the focus of value will continue shifting toward the layer of evaluation and orchestration. When the a16z essay called “AI transformation” the biggest business opportunity, it was anticipating exactly this shift. The work of implementing Evals that measure dysfunction company by company becomes relatively scarcer and more expensive the cheaper capability gets. The probability is high. Surplus capability is already observable in the data, and scarcity must, by definition, migrate to its complement.

Second, the two-tier structure Decagon describes—“discovery = frontier / production = open source”—is likely to remain stable, at least for now. The fact that processing volume flows to the cheap layer without spending flowing away shows that value is attached not to quantity, but to hard points of judgment; that tendency strengthens as capability becomes more uniform. As noted in the previous section, however, this forecast fails if frontier spending collapses—that is the branching point to watch.

Third, as Apple v. OpenAI suggests, the closer models get to parity, the more companies will enter high-friction conflicts over hard-to-copy physical layers: manufacturing know-how, talent, and trade secrets. Competition that no longer differentiates in software descends into messier assets.

The practical implication for readers is this: if the “capability” you work with is rapidly getting cheaper, it is a poor bet to keep piling more chips onto that layer. The real question is: what adjacent layer is indispensable for making that capability useful, and has not yet seen its supply increase? Is it the design of instruction and evaluation? Judgment in hard cases? Hard-to-copy physical assets? That single point—still scarce beside what has become abundant—is where the center of gravity for the next wave of value and competition will lie.
