## Introduction

Anthropic raised **$3.5 billion** in its Series H, reaching a **$61.5 billion post-money valuation**. At the same time, in improving Claude, the company has put forward an alignment approach that teaches not “what to do” but **“why to do it.”** Reuters Breakingviews, meanwhile, argued that Japanese companies, facing a **severe labor shortage**, are under pressure to raise productivity with AI. These facts may appear separate—fundraising, model design, and industrial adoption—but in fact they can be explained by the same underlying principle.

## Analysis 1: What massive capital is betting on is not “the model” but the ability to generate value broadly and reliably

The first point to confirm is the scale of market expectations signaled by Anthropic’s fundraising. According to the article, the company raised **$3.5 billion** in its Series H and reached a **$61.5 billion post-money valuation**. The company said it would use the funds for **“next-generation AI systems”** and business expansion. What matters here is that investors are not simply betting on improvements in benchmark performance alone.

That is because a valuation at the level of **$61.5 billion** is hard to justify with one-off demos or narrowly defined use cases. What is required is the ability to function consistently across diverse work environments and expand deployment while keeping the cost of failure under control. These facts suggest that what capital markets want from AI companies is not a **“high-performance model”** in itself, but the **ability to commercialize general-purpose, trustworthy intelligence that works even in unpredictable real-world settings**.

In addition, the phrase **“next-generation AI systems”** likely implies not just continued scaling, but simultaneous improvements in performance, safety, and practical operability. If adaptability in real-world environments is weak, then no matter how powerful the model is, monetization will stall in regulated industries and large enterprise deployments. The reason so much capital is flowing in is that a structural shift is underway: AI’s value is no longer being measured by **accuracy inside the lab**, but by **reproducible outcomes in the real world**.

## Analysis 2: Claude’s alignment improvements signal a shift from “rule-following” to “understanding principles”

Anthropic’s other article points to this structural shift even more directly. The key idea is that by teaching Claude not **“what to do” but “why to do it,”** the company is trying to improve OOD (out-of-distribution) generalization. This is not an approach based on listing surface-level rules, but on teaching the model the principles behind behavior.

This matters because in real operating environments, situations will inevitably arise that cannot be fully defined in advance. Goals such as safety, honesty, and respect for user intent can be codified on known tasks, but in unfamiliar contexts they often come into conflict. What is needed then is not mechanical obedience to individual instructions, but the **ability to derive concrete judgments from principles**. The very fact that Anthropic has made OOD generalization central suggests that it sees the bottleneck in model value not as a mere lack of capability, but as the **consistency of judgment in novel situations**.

The quotation worth noting here is precisely its emphasis on not **“surface-level rules”** but the **“principles behind desired behavior.”** While this is framed as a discussion of AI alignment, it also connects directly to the requirements of industrial deployment. In enterprise settings, AI that merely responds according to a manual quickly hits its limits. Internal documents, customer interactions, legal review, and procurement decisions are all full of exceptions, and depend less on fixed rules than on an understanding of goals and constraints. These facts suggest that the axis of competition in advanced models is shifting from **response accuracy** to **principle-based generalization performance**.

## Analysis 3: Japan’s labor shortage is forcing AI to function as adaptive proxy labor in the field

The situation facing Japanese companies, as pointed out by Reuters Breakingviews, also takes on a different meaning in this context. The issue is that Japan faces a **deepening labor shortage**, and companies are being forced to use AI to improve productivity. Demand for AI here is not adoption as a trend. It is urgent demand: because there are not enough people, companies need AI to **actually work** in order to sustain existing operations.

Japan’s productivity challenges have been discussed for years, but as the working-age population shrinks, a mere cost-cutting tool is not enough. What is needed is a system that can fill labor gaps not only in routine work, but also in semi-routine tasks and exception handling. This is where the alignment approach of teaching the “why” takes on industrial significance. If AI can operate only in a rule-based way, it will not be able to handle the complex ringi (bottom-up approval workflow), customer conventions, cross-department coordination, and ambiguous instructions common in Japanese companies. By contrast, if it can understand purposes and principles and make situational judgments, it can materially raise the productivity of lean organizations.

In other words, the macro pressure of labor shortages is asking companies not to “try AI,” but to **turn AI into an operational force**. These facts suggest that in the Japanese market, success or failure in AI adoption will be determined not by how flashy the model is, but by its **ability to generalize to workplace context and its reliability**. Anthropic’s R&D themes and the demand structure of Japanese companies are, in fact, pointed in the same direction.

## Presenting the common principle: From “Why” to “So What”

The most plausible underlying principle that explains all of these observations together is this: **the economic value of AI is determined not by performance on individual tasks, but by the extent to which it can generalize to unfamiliar real-world settings based on an understanding of principles, and operate stably not as a replacement for labor but as a complement to and extension of labor**.

Anthropic’s **$3.5 billion raise** and **$61.5 billion valuation** show that capital markets are pricing future cash flow into this generalization capability. The emphasis in Claude’s alignment improvements on **not “what to do” but “why to do it”** is precisely a technical prescription for improving that capability. And Japanese companies’ demand for AI-driven productivity is the real-world operating environment in which that capability will ultimately be tested. These facts suggest that AI value creation no longer ends with simply “making the model smarter,” but is shifting toward **acting on the basis of principles and making decisions aligned with goals even in unexpected situations**.

What matters here is that “Why” is not merely about improving explainability. To teach the “why” is closer to giving the model the generative principles behind norms of action. That, in turn, makes it possible in novel cases to do more than replay past answer patterns; it enables reasoning guided by an objective function. In alignment terms, that means improved safety. In business terms, it means a wider deployment range. In industrial terms, it becomes a response to labor shortages. In other words, **safety, profitability, and productivity are not separate issues, but different manifestations of a single capability: OOD generalization grounded in principle understanding**. That is the common principle running across all three articles.

## Forecasts and implications

The future predictions that follow from this principle are clear. First, over the next **2–3 years**, competitive advantage among AI companies is likely to shift away from temporary leadership on benchmarks and toward **lower failure rates in enterprise deployment, lower supervision costs, and better handling of exceptions**. The probability is **high (around 70%)**. The reason is that capital markets are already committing massive sums to “next-generation systems,” while OOD generalization has also become a major research theme.

Second, in Japan, labor shortages are likely to further increase the necessity of adoption, and AI is likely to shift from being a **“PoC target”** to being seen as **business continuity infrastructure**. The probability is **medium to high (around 65%)**. It will likely move first in back-office work, customer support, internal knowledge search, and document drafting and review. The key to diffusion, however, will be not performance but **governance and workplace adaptation**.

Third, AI alignment research is likely to be re-evaluated not as a peripheral topic around ethics and safety, but as a core technology that directly shapes ROI. The probability is **high (around 75%)**. The reason is that generalization grounded in principle understanding is what enables reductions in supervision workload, accident prevention, and expanded deployment scope at the same time.

There are three practical implications. First, when selecting models, companies should evaluate not only benchmark scores but also **consistency of judgment in unknown cases** and **ease of correction**. Second, in implementation design, rather than merely enumerating business rules, organizations need to clearly define **what they prioritize and why**, and connect that to the AI. Third, management should assess AI investment not only in terms of labor savings, but in terms of **how much decision density on the ground, including exception handling, it can absorb**.

In the end, what will decide the next era of AI dominance is not “how many answers it can produce.” It is **whether it understands why it should arrive at that answer, and whether it can turn that understanding into value in the field**. Anthropic’s fundraising, Claude’s alignment work, and the productivity pressures on Japanese companies already point to that reality.
