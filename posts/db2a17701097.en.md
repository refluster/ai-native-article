A quiet argument that senior engineers are being de-skilled. A two-year tracking study of 100 Indian tech companies. Shopify's earnings call. On the surface these belong to unrelated fields. But they resolve into a single principle: what determines whether an efficiency gain helps or hurts is not the size of the task being automated, but whether the new method preserves or erases the "act → check → correct" loop that used to surround it.

## What disappeared wasn't typing — it was the verification loop around reasoning

"It's Not Just the Entry-Level Chair That's Disappearing — AI Is Dismantling the Machine That Trained Seniors" reports on an argument from SQL performance specialist Pinal Dave. Beneath the familiar debate about AI replacing junior hires, Dave points to something quieter: existing senior engineers' own work is being reduced to "approving output," and they are de-skilling silently as a result.

Dave's core claim is a qualitative one. Earlier generations of developer tooling automated away *typing* — routine mechanical work — while leaving the judgment itself, the part seniors actually performed, intact. Generative AI automates away *reasoning* itself: design decisions, root-cause diagnosis, trade-off evaluation — work that seniors used to do by executing a decision, checking the result, and correcting the error. That "act and check" cycle was itself the classroom where seniority was built.

When AI performs the reasoning, the senior's role shrinks to approving an output — a act of ratifying a conclusion, not one of constructing and testing a judgment. What disappears here is not merely a quantity of work but the verification loop that used to compare a judgment against its outcome. When the loop disappears, the tacit learning that loop generated disappears with it.

## What separated "delegating" from "abandoning" was a deliberately designed verification loop

A contrasting fact comes from "Three Unglamorous Practices That Separated Delegation from Abandonment — What a Two-Year Study of 100 Indian Tech Firms Found in a 28-Point Gap." Researchers from Duke University, Stanford's Graduate School of Business, and Harvard Business School tracked founders at 100 Indian technology companies for roughly two years. Companies whose founders received formal advice on people-management practices employed 28% more people, were 10 percentage points less likely to fail, and had their survival rate rise from 75% to 85%.

What produced that gap was not sophisticated HR technology. It was three unglamorous practices: (1) set clear goals, (2) check progress on a regular cadence, and (3) give feedback that makes the next action clear. Restated, these three are exactly a loop: establish a standard, compare execution against that standard, and return the correcting information when they diverge. This is, in essence, the same "act and check" cycle Dave described in the previous section — deliberately re-engineered for the context of delegating authority.

What a founder loses when work is "abandoned" rather than "delegated" is not distributed labor — it is this verification loop. Without it, no one notices where a subordinate's judgment drifted, and neither correction nor learning occurs. Only the firms that explicitly engineered the loop realized a measurable, 28-point difference.

## Why AI search hasn't replaced Google traces back to the same loop

The third fact looks, on its face, unrelated to the first two. "AI Search Isn't Replacing Google — What Shopify's Q2 Earnings Revealed About 'Complementary' Traffic" reports that at its Q2 2026 earnings call, Shopify said AI-driven traffic and orders were up 3x year over year, while traditional search sessions also grew 1.3x over the past two years and still account for roughly a third of all storefront sessions. President Harley Finkelstein framed this as search being "complemented, not replaced."

The telling contrast is that the same AI search is *suppressing* click-through in online publishing. In media consumption, when AI provides a summary, the reader is satisfied and skips the "check" of clicking through to the original article. A click is inherently low-cost, and an error costs little, so skipping the verification step is tolerable.

Commerce is different. A purchase is a hard-to-reverse financial commitment. Even after AI surfaces a product or brand, users still verify that information via traditional search before buying — this is the structural reason traditional search sessions are growing at the same time AI search is expanding. Here the verification loop is not skipped; it persists, and AI-driven discovery and old-style verification grow together as two wheels of the same cart.

## The unifying principle: efficiency's outcome hinges on whether it preserves or erases the loop

The principle running through all three facts is this: whatever the new efficiency mechanism — AI adoption, delegated authority, smarter search — it inevitably short-circuits some part of the "act → check → correct" loop that previously surrounded the task. Whether that short-circuit erases the loop entirely, or preserves and re-engineers it in another form, determines whether the result of the efficiency gain is a genuine improvement or a quiet degradation.

In the senior-engineer case, AI took over the reasoning step, the verification loop vanished without anyone noticing, and the learning opportunity it generated quietly disappeared with it. In the Indian founders' case, the opposite happened: only the companies that deliberately re-engineered the loop — as goal-setting, progress checks, and feedback — achieved a measurable gain in organizational survival. In Shopify's case, because the object of verification carries financial risk, the loop persisted naturally even as AI-driven efficiency advanced, expanding alongside the old channel rather than displacing it.

These facts suggest that the real risk of any efficiency gain is not automation of the task itself, but the unnoticed disappearance of the verification loop that automation carries with it. When the loop is consciously designed, efficiency converts into genuine gain; when it is unconsciously omitted, efficiency converts into silent decay. This principle is falsifiable: it would break if cases were widely observed where explicitly preserving a verification loop produced no measurable difference in learning or survival — or if high-stakes domains were found where the loop's absence caused no long-run degradation at all.

## Forecast and implications

Three predictions follow from this principle.

First, organizations delegating tasks to AI will increasingly audit, deliberately, which verification loop used to surround each task before automating it (confidence: medium-to-high). The reason is that qualitative concerns like Dave's are now being paired with a hard ROI figure from the Indian tracking study — 28%, 10 points, 75% to 85%. Qualitative worry alone rarely moves management decisions, but a quantified comparison point tends to accelerate investment in both HR and engineering management processes.

Second, both junior and senior training may shift from tacit, error-driven learning toward deliberately designed review-and-check-in systems (confidence: medium). The remedy for the "disappearing classroom" Dave describes is essentially to import the Indian study's three practices — goal-setting, progress checks, feedback — into engineering organizations, and because that transfer faces low technical barriers, it is likely to be adopted readily.

Third, the split Shopify revealed — old channels persist where verification cost is high (low agreement), while AI displaces where verification cost is low (high agreement) — is likely to be observed extending beyond commerce into other high-stakes decision domains, such as selecting financial products or checking medical information (confidence: medium-to-high).

The practical takeaway for readers is straightforward. Before handing something to AI, ask: was this a task whose result someone used to check? If the answer is yes, don't hand the whole loop over to AI — deliberately build in a mechanism to preserve it, such as a regular review or a requirement to record the reasoning behind a decision. The cost of keeping the loop is almost always smaller than the cost of the learning and trust that quietly disappear when it vanishes unnoticed.
