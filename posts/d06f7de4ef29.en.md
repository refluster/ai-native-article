## Executive Summary

The 5-step process Elon Musk repeatedly hammers into teams at SpaceX and Tesla derives its value less from the steps themselves than from their order. Most people begin with optimization, acceleration, or automation, but Musk puts “question every requirement” and “delete” first. The heart of the piece, compiled by poster Jaynit (@jaynitx, source credit to @EricJorgenson) based on Walter Isaacson’s Musk biography, is distilled in Musk’s own words: “The most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist.”

## The 5-Step Sequence — and the Typical Failure of Running It Backward

The article presents the five steps in the following order.

- **One. Question every requirement.** (Question every requirement)
- **Two. Delete any part or process you can.** (Delete every part or process you can)
- **Three. Simplify and optimize.** (Simplify and optimize)
- **Four. Accelerate cycle time.** (Accelerate cycle time)
- **Five. Automate.** (Automate)
What makes this compelling is that the article explicitly argues that the order matters more than the steps. Most people start at step 5 with automation, or at step 4 with acceleration, or at step 3 with optimization. But if steps 1 and 2 have not come first, then you are “automating” or “optimizing” something that should not exist — that is the logic running through the entire piece. The article also quotes Musk saying: “It’s very common, possibly the most common error, to optimize something that should not exist.”

## Reading Steps 1 and 2 — “Question, Then Delete” — Through Concrete Examples

Rather than staying abstract, the article backs up the power of this sequence with concrete examples from Tesla and SpaceX.

- **Tesla’s fiberglass mat:** The process of applying a fiberglass mat to the battery pack had been precisely optimized through complex machinery and multiple steps. The reason was a “fire safety” requirement. But when they actually tested it, the mat contributed almost nothing to fire prevention. Musk removed the entire process.
- **A sensor in the Model 3:** A certain sensor in the battery pack had been treated from the outset as “required.” When Musk asked who had required it, nobody knew. Tracing it back revealed that the engineer who had originally specified it had left the company years earlier, and the sensor was solving a problem that no longer existed. They removed it, and nothing broke.
Musk’s discipline is that every requirement should be tied to a person’s name. The article quotes the following instruction: “Each requirement should come with the name of the person who made it. Never accept that a requirement came from a department.” Departments do not defend requirements; people do — that is the key point.

The article also gives a quantitative standard for how far deletion should go: “If you’re not adding back at least 10% of the things you deleted, you haven’t deleted enough.” As a concrete example, SpaceX’s Raptor engine reached a dramatically lower parts count than comparable engines by repeatedly removing and integrating parts. Tesla also used radar for autopilot for many years, as did the rest of the industry, but Musk questioned that too and completely removed radar in order to shift to a camera-only approach, on the theory that humans drive using only their eyes.

## Why “Simplify, Accelerate, Automate” Come Later

The reason steps 3 and beyond come later is also reinforced through examples of failure.

- **The Model X falcon-wing door** (a counterexample for step 3): The engineering team spent months optimizing a complex door loaded with sensors, motors, and hinges. But Musk later said, in effect, that the door had been a mistake and should never have existed. The optimization felt productive, but the direction itself was wrong.
- **Starship prototype explosions** (step 4): SpaceX builds and tests rockets faster than anyone else, deliberately allowing them to explode. Outsiders saw failure; Musk saw high-speed learning. But acceleration comes fourth. The article summarizes the essence of acceleration this way: “Speed is a multiplier. If you’re doing the wrong thing, speed just gets you to the wrong place faster.”
- **Overautomation in Model 3 production** (step 5): Automation hardens a process, makes it harder to change, and makes it less visible. In producing the Model 3, Tesla tried to automate nearly the entire process with robots, but it became so complex that it broke down repeatedly. They had automated processes that should not have existed in the first place. Musk later admitted: “Excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake. Humans are underrated.” In the end, Tesla removed automation from many tasks and brought human workers back in.
## Why Smart People Are Especially Prone to Getting the Order Wrong

Where the article goes furthest is in arguing that intelligence itself can become a trap. Musk is especially warning smart people. Because smart people are good at optimization, they end up optimizing things that should not exist. They can find efficiencies in bad processes, polish bad ideas, and even make the wrong direction feel productive. In the article’s words, “Intelligence becomes a liability when it lets you avoid the hard question of whether you’re working on the right thing.”

The poster says he experienced this firsthand. In 2023, he dropped out of college and moved from one venture to another: SEO, SEM, email marketing, copywriting, then dropshipping, Amazon FBA (where he lost money), an apparel brand, and affiliate marketing. In every case, he built systems before revenue and made management spreadsheets before he had customers — in other words, he jumped straight to steps 3 through 5 and skipped 1 and 2. The turning point was content and virality. This time, instead of starting with systems, he started with questions; he deleted “best practices” that were not working; he simplified down to the minimum needed for content to work; and only after that did he systematize. The article’s conclusion is clear about the right sequence: Question. Delete. Simplify. Accelerate. Automate. In that order.
