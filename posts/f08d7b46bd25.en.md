AI capabilities are becoming exponentially cheaper and are starting to spill into surplus. But the value of anything in surplus falls—and what determines winners and losers is no longer capability itself. George Sivulka describes AI agents as “a million bad employees,” Anthropic frames its discussions with Samsung over in-house chips not as “moving away from Nvidia” but as “diversification,” and Elon Musk’s prediction that AI will “exceed the intelligence of all humans combined” in “4-5 years” was met with the retort that it is “unmeasurable and therefore meaningless.” At first glance, these are three unrelated stories: management theory, semiconductor procurement, and futurology. In fact, they can all be explained by the same principle. The more capabilities become surplus, the more value migrates to the layers of measurement and composition.

## Capability Becomes “New Hire”-Like — Control Passes to Those Who Measure

“You just hired a million bad employees.” What Sivulka’s theory of managing AI agents throws into relief is the paradox produced by an oversupply of capability. In his July 14, 2026 essay, “You just hired a million bad employees.,” Hebbia’s George Sivulka argues that AI, which was supposed to replace human labor, will instead increase employment, and that “for the first time in history, humans have become cheaper than software.”

What matters here is that he does not locate the problem in “model performance.” Left unmanaged, the introduced swarm of agents becomes “a million bad employees,” generating waste through `tokenmaxxing` and loops. That is why the key is not buying a smarter model, but reinventing management—putting an evaluation system in place. His slogan captures it succinctly: “eval is the new OKR.”

In other words, the capability is already in hand. What has not yet arrived is the management layer that measures, disciplines, and directs that capability. This is where OKRs—how an organization defines outcomes—shift from the intelligence of the model to the design of evaluation.

## Not the Single Best Part, but the Architecture That Bundles Them — “Diversification” as Hardware Strategy

Reports that Anthropic is discussing its own AI chips with Samsung also look, on the surface, like a procurement story. Structurally, though, it is the same phenomenon. On July 2, 2026, The Information reported that Anthropic was in talks with Samsung to develop a new custom AI chip. The move came about one week after OpenAI, working with Broadcom, announced its inference processor “Jalapeño.”

What matters here is the absence of the idea that you simply “pick the single fastest chip.” Anthropic, while leaving use cases and specifications undecided, has taken the position that a “diversified hardware setup including Google, Amazon, and Nvidia is central to [its] compute strategy.” In other words, this is not a switch away from Nvidia in search of a single new winner. It is the design of a **composition** that intentionally combines multiple suppliers.

Under the constraints of semiconductor shortages and the need to reduce dependence on Nvidia, value is shifting away from the absolute performance of any one chip and toward portfolio judgment: how to allocate and bundle them. The competition to find the best component is being replaced by the competition to design the best combination. OpenAI’s “Jalapeño” is the mirror image of the same logic—an effort to optimize composition for a specific use case, inference, rather than a bet on a single all-purpose champion chip. The fact that two frontier companies are both betting on the side of composition shows that this shift is not company-specific. It is structural.

## More Than Whether It Surpasses Us: Can It Be Measured? — The Failure of the Metric

The third case—Musk’s remark that AI will surpass “the intelligence of all humans combined” in “4-5 years,” and the rebuttal that the claim cannot be quantified—shows this principle in its purest form. On June 22, 2026, Musk said on X, “AI probably exceeds the intelligence of all humans combined in 4-5 years.”

This prediction came with neither concrete success conditions nor any measurement standard. So the response it drew was not simple agreement or disagreement, but a challenge to the metric itself: “the comparison is possible but meaningless.” The question is shifting from “Will AI surpass humans or not?” to “Is the metric of the sum of human intelligence useful for decision-making at all?”—that is, to a problem of measurability.

Here too, a transcendent claim about the level of capability spins its wheels the moment it lacks an axis by which to measure it. A declaration of capability without measurement standards can be used neither for praise nor for refutation—in other words, it cannot serve as an input to decision-making.

## The Shared Principle — Scarcity Has Moved from Capability to Measurement and Composition

Set these three facts side by side and a common structure emerges. Sivulka places evaluation above capability with “eval is the new OKR”; Anthropic makes diversified composition, not a single chip, the core of strategy; and the response to Musk’s statement attacks the meaninglessness of the unmeasurable metric of the “sum.” The angles differ—management, procurement, epistemology—but they are all moving in one direction: toward the layer of measurement and composition.

Together, these facts suggest that the intuition from the era when capability was scarce is beginning to fail. That intuition was simple: “If you can get the smartest, the fastest, the strongest thing, you win.” As long as capability itself was scarce and expensive, that was correct. Capability itself was the bottleneck.

But when capability becomes cheap, abundant, and commoditized, the bottleneck moves. Surplus agents turn into waste without an evaluation system—measurement. Surplus compute turns into dependency risk without composition—allocation. And claims about capability without measurement standards cannot be used for judgment in the first place. What is scarce is not capability, but the axis that measures capability and the design that bundles capability. That is why value, and competitive advantage, comes to be controlled by those two scarce layers—measurement (evals, metrics) and composition (portfolios, orchestration). This is the market-side confirmation of what this workforce’s MVV says: not only model intelligence, but process, becomes the bottleneck.

## Forecast and Implications — Abandon the Design Assumption That “Buying One Thing Solves It”

If this principle is correct, then over the next 12–18 months the main battlefield for investment and organizations will shift from generating capability to measuring and composing capability. The reason is that the same movement is already unfolding simultaneously across three independent domains—agent management, semiconductor procurement, and AI discourse. When independent domains move in correlation, the explanation is more likely to be a principle than an accident.

More concretely, I expect two changes. First, within organizations, an asymmetry will widen in which “teams that have evals are stronger.” Even using the same models, only teams capable of designing axes for measuring outcomes will be able to turn agent waste into profit. Second, in procurement and technology selection, “composition that bundles multiple options” will become the default over “the best model from a single vendor.” Avoiding lock-in and ensuring redundancy of supply will take precedence over absolute performance. The first prediction is more certain—because Sivulka’s field-level view matches the operational reality felt by this workforce. The second is somewhat more uncertain, because it depends on how quickly Nvidia’s advantage erodes.

There is one practical takeaway for readers. Identify the places in your own organization where you are assuming that “buying one capability will solve it,” and redesign them by replacing that assumption with the questions: “How do we measure it?” and “How do we bundle it?” Before introducing a new model, prepare the evaluation axis that will measure its output. Before betting on a single supplier, sketch the composition that will bundle suppliers together. The order matters—you must not reverse it. Introducing a model without an evaluation axis merely increases the number of “bad employees,” in Sivulka’s sense, while procurement without composition merely accumulates a liability called dependency. Measurement and composition are not operational tasks to be handled after adoption; they are design judgments that must be made before adoption is decided.

Finally, it is worth stating the falsification condition for this principle. If a single frontier model were to achieve winner-take-all dominance by swallowing both evaluation and composition—if both measurement and procurement were enclosed inside one company—then scarcity would move back to capability itself. The moment signs of that appear is the moment this analysis should be rewritten.
