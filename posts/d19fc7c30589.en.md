South Korea's government just promised free generative AI to every citizen. Stripe agreed to buy AI-routing service OpenRouter for more than $7 billion. Anthropic reported that faster coding has simply pushed the bottleneck into the human-paced stages next to it. Three events, three industries, no obvious connection — except that all three are explained by the same principle: whenever one layer of a system is freed from friction, a new gate appears immediately next to it.

## The gate hidden behind "free"

South Korea's "AI for All" plan allocates up to 512 Nvidia B200 chips across three consortia — including the country's two major telecoms and Kakao-affiliated companies — with a public beta starting in September and no cap on usage. But the same plan requires that at least 80% of queries be routed to domestically built foundation models. What was liberalized was access itself, not the choice of which model answers the question. As the source explanation "South Korea Will Give Every Citizen Free AI Access" reports, the free offer is built on top of an explicit industrial policy: reducing dependence on U.S. and Chinese models.

A 512-chip cap on the compute being handed out for free is itself an admission that the underlying resource is not unlimited. When a scarce resource is distributed for free, the power to allocate it has to live somewhere — and Seoul chose to keep that power at the point where a query picks its model, via the 80% domestic-model quota. What looks like an act of removing friction (cost) is simultaneously an act of installing a new one (model choice), inside the same policy document.

## The moment "neutrality" stops being free

"What the Stripe-OpenRouter Deal Means for AI" describes a company that connected roughly 80 million developers to more than 400 models through a single API, taking about 5% of every dollar of inference that passed through it — and that company has just been bought for over $7 billion by the payments processor that already handled its settlement. OpenRouter's entire value proposition rested on being a neutral tap that connected fairly to any model. The moment routing decisions and billing sit inside the same corporate structure, that premise is up for question.

The size of the deal is itself informative: a 5% cut of inference spend is a fee structure that grows automatically as AI usage grows, which makes a "neutral router" look, from a payments company's vantage point, less like a public utility and more like a compounding collection point. Neutrality is not a permanent property of a routing layer — it is a property that only holds as long as the capital structure behind it stays independent. Once that structure consolidates, neutrality survives only as a claim, not as a guarantee.

## The faster the code, the more the next stage clogs

"Anthropic's AI-Native SDLC Playbook" argues that as coding accelerates, the stages that still run at human speed — plan, design, review, deploy — become the new bottleneck. Across the six stages it names (Plan, Design, Build, Test, Deploy, Maintain), speeding up Build alone does nothing for overall throughput if Plan and Deploy on either side of it still move at the pace of human judgment.

This is ordinary queueing behavior: accelerate one stage, and work piles up at whichever neighboring stage hasn't been accelerated yet. That Anthropic felt the need to prescribe governance moves for all six stages, not just coding, suggests its own Applied AI team learned the hard way that shipping a faster code-writing tool does not by itself raise an organization's throughput. Look specifically at Test and Deploy: code can now be generated in minutes, but reviewing it, judging its risk, and approving its release into production still depends on the finite resource of human attention. The structure is identical to the other two cases — the writing stage was liberated, and the gate simply shifted one stage over, to the judgment stage.

## The common principle: liberation relocates the gate, it does not remove it

What unites all three cases is that removing friction from one layer does not reduce the total amount of friction in the system — it transfers that friction to an adjacent layer. In Korea's case, the cost gate disappeared and a model-selection gate was installed by state policy in its place. In OpenRouter's case, the connectivity gate disappeared and a settlement gate came under one company's discretion. In Anthropic's SDLC case, the implementation gate disappeared and the judgment gate grew proportionally thicker.

These facts suggest a principle: the total friction in a system does not shrink without outside intervention. Liberalization that promises abundance — free access, neutral connectivity, faster execution — is always achieved by removing a constraint from one specific layer, but the constraint itself does not vanish from the system. It is pushed onto whichever adjacent layer has not yet been liberalized, whether that layer is national industrial policy, a company's payment infrastructure, or the speed of human judgment, and it recrystallizes there. Friction behaves less like something eliminated and more like something conserved.

So when a headline announces that a given layer has been "freed," the question worth asking is not "has the friction disappeared?" but "where did the gate move to?" Skip that second question and the story of liberalization gets consumed at face value, while the more consequential fact — that the location of control has been rewritten — goes unnoticed. If this principle is wrong, there should be at least one case where liberalizing a layer created no new constraint anywhere adjacent to it; finding that counterexample is the fastest way to test the claim.

## Forecast and implications

If this principle holds, three parallel developments should become visible over the next several quarters. First, other governments following Korea's lead into free national AI access will most likely bundle it with some form of domestic-allocation rule, similar in spirit to the 80% quota — the probability is high, because a government pursuing both free access and industrial-policy leverage has no structural reason to choose one over the other, as Korea's own design demonstrates.

Second, AI infrastructure intermediaries that market themselves as "neutral" — routers, marketplaces, evaluation layers — will remain attractive acquisition targets for companies that already control an adjacent chokepoint such as payments, cloud, or compute — a moderate-to-high probability outcome, since neutrality is sustainable only under an independent capital structure, and a neutral intermediary without independent capital has no market mechanism to block that kind of consolidation.

Third, organizations that adopt coding agents will, within the next few quarters, broadly report the same experience Anthropic already observed: implementation speed gains stall against the human-paced stages of planning, review, and deployment — a very high probability, since it is already documented in the source article itself. The practical takeaway for readers collapses to one habit: whenever you encounter the words "free," "faster," or "neutral," look at the layer immediately next to it. The gate has not disappeared. It has only moved somewhere you were not looking.
