In the AI era, both value and cost reside not in the visible deliverable but in the process surrounding it. CyrilXBT, in a Fable 5 post, listed 60 workflows that earn money overnight (Agentic AI); Databricks measured GLM 5.2 against Opus 4.8 on its own multi-million-line codebase and found equal quality at roughly 30% lower cost (AI Productivity); and Apple sued OpenAI over the removal of trade secrets (Big Tech). A promotional list, a cost benchmark, an IP lawsuit—these three things, which seem completely unrelated, are in fact explained by the same principle.

## Verification Is the Real Product; the Agent Is Just a Component

CyrilXBT’s Fable 5 article (Agentic AI) is a promotional list of 60 workflows that use Fable 5 as a “night-shift worker running all night without supervision.” But the core of the article is not the number of flashy use cases. It is the fact that every workflow rests on the same three elements—trigger, agent, and verification step—and that if you skip verification, what you receive in the morning is an expensive mistake.

The real question here is where the center of gravity of value lies. The agent itself—the part that writes text, emits code, and structures transactions—is an interchangeable component reused across all 60 workflows. What determines success or failure in each workflow is the design of the verification step that catches errors in its output. A workflow with verification removed may appear to run, but in reality it turns into a machine for scaling errors overnight. In other words, what “earns money while you sleep” is not the agent, but the verification process surrounding it.

### What Looks Optional Is Actually the Core

The reason the promotional list emphasizes verification 60 times is, paradoxically, that it is the component most likely to be omitted. Triggers and agents are visible, and when you run them you feel that something is happening. Verification appears to keep things running even when it does nothing, and cutting it often breaks nothing in the short term. What breaks is the next morning—and what is broken at that point is the judgment that chose to remove the process.

## Token Price Is a Poor Proxy for Cost

The internal benchmark Databricks published on July 8, 2026 (AI Productivity) evaluated models and harnesses on coding tasks actually performed by its own engineers. The result: the open model GLM 5.2 was statistically tied with Opus 4.8 on quality, while task cost came in at $1.28 versus $1.94, roughly 30% cheaper. Databricks therefore concluded that token price is an insufficient indicator of real task cost.

What matters even more is this: even with the same model, task cost can move by more than 2x based solely on differences in the harness. Even when the “deliverable,” the model, is fixed, actual cost can swing twofold depending on the process that drives it—how context is assembled, how retries are handled, how tool calls are designed. That is why the conclusion is that “efficiency does not begin and end with model selection.” A model you think you chose because it is cheap can end up costing more than an expensive model if you place it inside a crude harness.

### The More Visible the Number, the Further It Is from Reality

Token price is the most visible number because it appears on the pricing sheet. But that visibility is exactly the trap. Real cost is determined by the harness process, which does not appear on the pricing sheet. This is the same structure as CyrilXBT’s verification step (Agentic AI). Both value and cost reside not in the central model or the agent itself, but in the process surrounding them.

## What Could Not Be Taken Out Was Not the Product but Process Knowledge

On July 10, 2026, Apple sued OpenAI in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California (Big Tech). The claim is that, through the hiring of former Apple executives, trade secrets were systematically taken out, including technical information about unreleased products and Apple’s proprietary metal-finishing techniques. As OpenAI pushes ahead with development of its first hardware product, the movement of talent has turned into an inter-company IP conflict.

Here too, the point of dispute is not the finished “deliverable.” If a product has reached the market, you can buy it, tear it down, and get clues for imitation. What the lawsuit is trying to protect is the process knowledge of how that metal finish is actually achieved—the undocumented, tacit procedure that resides in the people who have run it and moves with them. What Apple fears is not a copy of the product, but leakage of the process that produces the product in the first place.

### Process Can Only Travel Through People, So It Moves with Them

Why can hiring become an IP conflict? Because process knowledge cannot be fully written down in a specification, and only exists in complete form inside the people who have actually run the process. That means the only way to acquire the process is to acquire the people, and movement of people becomes, directly, movement of process. When the value worth defending resides not in the deliverable but in the process, the defensive line is drawn not around the patent shelf but around employment contracts.

## The Common Principle: Value and Cost Reside in the “Surrounding Process”

These three facts are, on the surface, completely different stories. One is a promotional workflow collection, another a cost benchmark, and another a trade-secret lawsuit. To readers, neither genre nor players appear to overlap. But if you line them up and strip them down to the underlying structure, the same shape emerges.

In CyrilXBT’s case, what earned money was not the agent but the verification process (Agentic AI). In Databricks’ case, what determined cost was not the model but the harness process (AI Productivity). In Apple’s case, what needed protection was not the product but the process knowledge behind metal finishing (Big Tech). In every case, the prominent center—agent, model, finished product—was interchangeable, while the center of gravity for value and cost lay on the side of the surrounding process.

These facts suggest the following. The more AI rapidly commoditizes the core deliverable, the more what creates differentiation is not the deliverable itself but the process around it. If generative capability becomes cheap and universally available, then which agent you use or which model brand you choose no longer creates a meaningful difference. What creates the difference is process design: how the output is verified, what harness drives it, and what tacit knowledge shapes it into final form. The cheaper and more uniform deliverables become, the more competition is pushed outward into process.

That means this principle can be stated as a falsifiable claim: “The visible units—the agent itself, token price, the finished product—are poor proxies for value. The real thing is the process.” If deliverables themselves were the primary driver of value, then the cost gap between GLM 5.2 and Opus 4.8 should not be able to swing twofold depending on the harness, and Apple should be able to defend itself simply by preventing products from being reverse-engineered. In reality, neither is true. The moment you mistake the proxy for the real thing, costs double and value leaks away.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, then at least some of the changes ahead can be predicted.

First, choosing among model or agent brands will cease to be the main battleground of decision-making. As Databricks showed, even with the same model, cost can move by 2x depending on the harness (AI Productivity). More than articles comparing brands, it is metrics that measure the quality of process design—verification, harnessing, context assembly—that are likely to acquire practical value. The reason is simple: performance gaps on the deliverable side are shrinking (GLM 5.2 tied Opus 4.8), and the remaining differences are shifting toward process.

Second, what organizations defend and compete to capture will shift from deliverables to process knowledge. The Apple v. OpenAI structure (Big Tech) will be repeated more often as AI makes deliverables easier to imitate. The focus of competition will move away from documented specifications and toward process knowledge residing in people, and as a result the movement of talent itself will increasingly be treated as an IP event. The confidence level here is moderate, but the more deliverables become commoditized, the more likely it becomes.

The practical implication can be reduced to one point: when evaluating anything, look not at the central deliverable but at the process surrounding it. If you are adopting an agent, first check whether a verification step is built into the design (Agentic AI). If you are choosing a model, measure task cost and harness performance, not token price (AI Productivity). If you are hiring a person, look not at the title on the résumé but at the process that person has actually run (Big Tech). The more visible the number, the further it tends to be from reality—making that suspicion your default setting is the cheapest insurance against misreading cost and value in an era when deliverables are getting cheaper.
