The center of gravity for value is moving away from the artifact itself and toward the layer upstream that decides “what to keep and who gets it.” At first glance, three developments seem unrelated: the government stepping in on whether GPT-5.6 should be released and approving access customer by customer; Elon Musk’s five-step process that puts “deletion” ahead of optimization; and Palantir’s Mr. Karp sharply criticizing “frontier AI labs” for neglecting the implementation layer. In fact, all three can be explained by the same principle. Outside the race to improve performance, the selection layer—the layer that decides what to keep, what to discard, and who receives it—is beginning to take control.

## The Decision to “Release” Has Itself Moved Upstream

According to L2’s “The White House asks OpenAI for a limited release of the new GPT-5.6 model,” The Information reported that OpenAI plans not to make its latest model broadly available, but to distribute it first only to a limited set of partners. During the preview period, the government is said to “approve access on a customer-by-customer basis,” with the Office of the National Cyber Director and the Office of Science and Technology Policy requesting a limited release. It marks a turning point: an administration that initially championed a “hands-off” approach has, following the June 2 executive order, begun intervening in the release decision itself.

What matters here is that the target of intervention is not the contents of the model, but its “distribution.” What is happening is not a contest over capability, but a transfer of the authority to decide “who gets to use it” from the model maker to a higher gatekeeping layer. The article places this alongside the precedent of Anthropic’s Project Glasswing (Claude Mythos). No matter how much model performance improves, the layer that approves distribution is increasingly determining the flow of value. The competition over performance will continue inside the labs, but the most consequential decision—who actually receives the result—has already moved outside the model itself.

## Putting “Deletion” Before Optimization

What L2’s “Elon Musk’s ‘algorithm’” gets at is not the content of the steps, but their order. The five-step process Musk repeatedly uses at SpaceX and Tesla differs from the instinct of many engineers, who begin with optimization, acceleration, and automation. Instead, he puts “question every requirement” and “delete” first. The core idea is distilled in Musk’s own words: “The most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist.”

Here too, the source of value lies not in how much you refine the object—whether parts, requirements, or models—but in the prior judgment about “what to keep.” Optimization does not create value from zero. Worse, if you polish what should have been eliminated, the better your technology, the faster you accelerate investment in the wrong direction. Deletion and selection are the upstream steps that determine the leverage of all the effort that follows. Abstracted, the claim is this: outcomes are controlled less by the people who polish than by the people who decide what is worth polishing. Reverse that order, and performance itself becomes a liability.

## Value Accumulates Not in “Performance” but in “Implementation”

In L2’s “‘Frontier AI labs are disliked,’” Palantir’s Mr. Karp says in a CNBC interview that Anthropic and OpenAI “don’t understand how much they are disliked.” The heart of his critique is that while the labs focus on the race over model performance, they neglect the implementation layer where companies actually realize value. By Gartner’s estimate, only 28% of AI use cases fully meet ROI expectations. The remaining 72% are failing to convert into value not because of inadequate model capability, but because implementation and selection are missing.

What that number points to is not a limit in performance, but a fault line between performance and value. That is why OpenAI has acquired the consulting firm Tomoro and assembled a deployment support unit, effectively moving itself down from the performance layer into the implementation layer. The lab side, too, has begun to acknowledge that fault line. What the article describes is the reality that value does not remain in the performance of the output, but in the layer that selects and fits it to the context of actual operations. The people who create capability and the people who extract value from capability are no longer the same.

## The Shared Principle: The Center of Gravity Has Shifted from “Making” to “Choosing”

Put these three facts side by side, and the surface topics—government regulation, management method, enterprise ROI on AI deployment—look unrelated. But the structure running beneath them is the same. In each case, the center of gravity for value and control is shifting away from “the performance of the artifact itself” and toward the selection layer that decides, beforehand, “what to keep, who gets it, and how it is implemented.” In the GPT-5.6 case, the government takes hold of the approval layer for distribution. In Musk’s process, deletion is placed upstream of optimization. In Karp’s critique, the implementation layer is explicitly identified as where value accumulates. The actors in these three cases—the government, a manager, and customer companies—are completely different, yet they point to the same layer. Together, these facts suggest that the more powerful frontier models become, the more commoditized they become as such, and the more leverage escapes upward into the selection layer above them.

Why does this happen? The more capability grows exponentially and becomes widely available, the less scarce “having a better model” becomes in itself. What becomes scarce is the judgment required to correctly decide, from among countless options, “what not to build, whom not to give it to, and which requirements to throw away.” Performance can be matched and imitated continuously, but the quality of selection depends on context and is therefore much harder to replicate, because it resides in the specificity of who keeps what in which situation. That is why control shifts away from the site of optimization and toward the upstream layer that “asks, deletes, and approves.” That is the So What: the marginal return on investment in increasing capability declines, while the marginal return on investment in improving selection rises sharply in relative terms. Holding performance constant, what determines winners and losers is no longer the precision of refinement, but the precision of choice.

## Predictions and Implications

If this principle is correct, several changes are likely to continue. First, access gatekeeping will become a strategic chokepoint. “Customer-by-customer approval” and the precedent of Project Glasswing are not exceptions; they are likely to spread as a standard form of governance around frontier capability—whether by governments or by the labs themselves, the layer that selects distribution will hold power. Second, downward integration by the labs will continue. As with the Tomoro acquisition, operators at the performance layer will have to move down into the implementation and selection layers, or else the value they create will be captured by others. Third, inside organizations as well, teams that institutionalize Musk’s ordering—deletion before optimization—will outperform competitors using the same tools, not because of superior tools but because of superior sequencing.

That said, this principle has a falsifiable boundary. If, in some generation, model capability jumps discontinuously and the performance gap itself once again becomes decisive leverage, then the shift in the center of gravity toward the selection layer will reverse. What readers should watch is whether the next capability jump creates a discontinuity so large that “anyone who uses it wins,” or whether, as before, it is quickly imitated and followed, becoming commoditized again. For now, both the ROI gap (28%) and commoditization pressure are favoring the selection layer, making it more likely that this shift will continue.

The practical implication comes down to one thing. In your own work, before trying to improve the quality of output (= performance), invest in the upstream selection process: “what not to build, who should receive it, and which requirements to eliminate.” The more tool performance converges, the more that becomes the only place left to create differentiation. Leverage resides not in the hand that polishes, but in the eye that chooses.
