There are three facts.

First, Maor Shlomo built Base44 in 4 months, got it to roughly $1.5 million in monthly revenue within 1 month, and sold it to Wix for $80 million—almost entirely on his own. Second, one company received a $500 million Claude bill after failing to set usage caps, and Uber had already exhausted its 2026 AI coding budget by April. Third, Goldman Sachs forecasts that token consumption will increase 24x from 2026 to 2030. At first glance, these seem like unrelated stories: a solo-founder success, a cost-control failure, and a macro forecast. But in fact they are all explained by the same principle. AI has not eliminated labor costs; it has only shifted the locus of cost to tokens.

## What Remains After Labor Costs Disappear

The economics of Base44, as described in “Entrepreneurs Running ‘One-Person Companies’ with AI,” are often read as a story of liberation from labor intensity. Work that had been distributed across PMs, QA, and developers was consolidated into one person through AI agents, and Shlomo launched a business worth tens of millions of dollars without building a 100-person organization. But the same article makes clear that this consolidation is not free. Compute costs reached hundreds of thousands of dollars per month, while the absence of specialized judgment and the physical strain of operating alone 24 hours a day limited how far the model could scale.

In other words, the bottleneck of labor costs disappeared, but another bottleneck rose in its place: compute costs. You no longer need to hire an organization, but the token spend required to keep agents running remains as a fixed cost. The constraint of labor did not vanish; it survived by changing form.

What matters is that this shift is not just a simple swap from one cost category to another. Labor costs can be adjusted in stages through the on-off switch of hiring and firing, and the pace of increase or decrease is constrained by human decision-making. Token spend, by contrast, is a variable cost that can pile up without a ceiling based on however much the agents consume. Shlomo was able to operate at the scale of tens of millions of dollars alone because he replaced the heavy fixed cost of an organization with the light variable cost of computation. That lightness is agility, but it is also the loss of brakes. The “runaway billing” in the next section is precisely the flip side of that lightness.

## Why Bills Grow Even When Unit Prices Fall

“The Arrival of the Token Bill” shows what happens when this residual cost runs wild not at the level of an individual, but at the scale of a company. The paradox is that the per-token unit price is actually falling. And yet enterprise AI spending is still blasting through budgets. Uber exhausted its annual budget by April, and one company received a $500 million bill, not because unit prices were high, but because the spread of autonomous agents caused consumption volume itself to explode.

### Cheapness Triggers Consumption

This is a classic structure. The cheaper a resource becomes, the more total consumption of that resource rises. Falling unit prices do not mean savings; they mean release into more use cases. In the era when humans submitted each query one by one and remained conscious of the cost each time, consumption was naturally throttled by the brake of human attention. But once autonomous agents begin consuming tokens without a human in the loop, that brake comes off. Because it was human labor that had constrained volume, removing the human through agentization directly translates into an explosion in consumption. The “lightness” Base44 demonstrated in the previous section turns into runaway behavior at enterprise scale.

That is why the Linux Foundation has launched the Tokenomics Foundation, and why a vendor market for visualizing and optimizing token spending is forming so quickly. This is not accidental. Just as HR, accounting, and hiring management developed in an era when labor costs dominated, an age in which tokens dominate will require a new managerial discipline around tokens—as institutions: spending caps, departmental allocation, real-time monitoring. As L2 explicitly notes, the $500 million bill was caused by “failing to set usage caps.” It was an accident that fell into the gap before that discipline existed.

## The “Inflection Point” Wall Street Sees

In “The Token Explosion Created by Agentic AI and the ‘Margin Inflection Point,’” Goldman Sachs Research quantifies this structure at macro scale. As agentic AI spreads, token consumption is projected to rise 24x from 2026 to 2030, reaching 120 trillion tokens per month, while inference unit prices fall at an annual rate of 60–70%. The crucial point is that these two forces do not cancel each other out. Consumption is expanding 24x faster than prices are falling. That is why hyperscalers are likely to hit a “margin inflection point” within the next 3–12 months, according to analyst Jim Schneider.

The four axes Schneider lays out—demand explosion, falling costs, supply constraints, and asymmetry of adoption—all converge on a single point: falling unit prices do not mean falling total spend. Demand explosion and asymmetry of adoption push consumption upward, while supply constraints act to slow the pace of unit-price declines. In other words, these four axes each guarantee from a different angle the conditions under which growth in consumption continues to outstrip declines in unit price. Base44’s hundreds of thousands of dollars per month, the $500 million bill, and 120 trillion tokens per month all sit on the same curve, even if the scales differ. Across the three levels of the individual, the enterprise, and the macro economy, the same dynamic appears in self-similar form.

## Common Principle: Costs Do Not Disappear; They Move to the Scarcest Resource

These three facts suggest a single principle. What AI brings is not the disappearance of cost, but the migration of bottlenecks.

For a long time, the scarce resource in building software was skilled human labor. AI agents relieve that scarcity—as Base44 shows, one person can do the work of a team. But scarcity itself does not disappear. The moment labor becomes abundant, the next-scarcest thing—the compute required to run agents, in other words tokens—moves to the foreground as the new bottleneck.

And this migration does not stop just because the unit price of compute falls. Cheaper resources are released into uses that previously did not justify the cost, and consumption expands beyond the decline in unit price. As a result, total cost rises rather than falls, and the constraint shifts again—to somewhere else: power, semiconductor supply, and even the discipline required to manage the bill itself. Scarcity does not stay in one place; it moves through the system. That is the common dynamic running through what looked like unrelated stories of solo founding, runaway costs, and macro forecasting.

## Forecasts and Implications

If this principle is correct, several conclusions follow with high probability.

First, the expectation that “AI will save on labor costs” will in many cases end up as little more than a transfer onto token spend. If token consumption exceeds the labor cost saved, total cost will rise rather than fall. Uber’s budget overrun is not an exception; it is a leading indicator.

Second, the source of competitive advantage will shift from “being able to hire lots of people” to “being able to use tokens efficiently.” The rise of the Tokenomics Foundation and cost-optimization vendors is nothing less than the formation of an industry around this new scarcity. Functions once analogous to the HR department will become institutionalized as token management.

Third, the migration of bottlenecks will continue. Once tokens become manageable, the next scarce thing may be electricity, semiconductor supply, or human attention for validating agent decisions. The important thing is to begin from the premise that no matter which unit price falls, “constraints do not disappear.”

The practical implication is clear. Stop measuring the effect of AI adoption in terms of “labor cost reduction,” and start measuring it in terms of “where the bottleneck moved.” If you are running a one-person company, the next fixed cost is tokens. If you are running an enterprise, the next budget line item is tokens. Those who identify in advance where scarcity is moving will be the ones with the advantage on the far side of the inflection point.
