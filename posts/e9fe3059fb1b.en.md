AI competition is quietly shifting from “how smart the model is” to “which layer we delegate to machines.” Three pieces of news that surfaced over the past week look, at first glance, completely unrelated: semiconductor procurement, usage statistics for a coding platform, and changes in development style. But in fact, all three can be explained by the same single principle. Humans—or each player in the ecosystem—are steadily raising the layer they “specify themselves,” while delegating the layer beneath to machines or partners.

## The Silicon Layer: From Something You Buy to Something You Design

According to L2’s “Anthropic Discusses In-House AI Chip With Samsung,” The Information reported on July 2, 2026, that Anthropic is in talks with Samsung to develop a new custom AI chip. The move came about a week after OpenAI teamed up with Broadcom to unveil its inference processor “Jalapeño,” against a backdrop of semiconductor shortages and efforts to reduce dependence on Nvidia.

What matters here is Anthropic’s stance. The company frames this not as “moving away from Nvidia” but as “diversification,” saying that “a diversified hardware portfolio—including Google, Amazon and Nvidia—is key to our compute strategy.” In other words, this is not replacement. It is the addition of options.

What is happening here is an increase in the level of abstraction being specified. In the past, frontier companies specified “which GPU and how many to buy.” Now what they specify is “how much inference compute capacity is needed,” while the design of the chip that satisfies those requirements is pushed downward to partners such as Samsung and Broadcom. The role shifts from choosing what to buy to deciding what to delegate for design. The player’s control point has moved up one level, from procurement to architecture.

Even the choice of the word “diversification” supports that reading. While saying the use case and specifications are still undecided, Anthropic calls its hardware configuration “key to our strategy.” The goal is not to build every individual chip entirely by itself. The point is to hold onto the higher-order requirement—compute capacity—while distributing the implementation beneath it across multiple partners: Nvidia, Google, Amazon, and Samsung. The more delegation targets there are, the more the delegating side narrows its grip to the higher-level specification of “what” and “how much.”

## The Work Layer: From Instructing Steps to Delegating Jobs

L2’s “OpenAI’s Codex Use Accelerates” captures the same movement at a different layer. According to a report jointly compiled by OpenAI, Columbia, Duke, and the University of Pennsylvania, use of the agentic coding and work platform “Codex” is expanding rapidly on a token basis.

The numbers are striking. Of the output tokens generated by OpenAI employees, 99.8% go through Codex, while for individual users the figure is only 16.5%. And among external organizations, Codex’s usage share rose from just above 0% in August 2025 to about 17% in less than a year. The report reads this as evidence that AI use is shifting from chat and search toward “delegated work.”

Here too, the boundary line is moving upward. Conventional AI usage was a one-question, one-answer interaction: “Answer this question.” What Codex represents is delegation: “Take on this task.” What humans specify is no longer each individual step, but the unit of work itself. The chunk being handed to the machine has grown one level larger, from procedure to job.

And the size of the internal-external gap tells us that this transition is still far from evenly distributed. Even after a year for imitation, a distance remains between OpenAI internally—where delegation accounts for nearly all tokens—and the outside world, where organizations and individuals remain in the low double digits. The fact that this gap persists even though the same tool is publicly available will matter later.

## The Authorship Layer: From Writing Syntax to Writing Intent

The third item, L2’s “The Spread of ‘Vibe Coding,’” shows the same principle playing out in software development itself. The WSJ reports that programmers are rapidly adopting an interactive, iterative development style called “vibe coding” with support tools such as Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex.

The core point of the article is straightforward. The center of gravity in development is shifting away from handwriting code and toward communicating intent in natural language, then repeatedly generating and revising. As a result, the very assumptions of the software development lifecycle are being rearranged.

Once again, it is the same form. What developers specify is no longer syntax but intent, while the production of syntax is handled by the machine. The control point has moved up one level, from code to intent.

## The Common Principle: Why Does the Boundary of Delegation Rise?

These three facts—silicon diversification, work delegation to Codex, and intent-based coding—all point to the same movement. **Humans—or each player—raise by one level the abstraction they “specify themselves,” and delegate the layer beneath to machines or partners.** From procurement to architecture, from execution to delegation, from syntax to intent: the layers differ, but the line of “how much do I specify myself, and from where downward do I delegate?” is moving upward across all of them.

Why does it rise? Because the moment the lower layer can be handled by machines at sufficient quality, continuing to specify that layer yourself becomes pure cost. It is faster to talk about compute requirements than to scrutinize GPU model numbers, faster to hand over a job than to write out the steps, and faster to describe intent than to type syntax. Each time the lower side of the boundary is automated, rational players raise the point of specification by one level.

If that is true, then the So What converges into one conclusion. The source of competitive advantage shifts from absolute model performance to “the ability to accurately turn intent into specification and verify the resulting output.” This hypothesis can be stated in a falsifiable form: the enormous gap in Codex usage—99.8% inside OpenAI versus about 17% outside—cannot be explained by differences in model capability. External organizations can connect to the same model. What creates the gap is how deeply they have implemented the discipline of delegation and verification within the organization: in other words, their level of mastery. If this principle is wrong, then external usage rates should rise in lockstep with the speed at which models get smarter. If it is right, they will rise at the speed with which organizations acquire patterns for delegation and verification. Future adoption curves will decide which is true.

## Predictions and Implications

From this principle, we can draw three implications with differing degrees of confidence.

First, with high probability, silicon diversification among frontier companies will continue. It is no coincidence that Anthropic’s talks with Samsung and OpenAI’s partnership with Broadcom appeared in the same month. As long as the twin motives of supply constraints and reducing dependency remain in place, the move to design and distribute compute infrastructure more directly will not stop; it will only accelerate. That said, as Anthropic itself emphasizes, this is “diversification,” not “replacement,” and it does not mean Nvidia disappears.

Second, with medium to high probability, Codex-style “delegation” will spread outside company walls—but the rate-limiting step will be not the model, but the organization’s verification system. The scale of the gap—99.8% internally versus about 17% externally—is evidence enough. Organizations that first establish the discipline on the receiving side of delegation—what to hand over, how to check the output, and where humans step in with judgment—will be the first to climb this curve.

Third, as a forecast, the role of “turning intent into specification and verifying output” will become a newly scarce skill. What vibe coding shows is a handoff in which the relative value of the skill of writing quickly declines, while the value of the skill of “accurately stating what you want to build and evaluating what comes back” rises. The higher the boundary of delegation climbs, the more the upper side of that boundary—specification and verification—remains the main human arena.

The practical implication for readers is this: whether as an individual or as an organization, the muscle to build from here is not “move your hands faster,” but “delegate correctly and verify what comes back.” Each time the line of what can be entrusted to machines moves up by one level, your value will be determined by what you do above that line.
