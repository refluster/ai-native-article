Ten of the S&P 500's 84% five-year total return came from NVIDIA alone, and roughly 25 points — about a third of the index's five-year gain — came from just five stocks, according to an analysis financial-commentary account The Kobeissi Letter posted on X. The same account, in a separate post, claimed that 2026 US corporate software investment has hit an annualized $797.6 billion, roughly double the pace of ten years ago, framing it as evidence of "the AI boom's next phase." And on July 30, Okta agreed to acquire AI-agent identity-security startup Permiso Security for roughly $200 million, shifting its own center of gravity from "verifying a user at login" to "continuously monitoring what happens after access is granted." Three events, unrelated in industry and scale — yet they resolve to the same principle: in an AI-driven economy, trust no longer lives in the fact of having passed through the gate. It lives in whether anyone is continuously tracking what happens after.

## What concentration hides behind an index's "gate"

An index fund sells the reassurance of a gate you can check once: "diversified across 500 stocks." But The Kobeissi Letter's numbers show a different picture inside. Of the S&P 500's 84% five-year total return, NVIDIA (NVDA) alone contributed more than 10 points — roughly 12% of the index's total gain. Add Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom, and Alphabet, and the top five stocks' combined contribution reaches about 25 points, roughly a third of the index's five-year return. The post drew over 177,000 views and reactions on both sides.

Checking the fact "I bought the S&P 500" at the gate tells you nothing about what you're actually exposed to. Only tracking the after-the-fact breakdown — which stocks are driving the number — reveals that owning the index has become, in substance, a concentrated bet on AI-infrastructure names. Diversification is true at the gate; it stays true only if someone keeps checking afterward.

### Why the gate and the contents can diverge

The index's construction rule hasn't changed — the same market-cap weighting has simply produced concentration as AI-infrastructure stocks rallied. The gate (the construction rule) has been "correct" for five years running; what changed is the after-the-fact composition, and that shift is invisible without continuous tracking. Rule correctness and outcome diversification are different variables — confirming the first tells you nothing about the second.

## A headline number with no path back to its source

If the NVIDIA case is "the gate is honest but the contents need tracking," the $797.6 billion software-investment figure is its mirror image. The Kobeissi Letter's August 3 post framed that annualized number — twice the pace of a decade ago — as proof of "the AI boom's next phase," the shift from building models to deploying them across industries. But the post itself names no source agency, no dataset, for the $797.6 billion figure. A reader who wants to check it has no link to follow from the start.

This is the inverse failure of the index case. There, the entry claim ("diversified") was literally true, and only after-the-fact tracking exposed what it hid. Here, the entry claim ("$797.6 billion, double the prior pace") was never built with a path for after-the-fact verification at all — it's designed to be cited once and never re-checked. The forms differ, but both come from the same missing discipline: believing what's presented at the gate and never tracking what happens to it afterward.

## The day continuous monitoring got a price tag

Okta's acquisition is the market's answer to that missing discipline. Okta has spent its history selling exactly the gate: verifying a user at the moment of login. That same company, on July 30, bought Permiso Security, an AI-agent identity-security startup. Okta hasn't disclosed deal terms, but TechCrunch, citing people familiar with the matter, reported a valuation just under $200 million, structured almost entirely in cash. The acquisition signals that identity management's center of gravity is moving from "verify the user at login" to "continuously monitor what the user, application, or AI agent does after it has access."

Okta, by buying rather than building outward from its own core business, is effectively conceding the limits of the gate-verification model it built its business on. An agent that keeps acting autonomously after login makes the pass issued at that instant a guarantee about nothing that follows. The market has now put a price — at least $200 million — on the capability of tracking what happens after.

## The principle these three facts point to

Different in scale and industry, all three facts illuminate the same phenomenon from a different angle. An index's promise of diversification, a claim built on an investment statistic, a guarantee built on login authentication — each has treated the fact of having passed through the gate as the basis for trust. But as AI becomes a primary economic actor, that premise breaks down. An agent keeps acting autonomously after the gate; whether an investment turns into real returns is a separate question from whether it was made; and an index reveals what's actually driving its return only after the fact, stock by stock.

These facts suggest that the center of gravity of "verification" in the AI era is shifting from a point — a pass/fail judgment at the gate — to a line: continuous tracking after the gate. Okta's acquisition is the first explicit case of a price being attached to that shift: the market priced the capability of after-the-gate tracking itself at roughly $200 million. Conversely, a claim with no mechanism for after-the-fact tracking (the $797.6 billion investment statistic) and a structure invisible without after-the-fact tracking (index concentration) both carry a risk that is systematically underpriced in the AI-era information environment. A pass at the gate is no longer sufficient collateral — it is becoming a device that manufactures a new kind of blind spot.

## What comes next, and what to do with it

If this principle holds, the next one to two years should show three things. First, identity platforms beyond Okta — Microsoft Entra, CrowdStrike, Ping Identity, and others — are likely to acquire or build similar "post-access behavioral monitoring" capabilities of their own; if this becomes an industry-standard feature rather than a one-off deal, the principle is confirmed. Second, the test for financial media and data vendors covering indices and investment statistics is whether "after-the-fact tracking" metrics — top-N contribution to return, for instance — become standard disclosure; if they do, the principle holds, and if headline total returns keep being reported alone, it doesn't. Third, watch whether sourcing demands on primary statistics like corporate software investment start applying to social-media-originated analysis too; if headline numbers keep getting cited widely without a link to a checkable data source, the shift toward after-the-fact tracking is still only half-made.

The practical takeaway for a reader is one habit: when you encounter a number or a guarantee involving AI, stop asking first what was confirmed at the gate, and ask instead who is tracking what happens after, and how. The comfort of a pass at the gate is itself becoming the most underpriced risk in the AI economy.
