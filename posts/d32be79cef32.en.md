What determines whether an agent succeeds is no longer the intelligence of the model or even the correctness of the final answer. The survey paper “From Agent Traces to Trust” (arXiv:2606.04990v4) argues that we should stop measuring reliability by “the correctness of the final answer” and instead re-examine execution through six dimensions. George Sivulka’s a16z essay “You Just Hired a Million Bad Employees” (July 14, 2026) declares that “for the first time in history, humans are cheaper than software,” diagnosing token waste not as a capability problem but as a problem of instruction quality. One is a pre-peer-review technical survey; the other is an investor’s management argument. They appear unrelated. In fact, both can be explained by the same principle.

## The correctness of the final answer is not a proxy for trust

“From Agent Traces to Trust” starts with a blunt observation: “Final answer accuracy evaluates only the endpoint of execution and says nothing about how the output was generated, what evidence supported each claim, whether tool calls were justified, or which step caused failure.” The paper gives this missing piece a name: the **process-level accountability gap**. Even if the answer is correct, if you cannot trace why it was correct, then that correctness can neither be reproduced nor audited. The sharp edge of the paper’s argument is that a right answer is indistinguishable from a lucky guess.

Its proposed remedy is a six-part decomposition of the execution process: (1) trace sources (reasoning, retrieval, tools, memory, environment, inter-agent communication), (2) evidence and execution units, (3) provenance relations, (4) granularity and timing of tracing, (5) representation format, and (6) trust functions.

### Audit becomes possible only when “relations” are typed

The third dimension in particular—provenance relations—is highly concrete. The paper describes links between units using the types Support, Derive, Depend-on, Contradict, Invalidate, Trigger, and Update. Granularity ranges from the run level down to the token level, while timing spans pre-execution, runtime, post hoc, and continuous tracing. The claim is that only when the process is preserved as a “typed graph” does it become possible to attribute failures and trace evidence mechanically.

### The gap shows up as a safety defect

This gap is not an abstraction. The paper points to indirect prompt injection—such as the attacks shown by InjecAgent and AgentDojo, where adversarial instructions are embedded in external documents—and memory poisoning—attacks like AgentPoison, in which malicious records persist across sessions and later manipulate behavior—as threats that are difficult to detect precisely because the process is not traceable. And it reaches a decisive conclusion: “There are no benchmark suites that provide strong end-to-end coverage across evidence labels, tool calls, memory, inter-agent communication, safety attacks, provenance relations, and recovery.” In other words, even the arena for measurement itself has not yet been built.

## Waste comes not from lack of capability, but from the absence of verification

Now shift the lens to management. The central thesis of “You Just Hired a Million Bad Employees” is this: “For the first time in history, humans are cheaper than software.” AI, which was supposed to replace labor, has instead appeared as a waste machine that costs more than humans. Why? Because swarms of agents, trying to compensate for vague human instructions, repeatedly call themselves and fall into loops—“spending tokens to spend tokens.”

What makes Sivulka’s diagnosis sharp is that he does not treat this waste as a capability problem. In his words, “Only maybe 1 in 100 employees can provide context to AI.” That means the bottleneck is not the intelligence of the model but the people responsible for telling either humans or machines what to do, and for measuring whether they did it well.

### Dysfunction is a replay of failure in human organizations

The essay lists seven parallels between agent swarms and human organizations. Token maxing equals brute-force staffing. Loops equal meetings for the sake of meetings. Wasteful token use equals bloated indirect departments, where “80% creates nothing.” 100x more tokens equals the rare leverage of 10x engineers. Hoarding context equals self-preservation through knowledge gatekeeping. In other words, the failure modes of organization theory map directly onto agent operations.

### The prescription is the measurement apparatus itself

The sixth of those seven parallels gets to the heart of the matter: **Eval suites = OKRs**. Sivulka states flatly that “A company’s eval suite will become its most valuable asset.” Why is coding at the frontier of AI deployment? Because “coding has a built-in eval. The code either works or it doesn’t.” The implication is the reverse as well: in business domains that do not have such an obvious built-in verifier, one must be constructed artificially. And that construction work—implementing evals for each company, in other words “AI transformation”—is, he concludes, an even larger next wave of business opportunity than competing directly with existing companies.

## The constraining factor has shifted from the model to “process verifiability”

The technical survey and the management essay differ in vocabulary and audience. One talks about provenance relations like Support / Derive / Invalidate; the other talks about OKRs and headcount. But the absence they identify is the same. **The correctness of the final output is no longer the variable that distinguishes value.** The paper says “final answer accuracy evaluates only the endpoint,” while the essay asks not whether the model can produce an answer but whether it reached that answer without waste. Both explicitly pull evaluation away from the endpoint (final answer / task done) and back toward the process: how the answer was reached.

At this point, one inductive step follows. **Taken together, these facts suggest that the factor constraining the value of agents has shifted from model capability or the correctness of the final answer to whether the execution process can be verified and attributed.** The paper’s six-dimensional trace is the technical substrate for “making the process machine-readable and verifiable,” while the essay’s eval suite is the organizational substrate for “measuring whether the process was good or bad.” The names differ, but both implement the same function—from below and above: the verifiability of the process.

Why does this become the constraint? Model capability is becoming commoditized in the market—the essay’s “software cheaper than humans”—while the correctness of the final answer, without the process behind it, can neither be reproduced nor audited—the paper’s accountability gap. That leaves only one place where differentiation is possible: whether you can mechanically determine that a process was correct, and catch an incorrect process as failure on the spot. In other words, the only real differentiator becomes the presence or absence of a verification apparatus. In a world where capability is abundant, value is no longer determined by capability itself but by the mechanism that evaluates and attributes how that capability is used.

## Those who own the verification infrastructure will win — three falsifiable predictions

If this principle is correct, then the following shifts are highly likely. First, **eval suites and trace infrastructure will become a de facto moat** (high likelihood). The basis for this is the essay’s claim that “A company’s eval suite will become its most valuable asset” and the paper’s conclusion that “There are no benchmark suites” with end-to-end coverage. An asset for which supply is absent and demand is obvious will inevitably be enclosed as a scarce resource. Second, **we will see cases where organizations using the highest-performing models lose in outcomes to organizations using weaker models but able to verify the process** (medium likelihood). Third, the center of value will shift from “those who build models” to “those who evaluate and audit model processes,” and AI transformation businesses will grow, just as the essay predicts, to a scale larger than existing neofirms (medium likelihood).

This principle is falsifiable. If an organization that does no process tracing whatsoever and has no eval suite can nevertheless, simply by deploying the highest-performing model, consistently outperform competitors that have built robust verification infrastructure—then the principle advanced in this article should be rejected. Conversely, if that observation does not materialize, then the real constraint lies not in the model but in process verifiability. The shibboleth is not “Do you have a smart model?” but “Can you verify the process?”

The practical implication for readers is simple. Reallocate investment in agent deployment away from model selection and hit-or-miss final outputs, and toward **systems that preserve the process in machine-readable form and evals that automatically judge whether that process was good or bad**. Use the paper’s six dimensions as a design checklist—what trace sources to retain, at what granularity, and for what trust functions—and apply the essay’s question to every workflow: “What is the eval for this task? Can it be judged, as with code, by whether it works or it doesn’t?” Before you buy a smarter model, acquire a yardstick that can judge the process. That is the only place left to protect value in an era when capability has become cheap.
