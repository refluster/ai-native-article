The cheaper execution gets, the more value escapes upstream—to the design of structure. The claim that the work of writing prompts has been replaced by loop design, Musk’s warning that the biggest mistake smart engineers make is optimizing things that should not exist, and Gartner’s point that AI does not reduce labor costs but reconfigures them. These three ideas may look disconnected, but in fact they can all be explained by the same principle. Namely: the closer the cost of execution gets to zero, the scarcer and more valuable thing is not execution itself, but the design that comes before it—the structure that determines “what, in what order, and where to place it.”

## What “From Prompts to Loops” Reveals About the Shift in the Center of Gravity of Work

L2’s “The Argument for ‘Loop Design’” centers on @0xMortyx’s article “The 5 Levels of Loop Design,” starting from the line: “The person who built Claude Code barely writes prompts anymore; the loops write the prompts. His job is to design the loops.” The core claim is that the primary work of AI development has shifted from writing prompts to designing loops.

There is an important caveat that must be stated plainly here. As the same L2 explicitly notes, what could be verified was limited to the title, opening preview, and post metadata (87.8K Views); because the article itself is behind a login wall, the contents of the five levels could not be consulted as a primary source. So what is certain is not “the definition of the five levels,” but the proposition itself: that the center of gravity of the work has moved from prompt writing to loop design. This essay treats that proposition not as the substance of a maturity model, but as an observation indicating the direction of that shift in gravity.

What this proposition describes is a shift in what human hands are actually working on. Execution—the act of writing prompts one by one—is delegated to loops, while what remains for humans is the design of the loops, that is, the design of the generative structure that produces execution itself. From the person who writes, to the person who builds the mechanism that writes. The center of gravity has been lifted one level up, from “execution” to “structure.”

## How Musk’s “Algorithm” Uses Sequence to Show the Superiority of Deletion

L2’s “Elon Musk’s ‘Algorithm’” deals with the five steps Musk repeats at SpaceX and Tesla: (1) question every requirement, (2) delete parts or processes, (3) simplify or optimize, (4) accelerate cycle time, and (5) automate. And the core of that L2 was the observation that the value lies not in the steps themselves, but in the “order.”

Most people begin with optimization, acceleration, and automation. Musk puts “question” and “delete” first. The reason is captured in his own words: “The most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist.”

Read through the lens of the center of gravity described above, the structure becomes clear. Optimization, acceleration, and automation (steps 3–5) are acts at the execution layer: how to carry out a given task better. By contrast, “question” and “delete” (steps 1–2) are acts at the structural layer: deciding what should be executed in the first place. When Musk says “order determines value,” that is effectively the same as saying: put structural design ahead of execution optimization. To optimize something that should not exist is exactly the kind of failure that inevitably occurs when you jump into the execution layer without first designing the structure.

## What Gartner’s “Reconfiguration” Shows About Where Non-Disappearing Costs Go

The Gartner point discussed in L2’s “AI Does Not ‘Reduce’ Labor Costs, It ‘Reconfigures’ Them” provides a third angle. Gartner rejects the widely shared expectation that AI will reduce labor costs, arguing instead that “AI does not reduce costs; it reconfigures where and how they arise.”

The forms this reconfiguration takes include unplanned costs such as sharply rising compensation for AI talent, rehiring costs associated with AI-driven workforce reductions, and distortions in performance-based compensation models. Gartner concludes that the biggest ROI risk lies not in the cost of AI technology, but in unplanned workforce costs.

Translated into the language of the center of gravity, the point is this: even if you automate execution—the tasks previously done by people—the costs do not disappear. They move to the structural side and reappear in the labor market, organizational design, and pricing models. An undesigned structure calls back costs that were supposed to have been removed. A company that thinks it has saved money at the execution layer ends up receiving an unexpected bill at the structural layer. That is the essence of Gartner’s observation.

## A Common Principle: The Cheaper Execution Gets, the More Expensive Design Becomes

Put the three facts side by side. Loops write prompts. Deletion comes before optimization. Costs do not disappear; they are reconfigured. The contexts are completely different—development work, design process, labor economics—and yet they all point in the same direction. These facts suggest the same asymmetry: as the marginal cost of the execution layer falls, the importance of the structural layer rises.

Let us make the inductive step explicit. The more execution is automated and made free, the less the differentiator is “the ability to execute quickly,” and the more it becomes “the ability to decide what not to execute, in what order, and where to place it.” The important point is that this same directional shift is being observed simultaneously in three independent domains. If it appeared in only one domain, it could be explained as coincidence or fashion. But when the same asymmetry appears in unrelated contexts such as development, design, and labor economics, it is more plausible to see it not as a domain-specific story but as a general dynamic common to systems undergoing automation.

And the three cases even share a common failure mode. When you optimize and automate execution without designing the upstream structure, you end up producing “things that should not exist” at high speed (Musk), while increasing elsewhere the costs that were supposed to disappear (Gartner). Even if you make loops run faster, if you do not design what the loop is for, all you do is become wrong faster (the reverse side of the loop-design proposition). Automation does not correct a bad structure. On the contrary, it amplifies errors and hardens them into place.

From this, let us state one falsifiable claim. In a world where execution costs approach zero, the people who hold leverage will not be “the people who make things faster,” but “the people who decide what not to make.” This is falsifiable—if execution throughput itself remains a decisive source of differentiation, then this claim is wrong.

## Predictions and Implications

If this principle is correct, several changes follow.

First, as a high-probability prediction, the value of jobs will be reassessed from “execution throughput” toward “structural design.” The scarcity premium will rise for people who can make upstream judgments: what requirements to keep or discard, how to sequence processes, how to allocate cost and risk. The basis for this is precisely that the same shift is being observed simultaneously across three domains. In coding, the scarce person will be not the one who writes prompts, but the one who designs loops; in organizations, not the one who carries out tasks, but the one who designs the structure in which costs arise.

Second, with medium probability, the ROI of automation investments will diverge not on the basis of “what was automated,” but “what was deleted before automation.” If Musk’s ordering works as a rule of thumb, then automation that skips the deletion phase will rebound as the kind of unplanned costs Gartner describes. The consistency between these two L2 pieces—one stating it normatively, the other observing it empirically—supports the plausibility of this prediction.

One caveat is necessary here. The details of the five levels of loop design remain unverified, and this essay does not use them as a concrete maturity model. It uses them only as evidence of the “direction” of the shift in the center of gravity. Once the contents of those levels are made public and can be verified, this prediction should be refined.

The practical implication is simple. Try dividing your own work into “the part that is executing” and “the part that is designing the structure.” And before starting any automation or optimization effort, run Musk’s sequence once: should it exist at all? Question that first. Then remove what can be removed. Optimization can come later. In an era when execution is becoming free, what is scarce is not the hand that can build quickly, but the judgment that decides what not to build.
