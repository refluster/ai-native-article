## Executive Summary

On July 4, 2026, to coincide with the 250th anniversary of the United States, Unanimous AI conducted a 20-minute online deliberation asking 277 randomly selected Americans to identify “the top 3 contributions America has made to the world over the past 250 years.” By dividing participants into parallel discussion groups of 4 to 5 people and connecting them in real time through a method called “Thinkscape,” the process converged from 94 initial ideas to a top 10 and ultimately to 3 final answers: 1st = the internet, 2nd = advances in medicine, 3rd = the spread of democracy. At the core of designer Louis Rosenberg’s argument is a single claim: the output is “100% human intelligence,” and AI’s role is not to replace people, but to connect them.

## Experiment Design: 277 People, 20 Minutes, and a Swarm of Agents Linking Parallel Groups

Unanimous AI (founded by Stanford PhD Louis Rosenberg) chose the date of the 250th anniversary to test a single proposition. The question was: “the top 3 contributions America has made to the world over the past 250 years.”

evidence (source facts):

- The participants were **277 randomly selected Americans**.
- The format was a **20-minute online discussion**.
- Participants were divided into **parallel discussion spaces of 4 to 5 people each**.
- Rosenberg’s explanation: “There were 277 people, each of them debating the issues with four or five other people in parallel discussion spaces. The magic is the swarm of AI agents that connect all the small groups together into a single real-time deliberation.”
- The method is called **Thinkscape**, and it enables “massively scalable discussion” through text, audio, and video.
Implication: scale and quality are often treated as a tradeoff. Put everyone in one room and a small number of voices dominate; split them up and you lose convergence across the whole. What this experiment suggests is a design philosophy aimed at bypassing that tradeoff by preserving deliberation in small groups while connecting those groups through agents.

## The Convergence Process: From 94 Ideas to 3

evidence (source facts):

- The discussion first generated **94 distinct ideas**.
- Those were narrowed to a **top 10**, and then further converged into a **top 3**.
- Final results:
- **1st = the internet**. In Rosenberg’s words, “Our collective perspective is that America's greatest contribution to the world over the past 250 years is the internet.” It was evaluated as having emerged from U.S. academic and government research and having “transformed communication, democratized information and education, enabled commerce, medicine, research and cultural exchange.”
- **2nd = Advances in medicine**. Vaccines and treatments originating in the United States were said to have “saved and prolonged hundreds of millions of lives worldwide.”
- **3rd = Spreading democracy**. The Constitution was described as “a vital blueprint for representative government, inspiring democratic movements and revolutions worldwide.”
Implication: what matters here is not just the result itself, but the fact that the path of convergence — 94 → 10 → 3 — remains as a recorded trace. A simple vote or survey leaves only a distribution of preferences; this method makes visible the deliberative process itself: which raw ideas were eliminated at what stage, and what they were ultimately condensed into. It turns the output of consensus formation into an artifact backed by a verifiable history.

## The Central Claim: AI “Connects” Rather Than “Replaces”

The core argument behind this method, which Rosenberg presents as a new technological category called “hyper-communication,” is a clear counterpoint to the dominant current narrative that generative AI substitutes for human thought.

evidence (source facts):

- “the role of the AI agents in a hyper-communication system is to connect people, not replace them.”
- The agents enable “scalable human deliberation,” in which “every participant is given optimized ability to express their views, respond to others, and converge on solutions based on their merits.”
- Closing line: “It's important to remember, this is 100% human intelligence — a pure reflection of the collective knowledge, wisdom, and values of 277 randomly selected Americans.”
- Two years earlier, Rosenberg also published an essay titled “Collective Superintelligence,” and this experiment belongs to that same lineage.
Implication: designing AI around “augmenting the throughput of human groups” rather than “replacing humans” changes the criteria by which agent technologies are evaluated. Here, the agents are not the subject that produces the answer; they are the infrastructure that expands the bandwidth between people. The legitimacy of the output depends not on model performance but on the representativeness of the participants — 277 randomly selected Americans. In other words, the basis of trust shifts from algorithmic accuracy to sample design.

## What This Experiment Is Actually Measuring

The article takes up a symbolic Fourth of July question — ranking America’s contributions — but what is really being tested is not the ranking itself. It is the viability of a method: how far AI agents can extend human collective intelligence as mediators.

- What is being tested is the **method** (Thinkscape / hyper-communication), not the conclusion (that the internet came in 1st).
- The persuasiveness of this approach depends not on whether the AI is smart, but on how well a sample of 277 represents the broader population.
- Rosenberg repeatedly stresses “100% human intelligence” because he is grounding the authority of the output not in the model, but in human deliberation.
Strip away the stagecraft of the 250th anniversary, and one practical question remains: can AI agents scale human collective judgment in a measurable way without being asked to stand in for human judgment? This experiment offers one implementation-level answer to that question.
