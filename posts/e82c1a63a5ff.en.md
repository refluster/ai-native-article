AI investment lands in the economic statistics the moment it is spent; it starts running as capability years later. What sets the length of that gap is not model progress but the throughput to verify and integrate.

Three pieces of material in front of me appear to be about different things. A market statistic: private capital expenditure has reached a $1.5 trillion annualized rate. A practitioner's claim: the rate limiter on physical AI is not model intelligence but engineering-system throughput. And a macro analysis: AI investment is inflationary first and disinflationary later. In fact one principle explains all three. They are not three phenomena. They are one lag, observed from its entrance, its cause, and its exit.

## Only the spending side is being measured

The figures in "AI-related private capital expenditure hits a $1.5 trillion annual rate — what the claim that it accounts for 25–33% of US GDP growth actually contains" are not in doubt as to scale. Private-sector capital expenditure in AI-related categories rose $300 billion year over year in the second quarter of 2026 — a 25% increase — reaching a record $1.5 trillion annualized. "Computers and peripherals" led, followed by communications equipment, software, and data centers. The same source states that direct AI investment accounts for 25% to 33% of recent US GDP growth.

What deserves attention is the nature of the line items in that composition. Computers, peripherals, communications equipment, data centers — every one of them is a purchase. National accounts book capital expenditure at the moment of expenditure. Not on the day the server is racked, and not on the day something begins running on it, but at the point an order becomes a payment.

So the sentence "AI accounts for 25–33% of GDP growth" is a sentence about the input side. It says nothing about whether the purchased capability is running. This distinction is not wordplay: the two pieces of material below both stand on it. As that L2 itself sets out, the figures originate in a market-commentary post rather than a statistical agency, and the definitions of the line items hold only inside that post's own frame. The order of magnitude is not in question, but this is not a number whose precision should rest on a single source.

## The running side is bound by the smaller factor in a product

"The next AI moat isn't a better model: the real bottleneck in physical AI, per Applied Intuition's CTO" formalizes that running side directly. According to Peter Ludwig, co-founder and CTO of Applied Intuition, deployed physical AI is the product of two variables. The first is model capability. The second is the engineering-system throughput surrounding the model — the capacity to verify that intelligence, integrate it, and deploy it.

Ludwig's point is that the industry pours everything into the first variable while the second sits at a decade-old level, premised on quarterly releases and hundred-person integration teams.

That it is a product is decisive. In a sum, raising one term raises the total. In a product, the smaller factor governs the whole. If the second variable is pinned at a ten-year-old level, additional investment in the first converts into almost no running capability. It becomes inventory, not output. Recall that the $1.5 trillion composition from the previous section consists almost entirely of first-variable purchases.

### The weight carried by a single number: 20×

The only quantitative support for this claim is a measured result: on Dana, the agent platform the company built for itself, development cycles ran roughly 20 times faster. The evidentiary limits should be stated plainly. It is one company's internal figure, self-reported, benchmarked against that same company's earlier process.

But the load-bearing claim for this analysis is not the multiple. It is that the second variable is movable — that it is an investable target rather than a fixed environmental condition. If 20× were 5×, the implication would not change.

## The two price phases are the accounting signature of that lag

In "AI investment has become a macro cycle — $720 billion of hyperscaler capex in 2026, and the order in which it pushes inflation first," LSEG macro analyst Erwan Jacob argues that AI has already become a macroeconomic force rather than a technology theme. The top five US hyperscalers are on track for roughly $720 billion of 2026 capital expenditure, and it is already moving the prices of semiconductors, power, and imported goods.

The most important element of Jacob's analysis concerns order, not level. AI's effect on prices is sequential rather than simultaneous: inflationary in the investment phase, disinflationary in the diffusion phase. That is precisely what forces hard judgment on central banks.

Lay that sequence over the previous two sections. The inflationary investment phase is the phase in which the first variable is being bought; semiconductor, power, and imported-goods prices move because those purchases demand them. The disinflationary diffusion phase is the phase in which the second variable finally begins delivering — capability converts into operation and pushes unit costs down.

And the void between the two is exactly the bottleneck Ludwig named. Jacob's "sequential" is the signature that slow integration throughput leaves in price data. The separation of phases he observes does not exist independently as a macro phenomenon; it is the price-level expression of an engineering lag lying between purchase and operation.

## The unifying principle — inputs book instantly, outputs arrive only at the speed of throughput

These facts suggest a single principle. **AI investment is booked instantly at the moment of expenditure, while AI capability reaches operation only at the speed of verification and integration throughput. The "AI economy" now being measured is therefore a measurement of the input side, and what sets the timing of the output side is not model progress but integration throughput.**

Why should this hold? Economic statistics record transactions: once an expenditure occurs, it books that day. Deployment, by contrast, exists only after passing through verification and integration — a process rate-limited, in Ludwig's description, by quarterly releases and hundred-person teams. The former moves at transaction speed, the latter at process speed. Two different speeds make the two structurally asynchronous. This is not a measurement error. It is the accounting working as designed.

Standing on that principle changes the relationship between the three pieces of material. They are not three topics but three observation points on one lag. The Kobeissi Letter's $1.5 trillion measures the lag's entrance. Ludwig's two-variable model names the variable that sets its length. Jacob's two phases put a price on both of its ends.

The implication that carries the most consequence is this: the arrival date of the disinflationary phase is a function of something that appears as a variable in no macro forecast. Integration throughput does not exist in a central bank's observation system. It is nonetheless advancing the clock on the diffusion phase. Central banks are currently forecasting the timing of a disinflation whose clock is being kept inside engineering organizations.

The principle is falsifiable. If integration throughput stays at the level Ludwig describes — quarterly releases, hundred-person integration teams — and the diffusion phase's disinflation still arrives on the schedule macro analysts assume, then something else was setting the lag and this principle is wrong.

## Forecasts and implications

First, something predictable with high confidence: the divergence between AI's measured contribution to GDP and observable productivity output will widen before it narrows. The grounds are two structural facts — that in a product the smaller factor governs, and that the $1.5 trillion composition is overwhelmingly first-variable purchases. Purchasing continues; integration does not keep pace; the gap comes first.

Second, something that can be said with moderate confidence: the highest marginal return on AI investment shifts from buying capability to integration throughput — tooling, verification, deployment frequency. The grounds are Dana's measured 20×, but since that is one company's self-report, this is a read on direction rather than a calibrated forecast. The moderate confidence reflects the thinness of that evidence.

Third, the error in macro forecasts will concentrate in timing rather than direction. The grounds are Jacob's own sequencing claim: the order itself — inflation then disinflation — is the conclusion of his analysis, so what is contested is not whether the order is right but when the switch comes. And as shown above, the variable that sets that timing sits outside the forecasting system.

There are two practical implications for a reader. One concerns how to read the statistics: when you see "AI accounts for X% of GDP growth," ask which variable was measured. In most cases the answer is the first variable, and the sentence is not evidence that capability is running.

The other concerns how to frame the question inside your own organization. The question that matters is not which model you adopted. It is how many days pass between a working model and a deployed one. That number sets when spending turns into output. The remaining room to move is on the side of that number, not on the side of the $1.5 trillion.
