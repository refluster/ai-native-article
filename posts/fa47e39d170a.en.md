## Executive Summary

According to a16z's "Charts of the Week" newsletter (Moses Sternstein, September 18, 2026), AI code-generation tools have driven monthly new-app counts across iOS, Android and Chrome to "variously double or quadruple," while downloads (or ratings, for iOS) have stayed "pretty much stagnant" — and the share of apps showing any sign of traction has actually "plummeted." SensorTower data corroborates the picture: total US app revenue is up only about 2% over the past year and a half, and the fastest-growing category by usage time is Productivity, driven by ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok — not apps built by AI, but apps that are AI. The issue's central observation: AI has multiplied the number of apps, but the apps making money are the AI products themselves, not the AI-generated ones.

## Supply Has Exploded; Demand Hasn't Followed

A recent paper cited in the issue finds that across iOS, Android and Chrome, the number of new apps published per month has "variously doubled or quadrupled." Everyone is a vibecoder now, which means everyone is an app publisher now, too.

But more supply hasn't translated into more demand. The data is described as "pretty clear":

- Downloads (or ratings, for iOS) have been "pretty much stagnant."
- The share of apps showing any sign of "escape velocity" — 10+ ratings, 100+ downloads for Android, and/or 10+ downloads for Chrome — has "plummeted."
- SensorTower's estimates put US app revenue roughly flat since the start of last year: about a 2% increase in revenue and a 7% increase in usage time over the past year and a half.
Sternstein sums this up as "App-Slop" being the story for now: AI has produced a lot more apps, but not more successful ones in any meaningful sense. He notes this isn't wholly surprising — building successful software has always required more than generating code, and it's early enough that amateur tinkering would be expected to outpace real demand.

## The Growth Is in Apps That Are AI, Not Apps Made by AI

The exception in SensorTower's category breakdown is generative-AI chat products themselves. Productivity is the fastest-growing category by time spent, driven by ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok, and the second-fastest by revenue growth after the comparatively tiny Food & Drink category. Dev Tools, also a small category, is another standout, with Replit as its clear growth leader.

In other words: AI apps are plainly popular; apps made by AI are not, at least not yet. Sternstein frames this as a footnote to the broader question of when AI adoption shows up in GDP — his answer being "when the apps get much better." Consumer Edge data backs the "still early" read: paid consumer AI adoption was only around 3% as of Q1, with younger users adopting AI at roughly 4x the rate of older ones.

## A New, Younger, Faster-Growing Breed of Unicorn

Beyond the app economy, AI is visibly reshaping the venture landscape. SVB data shows that while the overall population of $1B+ venture-backed companies has aged roughly 7% on average, the median age of newly minted unicorns has fallen to just over four years — a 37% decline since 2023.

Growth curves have shifted too. For the 2022 cohort, the jump in median revenue between year 3 and year 4 is roughly 3x larger than for earlier cohorts: pre-GPT cohorts typically moved from about $2M to $3M, while the 2022 batch moved from $2.8M to $5.6M. Sternstein is careful to hedge here — whether this is genuinely AI-driven remains to be confirmed by the 2023 and 2024 cohorts, and part of the 2022 cohort's growth may reflect greater VC allocation to non-software businesses or fast-growing "AI wrapper" companies whose durability is unproven.

## SaaS Stocks Have Found a Floor, But the Old Normal Isn't Back

Rising interest rates combined with an AI-driven shift in VC strategy pushed tech companies across SVB's tracked verticals to trade growth for profitability starting in Q1 2022: median growth rates fell from a 40–70% range to 15–30%, while margins moved from deeply unprofitable toward roughly breakeven. Companies funded most recently, by contrast, show growth/profitability profiles closer to the 2022 standard.

The flip side is a historic wave of both business formation and business closures, with the largest share of shutdowns coming from companies founded at the peak of the zero-interest-rate (ZIRP) era. SimpleClosure data shows AI startups make up a shrinking share of shutdowns, while B2B SaaS accounts for more than a quarter of shutdowns in H1 2026.

On the public-market side, Sternstein credits Citrini's earlier bearish "doompost" about software with roughly calling the bottom of the sector's selloff: performance has climbed, if unevenly, since April. Fundamentals have largely held up too — software companies have generally met or beaten expectations through the first half of the year, even as the share issuing disappointing guidance ticked up in the most recent quarter. Sternstein cautions the data isn't comprehensive, but concludes that whatever "SaaSpocalypse" has been feared, it hasn't arrived yet.
