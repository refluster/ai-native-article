## Executive Summary

In the AI era, enterprise software competition is increasingly moving toward “who owns company-specific context,” and Salesforce, Microsoft, ServiceNow, Databricks, and others have begun building their own context layers. The original source for this article (Ryohei Yamazaki / Delta X Fund) identifies Palantir’s key differentiator as the fact that it started not from existing applications but from a “world model that represents the enterprise itself,” and uses the Airbus A350 case—where deliveries were accelerated by 33%—to break down how that structure works.

## A Different Starting Point: Others Start from “Apps,” Palantir Starts from the “World”

The first argument laid out in the original source is that the path each company took toward something ontology-like is fundamentally different.

- Other companies start from existing business applications. Salesforce from CRM, SAP from ERP, Microsoft from Office and email, ServiceNow from ITSM / workflow management, Databricks from the data platform—each has built its context layer as an extension of its flagship products.
- Palantir reversed that order, starting from the question of “how to represent the enterprise as a whole” and building its ontology as a unified context model from the outset.
According to the original source, this difference in starting point is what led to a design that includes “not only nouns, but also verbs, state transitions, and write-back.” It argues that the essence of Palantir is better understood not as a “data integration company” or an “AI company,” but as a “digital twin of the enterprise = a world model of the enterprise” built over more than 20 years. In this structure, Foundry integrates data, the ontology defines enterprise context, and AIP (AI Platform) puts AI to work on top of it.

## The Ontology’s Three-Layer Structure: Nouns, Verbs, and Dynamic Behavior

The original source relies on Palantir’s own framing—“nouns” and “verbs”—and organizes the ontology as follows.

- **Layer 1: Semantic Layer / Nouns** — “What exists.” This consists of three elements: entities (Customer, Aircraft, Contract), relationships, and attributes, and it reconciles scattered labels such as “user / client / individual” into a single Customer entity. BI tools’ semantic layers generally operate up to this point.
- **Layer 2: Kinetic / Operational Layer / Verbs** — “What can be done.” This models business actions such as Renew, Approve, Assign, and Schedule Maintenance as part of the ontology itself.
- **Dynamic** — Palantir’s true point of differentiation. Not a new layer, but a property given to the semantic and operational layers. For example, once you define a state transition such as `Prospect → Active → Renewal → Churn`, along with the actions allowed in each state, execution permissions, and transition conditions (policies), the ontology becomes “dynamic.”
In the original source’s formulation, Palantir defines the ontology as an “operational layer” for the organization, positioned as an intermediate layer between the data platform and business execution. And it argues that the first version of that middle layer must be built by FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) embedded at the customer’s actual site—whether that is a factory final assembly line, a military base, or a financial institution.

## Airbus A350: Turning 5 Million Parts into “One Aircraft” and Accelerating Deliveries by 33%

The main example used in the original source to show the effect of a dynamic ontology is the Airbus A350. The facts presented are as follows.

- The A350 consists of roughly 5 million parts and was manufactured across hundreds of teams, 4 countries, and more than 8 factories. Previously, delays in parts and quality data were scattered across systems, leaving the organization in a state where “no one could grasp the full picture until the aircraft was completed.”
- The partnership between Palantir and Airbus began around the end of 2015 with the goal of ramping up A350 production, and in 2017 it was expanded into the industry platform Skywise.
- By integrating previously fragmented data into Foundry’s ontology and linking dependencies between parts and schedules, Airbus was able to identify and prioritize bottlenecks; according to official materials, this **accelerated A350 deliveries by 33%**.
- Skywise handles up to 20,000 sensors per aircraft (about 1 million data points per flight), with more than 100 airlines participating. Third-party estimates suggest it could generate an annual revenue opportunity of more than $850 million, and Palantir releases state that it connects to about 50% of the Airbus fleet in operation worldwide (all figures as of the time they were published).
- easyJet has introduced the predictive maintenance service Skywise Fleet Performance+, with the aim of preventing delays and cancellations by intervening before parts fail.
The original source is careful about causality. The 33% improvement, it argues, was not the result of “AI casting a magic spell,” but the logical consequence of: (1) integrating fragmented data according to the structure of reality, (2) explicitly linking dependencies, (3) making bottlenecks visible, and (4) speeding up decision-making.

## How the Dynamic Part Is Implemented: Write-Back and the Action Log

As the decisive distinction from dashboard-style analytics platforms, the original source points to two mechanisms.

- **Write-back** — By setting a webhook on an action, workflows inside Foundry can connect directly to source systems, allowing data and decisions to be actually reflected in operational systems. The article quotes Palantir’s conclusion: “Unless decisions taken are synchronized with operational systems, everything has only limited value.”
- **Action Log** — A mechanism for recording in structured form “when, who, and what was decided.” It captures not only before-and-after values, but also other objects edited at the same time and the state of the world at the moment of execution. An action that “closes” 10 alerts at once generates a single log object linked by foreign keys to those 10 alerts.
The original source groups these together under Palantir’s “four-fold” model—data, logic, action, and security. On security in particular, it stresses that AI agents must inherit their security scope from the permission structures of human users and projects, and presents this as a “prerequisite for production deployment.”

## Implications for AI Product Design

The original source extracts four design principles at the end.

- Start the design from “decision-making,” not “data integration.”
- Include verbs (actions), not just nouns, in the design—only when nouns and verbs are combined do you get a “sentence” = a decision.
- Don’t stop at “read only”; connect execution and record-keeping into a single flow through write-back and the Action Log.
- Make AI agents inherit the same permissions and governance as humans.
At the same time, the original source makes a practical caution explicit. As critics point out, building and maintaining an ontology that accurately represents the organization as a whole is extremely complex and requires advanced expertise and continuous updating—Palantir’s extensive consulting involvement is the flip side of that construction cost. In conclusion, the article argues that competitive advantage in AI products will be determined less and less by “how smart the model is” alone, and more by the depth of context: “how faithfully, dynamically, and in sync with reality you can model your own operational world.”
