## Executive Summary

In an interview with CNBC, Palantir CEO Alex Karp sharply criticized frontier AI labs such as Anthropic and OpenAI, saying they “don’t understand how unlikeable they are.” At the core of his critique is the idea that while the labs are focused on competing over model performance, they are neglecting the layer of “implementation” where enterprises actually realize value. Gartner estimates that only 28% of AI use cases have fully met ROI expectations, and moves such as OpenAI’s acquisition of consulting firm Tomoro to build a deployment support unit suggest the labs themselves are beginning to acknowledge that gap.

## What Karp Said

According to The Register (June 11, 2026, reported by Brandon Vigliarolo), Karp argued in an interview with CNBC’s Sara Eisen that dissatisfaction with frontier AI labs is spreading across both society and the business world. The key points of his remarks were as follows.

- On the labs’ attitude: “They believe all problems present, past, and future...are going to be solved by them. Enterprises are fed up because they know this doesn't actually work this way”
- On the spread of dissatisfaction: “It's not just the man and woman on the street who are unhappy with the frontier labs...every single enterprise we deal with being frustrated”
- On how enterprises react: “People come out of there screaming, saying 'this could never work for me, they don't understand the enterprise'”
- On the San Francisco mindset: “their basic vibe is 'we don't have to solve your problem today because tomorrow you're going to go away'”. Karp dismissed this outright, saying, “It's a complete farce. They don't understand how unlikeable they are.”
According to the article, Karp argues that the labs are prioritizing “tokenmaxxing” — maximizing token consumption — while failing to understand real enterprise demand. The frontier labs named were Anthropic and OpenAI, and Google was also mentioned.

## The Numbers Back the Adoption Barrier

Karp’s remarks cannot be dismissed as mere positioning, because third-party data points to the same fault line.

- Gartner estimates that only **28%** of AI use cases have fully met expectations for ROI (return on investment).
- The article notes that many AI projects fail without ever making it beyond the pilot stage.
In other words, the view from the adoption side — “the models are powerful, but integrating them into enterprise operations and capturing value from them is the hard part” — has quantitative support.

## Even the Labs Are Starting to Move Toward the “Implementation Layer”

Ironically, the very gap Karp criticized is now being addressed by the labs he criticized.

- OpenAI acquired the UK AI consulting firm **Tomoro** and created a deployment support organization called **OpenAI Deployment Company**.
- That move can be read as a de facto admission by the labs that providing models alone is not enough to reach enterprise value.
Karp himself also said, “It is not that LLMs aren't crucial for the world, it's just that the implementation is where the value is,” making clear that he is not denying the importance of the models themselves. The dispute is over one point: whether value resides in the model layer or the implementation layer.

## Assessment — The Interests Behind the Remarks, and the Issue That Still Remains

Up to this point, these are the reported facts. What follows is this article’s assessment.

Karp’s criticism is clearly tied to his own interests. Palantir is precisely a company that sells the “implementation layer” — the integration of enterprise data foundations and AI — and the worse the reputation of frontier labs becomes, the more Palantir’s relative value rises. His sweeping claim that “every single enterprise we deal with” is frustrated should also be discounted to the extent that it reflects observations drawn from the company’s own customer sample.

Even so, two issues remain. First, Gartner’s 28% figure and the high failure rate of projects that stall at the pilot stage exist independently of Karp’s incentives. Second, OpenAI’s acquisition of Tomoro is a case in which the lab’s own capital allocation affirms the proposition that “implementation is where the value is.” While the race over model performance continues, the main battleground of competition is beginning to slide toward the layers of deployment and operations — that is the structural shift this article points to, and for companies that stand not on the side of “building” AI but on the side of “embedding” it, it is also a signal that the balance of power with frontier labs is becoming more negotiable.
