The yardstick for evaluating AI has quietly shifted from technical superiority to strategic calculation by the state—that is the real turning point that arrived in 2026. The Kobeissi Letter forecasts that AI capital expenditures by the five biggest players will exceed defense spending in 2027; an RBC survey found that 100% of CIOs have budgeted for AI; and the Trump administration put the brakes on the public release of GPT-5.6. Capital expenditure, corporate budgets, government regulation—these three developments may look disconnected, but they can in fact be explained by the same principle. AI is no longer a mere product. It is being reclassified into a “strategic asset” that states, companies, and governments are each repricing according to their own yardsticks.

## Capital Spending Has Started Being Compared to “Defense Spending”

According to “AI Capital Expenditures to Reach 3.2% of US GDP by 2027,” AI CapEx by Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle will exceed $800 billion in 2026 and expand to a record $1.1 trillion in 2027, reaching about 3.2% of US GDP. The forecast says this will exceed expected defense spending of about 2.7% in the same year for the first time in history.

### The Yardstick Has Changed from “Corporate R&D” to “Share of GDP”

What matters here is not the amount itself, but the unit of comparison. Traditionally, tech-company investment was discussed in terms of R&D budgets or as a share of revenue. Here, however, the scale of investment is measured as a share of GDP and placed alongside defense spending. As the article puts it, “the core point of this post is that the scale of capital expenditure has begun to be discussed not as the budget of a single industry, but as a component of the macroeconomy.” AI investment is no longer just a corporate management decision; it has become something evaluated using the yardsticks of national finance. The people doing the measuring have changed—from management analysts to macroeconomic analysts. That is the first sign.

## On Corporate Ledgers, It Has Become a “Fixed Cost”

“RBC’s CIO Survey Shows the Shift in Enterprise AI Spending from ‘Proof of Concept to Production’” reports on a semiannual spending survey of more than 100 CIOs and technology leaders. More than half of respondents said “AI is already in production,” and 35% expect to move into production within six months. And 100% of respondents had allocated budget for AI/LLMs, with 91% creating entirely new budget lines. As for token budgets, 90% said they were “manageable.” By vendor, ChatGPT was the most frequently used at 57%, far ahead of Claude (12%).

### “From Proof of Concept to Production” Means “From Discretionary Spending to Fixed Cost”

What these figures mean is that AI has shifted from experimental discretionary spending to a standard cost that cannot be avoided, like electricity or communications lines. The 100% budget-allocation rate and the 91% creation of new budget lines show that AI is no longer something companies are deciding whether to try; it has become a standard budget item. The fact that 90% describe token budgets as “manageable” carries the same implication. It is no longer being treated as an uncontrollable variable cost to be feared, but as a recurring cost that can be budgeted in advance. Here too, the people doing the measuring have changed—from early adopters reacting to technological novelty to finance and IT departments, the buyers, quietly stacking fixed costs.

## The State Has Moved to Stop “Release Itself”

What “Trump Administration Asks OpenAI for a Phased Release of GPT-5.6” reports is this: the administration asked OpenAI to proceed with the public release of its next model, GPT-5.6, in stages, limiting access to government-approved partners. The article positions this as “the first case in which the US government sought prior restraint on the release of a model by a domestic AI company,” and argues that national-security concerns have begun intervening in release decisions themselves, using “models above a certain capability threshold” as the trigger.

### This Is Not a “Product Launch” but “Export Control for Strategic Goods”

For ordinary consumer goods, governments do not intervene in whether and when a product can be launched. But here, the state is using model capability as a threshold to decide “who gets to use it.” That is not the logic of a new product launch; it is the same logic used in export controls for weapons and advanced semiconductors. It is also significant that the trigger for intervention is set at “a certain capability threshold,” a technical metric. AI has begun to be treated as a strategic good with military implications while still being a civilian product—a dual-use technology. The people doing the measuring have changed again—from the companies developing the models to the national-security authorities.

## The Common Principle: The Sign of Reclassification Is a Change in “Who Measures It, and by What Yardstick”

These three facts suggest that the axis on which AI is being evaluated has shifted from “technical superiority” to “scarcity and controllability as a strategic resource.” Put differently, AI has left the category of “technology product” and been reclassified into the category of “strategic infrastructure/strategic asset.”

The sign that this reclassification has occurred is neither a new performance benchmark nor another digit in the investment total. It is a change in “who measures it, and by what yardstick.” Set these three articles side by side and the measuring actors and their yardsticks line up cleanly. In the capital-spending article, the ones measuring are macroeconomic analysts, and the yardsticks are share of GDP and defense spending—that is, the framework of national finance. In the CIO survey article, the ones measuring are enterprise buyers, and the yardsticks are fixed costs and standard costs—the framework of management infrastructure. In the model-release article, the ones measuring are national-security authorities, and the yardsticks are capability thresholds and export control—the framework of strategic goods.

What all three have in common is that in every case, “third parties who are not the makers” have begun assigning value to AI using yardsticks imported from domains other than technology. The universal sign that a technology is becoming infrastructure is not how much smarter it has become, but that people who do not make it—fiscal authorities, executives, national-security officials—begin applying their own measures to it. Electricity, railroads, and telecommunications all followed the same path from “invention” to “social infrastructure.” AI is now standing at the entrance to that phase transition. That is why the true turning point was not the day a model surpassed humans, but the day its makers could no longer decide unilaterally what AI should cost and whether it could be made available at all.

## Forecasts and Implications: The Yardstick of a “Useful Tool” Is Already Obsolete

First, model release will shift from “launch” to “licensing/approval” (high probability). The basis for this is that the first intervention reported in “Trump Administration Asks OpenAI for” rests on a logic—“a certain capability threshold”—that is easy to turn into precedent. Threshold-based rules are easy for other companies and other countries to imitate, and once a regulatory template is established, it spreads. The practical implication for developers is clear: if they do not build regulatory lead times into their model roadmaps, they may end up with models that are finished but cannot be released.

Second, AI budgets will be institutionalized as “fixed costs that cannot be cut” (high probability). The basis for this is the RBC survey’s figures: 100% budget allocation, 91% creation of new budget lines, and 90% saying token budgets are “manageable.” Once an accounting item is this standardized, it is not the first thing to be cut even in a downturn. But the oligopolistic lead of ChatGPT at 57% creates another risk: the ossification of fixed costs—vendor lock-in. The practical implication for buyers is that now, before this spending fully hardens into recurring cost, is the time to preserve room for multi-vendor procurement. Paying switching costs after it becomes a fixed cost will be expensive.

Third, as CapEx becomes a macro indicator, AI investment will become one driver of the business cycle (medium probability). The basis for this is the scale shown in the capital-spending article: $1.1 trillion, or 3.2% of GDP. That is no longer a variable at the level of a single company; it is a variable for the economy as a whole. The implication is that reversal is also possible. The biggest risk is not AI performance but whether these enormous investments can be recovered—that is, the bubble question. Once the yardstick has become national in scale, any slowdown will also be national in scale.

And this reclassification is not irreversible. That gives us a testable bet. If the $1.1 trillion in CapEx fails to generate returns and companies revert to treating AI budgets—supposedly “manageable”—as the first thing to cut, then AI will slide back from the category of “strategic asset” to that of “product.” Whether the reclassification is real can be tested in the next recession: if AI budgets are the first to be cut, then it is still a product; if they are not, then it has become infrastructure. Either way, any organization still procuring AI as a mere “useful tool” is already using an outdated yardstick. The next move is to redesign budgeting, procurement, and regulatory response as the management of a strategic asset.
