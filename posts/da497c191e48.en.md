What determines whether AI is real is not the narrative of expectation, pessimism, or gut feel, but measurable substrata such as electricity consumption, stock-market selection, and usage logs. A power forecast that data centers will reach 194 gigawatts in 2035, software stocks sold off “selectively” not because of deteriorating performance but because of doubts about long-term sustainability, and the reality that felt AI usage consistently exceeds observed usage—these three things may look unrelated, but in fact they can be explained by the same principle. In every case, the narrative had drifted away from reality, and the truth lay on the side of the measured substrate.

## Electricity: the constraint nobody was talking about

The Kobeissi Letter’s analysis, “Data center power demand to hit 194 gigawatts by 2035,” argues that U.S. data center electricity demand will rise 253% from 2026 levels and reach a record 194 gigawatts in 2035. Data centers alone would account for roughly 20% of U.S. electricity consumption, and the load would be concentrated in just a handful of grid regions, including PJM.

The report’s conclusion can be stated in a single line: AI’s next constraint is not model intelligence, but electricity. The key point here is that the constraint has shifted from the dimension people had been talking about to one they had not. Engineers and investors alike have discussed AI’s limits in terms of how smart the models are. But what actually constrains the system is a measurable physical quantity: gigawatts. Intelligence is easy to turn into narrative; electricity is not. That is exactly why electricity was overlooked—and became the real ceiling.

The fact that the load will be concentrated in only a handful of grid regions, including PJM, reinforces the structure of that blind spot. People talking about AI have focused on “what the model can do,” while geographically grounded, measurable units like transmission grids have remained outside the narrative. The 253% growth rate and the 20% share of electricity consumption are not the heat of a story; they are figures etched by instruments. No matter how fast the story of intelligence accelerates, those numbers do not loosen.

## The market measures sustainability, not mood

a16z’s “Charts of the Week” frames the decline in public software stocks from a different angle. The selloff, it argues, was not about deteriorating business performance but about “market skepticism around long-term durability,” and it was not an indiscriminate collapse. It was a move in which stocks were clearly sorted based on whether they had AI defensibility.

### The narrative of a crash, the measurement of selection

Against the pessimistic narrative that “software is dead,” the prices the market actually assigned were anything but indiscriminate. This was selective repricing, distinguishing between companies with defensibility and those without. The market, as an instrument of measurement, was not gauging mood; it was gauging sustainability. The narrative of a crash suggests a monolithic decline, but the measured price action instead recorded the fine-grained nature of selection. Here too, the coarseness of narrative clashes with the resolution of measurement.

### Three counterpoints that challenge the pessimists

a16z also presents three data-based rebuttals to the pessimistic case. First, a Jevons-style dynamic in which the collapse in the cost of intelligence actually boosts demand. Second, resilient employment data in AI-related jobs. Third, an inverse correlation between data centers and electricity prices. All three directly contradict the narrative that “AI erodes jobs and the economy.” The inverse correlation in electricity prices is especially notable because it connects, beneath the surface, to the power constraint described in the previous section—the same physical quantity in the substrate reappears here from another angle.

## Felt experience consistently exceeds observation

The June 2026 edition of the Anthropic Economic Index, “Cadences,” measures this divergence most precisely. By linking Claude usage logs to self-reported surveys from approximately 9,700 people, it shows that AI usage has a structured “schedule,” patterned by time of day, day of week, and occupation.

### The automation paradox

Its biggest finding is the automation paradox. The more a user delegated tasks to AI, the less pessimistic they were about AI’s impact on their job the following year—in fact, they tended to be more optimistic. Those who actually put their hands on the work and delegated it—those who took actions that could be measured—were the ones farthest from the narrative of fear.

### The gap between felt experience and observation

And there is one decisive line in the report. Self-reported perceptions of AI capability consistently exceed the “observed exposure” captured in usage logs. The AI people talk about is always larger than the AI their logs record. Once again, narrative was skating above reality.

## The common principle: truth sinks into the measured layer, while narrative skims across the top

Put these three facts side by side, and a single principle emerges. The truth about AI does not reside in the layer where people talk—intelligence, mood, gut feel—but in the measurable substrate beneath it: electricity consumption, selective stock pricing, and usage logs. And narrative systematically drifts away from that substrate.

That drift has a direction. In flashy, attention-grabbing dimensions—AI capability, the sense of impending collapse—narrative exceeds measurement. a16z’s narrative of a “crash” exceeded the underlying selective data, and Anthropic’s perceived capability exceeded observed exposure. In mundane, less visible dimensions—the physical constraints that actually govern the system—narrative falls short of measurement. The electricity in Kobeissi’s analysis was the ceiling left out by narratives focused on intelligence.

Taken together, these facts suggest the following: overestimation and underestimation of AI are two sides of the same coin, and both occur when measurement is replaced by narrative. People over-narrate the dramatic dimensions and fail to narrate the boring ones. That is why bubbles of expectation and the overlooking of boring constraints can happen at the same time. These three L2s come from different domains and different sources, but Kobeissi’s electricity, a16z’s stock prices and employment, and Anthropic’s usage logs all point to the same directional drift. A drift that reproduces across domains is not chance; it is structure. There is only one way to get at the truth: doubt the narrative and read the measured substrate. Just as the market measured sustainability through selection, and just as Anthropic checked felt experience against exposure.

## Forecasts and implications: read the direction of the divergence

If this principle is right, several consequences follow with fairly high confidence.

First, AI’s near-term constraint is more likely to emerge not as a limit of intelligence but in the physical substrate, especially electricity (basis: Kobeissi’s 194 gigawatts and the concentrated load amounting to roughly 20% of electricity consumption). As models get smarter, the ceiling shifts from computation to power delivery. The focus of investment and policy will move from competing over intelligence to competing over the grid.

Second, pessimism about markets and labor is likely to keep being partially rebutted by real data (basis: a16z’s three counterpoints on employment, demand, and electricity prices). That does not mean “AI is harmless.” What the selective repricing shows is the cold fact that those without defensibility will be culled. Optimism and culling can coexist.

Third, the structure in which felt experience exceeds observation, as Anthropic shows, is likely to continue for the time being. People will keep talking about the AI they use as larger than what their logs record. And what the automation paradox shows—that users who took the measurable action of delegation were more optimistic—is that fear resides on the side of felt experience, while reality resides on the side of action. This divergence itself becomes a thermometer for overheated expectations.

The practical takeaway can be reduced to one thing: whenever making a judgment about AI, ask first, “Is this narrative, or is it measurement?” Then read the direction of the divergence. If, in the flashy dimensions, narrative exceeds measurement, suspect overheating. If, in the mundane dimensions, narrative falls short of measurement, suspect an overlooked constraint. This principle is falsifiable. If, going forward, felt experience and observation converge, stock declines become indiscriminate, and intelligence rather than electricity proves to be the true ceiling, then the principle will fail. But as things stand now, all three measurements indicate the same thing: narrative is skating above reality.
