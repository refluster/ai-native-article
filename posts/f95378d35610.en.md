## Executive Summary

According to TechCrunch on June 27, 2026, after the Trump administration prohibited exports of Anthropic’s cybersecurity model “Mythos” and its more heavily restricted version, “Fable 5,” to keep them out of the hands of non-U.S. persons, Asian AI startups have been rushing to launch alternatives. Tokyo-based Sakana AI announced “Fugu” for agents, while China’s 360 unveiled the vulnerability-discovery AI “Tulongfeng” and the defense-automation AI “Yitianzhen.” This article organizes that series of developments around the question of who fills the vacuum opened by regulation, and how, and shows that the point of contention is shifting away from “bigger models” and toward continuity of access to top-tier models.

## What Happened — Export Controls and Asia’s Rapid Response

The facts as reported are as follows.

- The Trump administration has, for now, banned non-U.S. persons from using both Anthropic’s cybersecurity-specialized model “Mythos” and “Fable 5,” its more tightly restricted version. The measure took effect about 2 weeks before the June 27 publication date of the article.
- The fact that the targets of the restrictions are models “for cybersecurity” defines the nature of this move. Capabilities directly tied to vulnerability discovery and the automation of defense sit in one of the most sensitive areas for export controls.
- As context, the report states that Anthropic’s run-rate annualized revenue exceeded $47 billion in May 2026. The restrictions are being applied to the frontier capabilities of a company that is continuing to scale commercially.
The takeaway from this is straightforward. The moment frontier capability is placed under one country’s export-control regime, users outside that country who depend on it are forced into a binary choice: build an alternative or wait. Asian players chose the former.

## Orchestration as the Answer — Sakana AI’s “Fugu”

“Fugu,” launched by Tokyo-based Sakana AI, is designed for agents and acts as a model that bundles, or orchestrates, access to other models via API. What stands out is that this product centers not on “a bigger foundation model” but on a layer that aggregates access.

The difference in perspective between the co-founders helps explain that design philosophy.

- Co-founder and CEO David Ha, formerly of Google, positioned orchestration models as “the next frontier, beyond bigger models,” and said, “Access to top models can disappear overnight.”
- Co-founder Ren Ito, who previously held executive roles at Mercari and Stability AI, argued that the U.S. priority should be to “preserve access” for its closest allies.
- A Sakana AI spokesperson described the company’s aim as “delivering frontier capability without the risk of export controls,” while also saying that “U.S. models remain important to Asia,” framing the current moment not as a permanent realignment of power but as a temporary phase.
In analytical terms, Fugu’s design is a bet on replacing “dependence on a single model” with “the ability to coordinate multiple models.” If sources of supply are becoming politically unstable, the logic seems to be that it is more rational to control the layer that can orchestrate whatever models remain available than to try to reproduce any one specific frontier model.

## Vulnerability AI as a “National Strategic Asset” — 360’s Two Products

The Chinese cybersecurity company 360 has released two tools.

- **Tulongfeng** — A model designed to automatically discover software vulnerabilities.
- **Yitianzhen** — A model that automates cyber defense and incident response.
These map directly onto the very “cybersecurity” functional domain covered by Mythos/Fable 5, the models targeted by the export restrictions. Zhou Hongyi, founder of 360, called vulnerability-discovery AI “a national strategic asset.”

That single phrase throws the contrast with Sakana AI’s framing into sharper relief. Where Sakana AI speaks in the language of continuity—“preserve access” and “a temporary phase”—360 frames the same class of capability as a national strategic asset. Faced with the same regulatory vacuum, one side responds with the language of “rebuilding bridges,” while the other responds with the language of “building and enclosing it yourself.”

## Implications — The Issue Is Shifting from “Capability” to “Continuity of Access”

With the facts laid out, this article’s conclusion is as follows.

The essence of this moment is not a capability-race story in which “Asia has caught up with the U.S.” Sakana AI’s own spokesperson explicitly said this is not a “permanent realignment” toward one camp or the other. The real issue is **continuity of access** for users. What David Ha’s phrase “can disappear overnight” points to is the user-side risk that when a frontier model is embedded as a product assumption, its supply can be cut off by a single policy decision.

From that perspective, both Fugu’s orchestration strategy and 360’s in-house tools can be read as preparations not for “which model to use,” but for “what remains when access is cut off.” Export controls are supply-side decisions, but what they create is a continuity problem on the demand side. The key question to watch is whether, if the restrictions drag on, the “temporary” language companies are using becomes a permanent reality, or whether the argument for “preserve access” is reflected in policy. That split will redraw the supply map for frontier AI in the next quarter.
