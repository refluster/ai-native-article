A McKinsey survey put AI adoption at 78%, yet more than four-fifths of companies saw no measurable EBIT impact. Around the same time, private AI-related capital investment hit an annualized $1.5 trillion, reportedly accounting for 25–33% of US GDP growth. Adoption is climbing and capital is flooding in, but nobody is saying where the returns actually land. These two facts turn out to share one mechanism: concentration in spending arrives first, and concentration in returns follows the same shape later.

## The barbell already visible at the firm level

An analysis published on X by the CEO of Varick Agents, *"AI Adoption is a Myth"* (source L2 title: "AI導入率78%でも8割が無成果——『使っているか』では測れない企業のAI格差"), sets McKinsey's "78% adoption, under 20% with measurable EBIT impact" finding next to MIT NANDA's "GenAI Divide" report, which found that 95% of integrated AI pilots produced zero return. The piece argues that enterprise AI usage splits into a "barbell": a small population of power users and a large population that barely uses the tools at all. In one $10 million-scale deployment it cites, the top 10% of users accounted for 90% of token consumption. Two engineers handed the identical ticket produced starkly different outcomes. Its conclusion: companies should stop reporting whether they "have adopted" AI and start reporting what share of the actual work has been automated.

### What "adoption rate" is actually measuring

A 78% adoption figure only tells you how many companies have touched the technology. Whether that touch converts into a result follows an entirely different distribution. The fact that the top 10% of users consume 90% of tokens is the most direct evidence that the spread of spending and the spread of returns are not the same curve. The anecdote of two engineers with the same ticket and different outcomes is the individual-level version of the same observation: access to AI is roughly equal, but the ability to extract results from it is not.

## The same concentration is now visible at the macro scale

Data published by The Kobeissi Letter on August 6, 2026 (source L2 title: "AI関連の民間設備投資が年率1.5兆ドルに到達——米GDP成長の25〜33%を占めるという主張の中身") shows AI-related private capital investment rising 25% year-over-year (+$300 billion) in Q2 2026, reaching a record annualized rate of $1.5 trillion. "Computers and peripheral equipment" led the increase, followed by telecom equipment, software, and data centers. The same source claims this direct AI investment accounts for 25–33% of recent US GDP growth.

LSEG macro analyst Erwan Jacob, in *"AI投資は『マクロの循環』になった——2026年ハイパースケーラー資本支出7,200億ドルと、インフレを先に押し上げる順序"*, goes a step further, arguing AI has stopped being a technology theme and become a macroeconomic force. The top five US hyperscalers alone are on track for roughly $720 billion in 2026 capital expenditure. Set against the $1.5 trillion figure, a striking share of that investment traces back to a handful of companies — the same concentration shape that showed up as "top 10% of users, 90% of tokens" inside a single firm, now recurring in how macro capital is allocated.

### The price effects have already started

Per Jacob, AI-related investment is already moving prices for semiconductors, electricity, and imported goods. Lining up $1.5 trillion in aggregate annual investment against $720 billion concentrated in five hyperscalers shows that a large share of the recorded spend is really the capital-allocation decision of a very small number of actors. Calling AI a "macroeconomic force" signals that AI investment is no longer the sum of many independent corporate capex decisions — it has become a stage where a handful of large capital allocators move the economy's entire price series.

## The lag between the investment stage and the returns stage

The most important part of Jacob's argument is the *sequence* of the price effect. AI's impact on prices is inflationary during the investment phase and disinflationary during the diffusion phase — not simultaneous but sequential — and that sequencing is what makes the call difficult for central banks.

Read against the firm-level findings, this "lag" looks like the macro expression of the gap between adoption and results observed inside companies. The $1.5 trillion in capital is being booked now, counted now as GDP growth. But whether it actually converts into productivity is decided on a different timeline — and, if the firm-level data is any guide, concentrated among a small number of actors when it does.

## The shared principle

On their surface, the three pieces cover separate topics: enterprise AI usage, macro capital expenditure, and inflation dynamics. Read together, the same structure repeats at different scales.

Data directly observable inside firms — the top 10% of users consuming 90% of tokens, 95% of integrated pilots returning zero — shows that spending on AI and returns from AI do not follow the same distribution. Spending spreads widely; returns concentrate narrowly.

If that structure isn't a quirk internal to individual firms, the same thing should be happening in macro capital allocation. And it is: of the $1.5 trillion annualized investment figure, roughly $720 billion traces to five hyperscalers alone — a macro-scale replica of the firm-level concentration pattern. In other words, from the directly observed fact of concentrated internal usage, we can induce that the same concentration structure governs the macro investment flow that is only visible in aggregate statistics — that is the inductive leap this piece is making.

The principle that follows: the figures currently cited as "25–33% of GDP growth" or "78% adoption" measure the volume of spending and participation, not the realization of returns. And because the distribution between spending and returns is already shown to be extremely skewed at the firm level, a large share of the $1.5 trillion booked at the macro level is likely, in the end, to convert into returns concentrated among a small number of actors. The rest will remain recorded as "growth" in the statistics while never translating into a real productivity gain — investment that functions, in practice, as a bet.

## What to expect next, and what it means

Three changes follow if this principle holds.

First, when the "disinflation of the diffusion phase" that Jacob describes actually arrives, its benefit is unlikely to spread broadly. Consistent with the firm-level barbell, that benefit should skew toward the actors already harvesting returns — the top 10% of power users, the five hyperscalers already capturing the capex — rather than diffusing evenly (medium confidence).

Second, central-bank judgment gets harder, not easier. It's not only that the price effect flips direction between the investment and diffusion phases; if the benefit itself is unevenly distributed, treating "AI-driven price movement" as a single aggregate number risks misreading the underlying reality (medium confidence).

Third, reliance on aggregate indicators like "adoption rate" or "AI's share of GDP" for investment and management decisions will get more dangerous, not less. Expect a shift toward outcome-adjacent metrics — like the "automation share" the first source proposes — at both the firm and macro level (medium-to-high confidence).

The practical takeaway for readers is one thing: when you see a number like $1.5 trillion in investment or 78% adoption, don't read it as a measure of returns. Concentration in spending and concentration in returns are the same shape; if you want to know the latter, check the distribution of the former — who is spending, how much, and on what. This forecast is falsifiable on its own terms: if the diffusion phase spreads real benefit beyond the top 10% of users and beyond the hyperscalers, the hypothesis is wrong.
