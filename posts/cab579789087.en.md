## Executive Summary

More than 100 companies — including OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon alongside Broadcom, Capital One, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, General Motors, IBM, Mastercard, Oracle, Robinhood, Shopify and Visa — issued a joint letter in late August 2026 warning there is "a limited amount of time to make our digital world much more secure" and calling for a society-wide defensive surge against AI-driven cyberattacks. The letter warns that AI-enabled attacks will become far more widespread in the coming months, urges governments to expedite "trusted access" programs that give select firms early access to more powerful models, and asks every organization to make cyber defense an immediate leadership priority. The appeal lands against a backdrop in which the U.S. cyberdefense agency CISA has lost roughly a third of its staff over the past year — exposing a gap between the urgency the letter describes and the state of the defensive infrastructure it is addressed to.

## What the Joint Letter Demands

Issued Thursday, the letter states plainly:

> "In the coming months, AI-enabled cyberattacks will become far more widespread as models around the world become increasingly capable."

It goes on to call on government and industry leaders "to bring the full weight of their technology, resources, and expertise to this effort." This is not a generic advisory — it is a warning, delivered by the very companies building and operating the models in question, that the scale of the emerging threat is outrunning current defensive capacity.

## What the Signatory List Reveals

The letter's signatories extend well beyond AI developers. OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon — companies that supply frontier AI models — are joined by Broadcom (semiconductors), Capital One, Mastercard and Visa (finance), Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, Oracle and IBM (infrastructure and security), General Motors (manufacturing), Robinhood (fintech) and Shopify (e-commerce), among more than 100 total signers. That both the supply side of AI and the sectors most exposed to AI-enabled attack signed the same letter suggests the concern is no longer confined inside the AI industry — it has spread across a wide swath of the real economy.

## The Specific Ask: "Trusted Access" Programs

Among the letter's concrete requests is faster rollout of "trusted access programs" — arrangements that give certain companies access to more powerful AI models ahead of general public release. The rationale is to let defenders test and deploy stronger models before attackers can, rather than slowing model capability itself; the goal is closing the gap between how fast offense and defense improve. The letter also asks every other organization to "make cyber defense an immediate leadership priority," extending the ask beyond a handful of large companies to institutions broadly.

## The Silence on the Other End: CISA's Reduced Staff

Neither the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) nor the White House immediately responded to a request for comment on the letter. Compounding that, the Trump administration has imposed deep cuts on CISA, and the agency's staffing has fallen by about a third over the past year. The companies are asking government to commit "the full weight" of its technology, resources and expertise at the same moment the federal agency most directly responsible for that mission has been shrinking — a structural mismatch between the letter's stated urgency and the current state of public-sector defensive capacity.

## The Five Eyes Warning — and a Threat Already Under Way

The letter follows a rare joint statement issued in June 2026 by the "Five Eyes" intelligence alliance — the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and New Zealand — warning that AI advances were poised to "fundamentally transform" cybersecurity. The letter's own framing adds that rogue actors are already increasingly deploying large language models to break into organizations worldwide. Read together, this is not a preventive appeal about a future risk: it describes a threat that has been escalating since the Five Eyes warning was issued, now voiced publicly by the model developers themselves. That the companies building the offensive-capable technology are the ones sounding the alarm on defense marks a notable point in how seriously AI-enabled attack capability is now being treated as an operational, present-tense problem rather than a speculative one.
