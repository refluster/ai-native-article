## Executive Summary

Gartner concludes that, contrary to the widely shared expectation that AI will reduce labor costs, “AI does not reduce labor costs; it reconfigures where and how they arise.” Many of the costs that emerge—including surging compensation driven by the scarcity of AI talent, rehiring costs resulting from AI-driven reductions in force, and distortions in performance-based pay models—arise outside the plan and are difficult to forecast or manage. The biggest ROI risk lies not in the cost of AI technology itself, but in the “unplanned workforce costs” required to support its adoption.

## The Premise of AI as a “Cost-Cutting Tool” Is Breaking Down

AI is widely seen as a lever for efficiency, productivity, and cost reduction, and 88% of surveyed organizations are planning to increase AI spending. As spending rises, so too does the expectation that labor costs will fall.

But Gartner’s analysis directly rejects that premise.

- AI does not reduce labor costs; it reconfigures where and how those costs appear.
- Many of the newly emerging costs are unplanned, difficult to estimate in advance, and even harder to manage.
- As a result, conventional cost-cutting approaches are no longer sufficient for optimizing labor costs in the AI era.
The implication is clear. What is required of the CHRO (Chief Human Resources Officer) is not reactive cost cutting, but a shift to an integrated, enterprise-wide workforce strategy.

## Compensation for Scarce AI Talent Is Rising Faster Than Expected

The first hidden cost lies in the competition for talent. According to Gartner, the race for AI skills is driving compensation sharply upward, with some AI roles earning 3〜4 times the compensation of the average worker.

At the same time, the useful life of that value is getting shorter.

- The lifecycle of technical skills has compressed from the traditional 8〜12 years to just 2〜5 years.
- In other words, organizations may be paying premium compensation for capabilities that lose value rapidly.
The combination of “high upfront cost” and “declining long-term usefulness” creates a structural risk to ROI. Organizations that overhire and overpay for AI talent could find themselves locked into a fixed-cost structure that exceeds the business value created.

## The Long-Term Cost Pressure Created by Workforce Reductions

The second cost is tied to reductions in force (RIF) triggered by productivity gains from AI. These cuts tend to be concentrated among younger and entry-level roles. While they may reduce costs in the short term, they can instead put pressure on finances over the long term.

- Gartner predicts that as much as 30% of jobs replaced by AI will be rehired by 2029—often at higher cost.
- The combined total of rehiring expenses, compensation premiums, and recruiting costs can exceed the original savings.
In addition, cutting entry-level roles weakens the internal talent pipeline and forces organizations to rely on costly external hiring for mid-level and senior positions. Changes in workforce composition also ripple into benefits structures, pushing up healthcare and retirement-related costs. Short-term savings can end up embedding structural cost increases.

## Distortions in Performance-Based Pay Models

The third risk lies in evaluation and compensation systems. In many companies, AI adoption is focused not on fundamentally redefining roles, but on “augmenting” existing work—increasing the speed and volume of output. But expectations and compensation models have not kept pace with that change.

- Despite the availability of AI tools, only 25% of employees said they are expected to produce more output.
This gap creates risk for performance-based compensation systems. As AI support increases employee output, legacy metrics can produce the following side effects:

- Trigger compensation payouts beyond what was anticipated
- Reward incremental output rather than strategic outcomes
- Encourage unintended behaviors
Unless these systems are redesigned, performance-based pay models will not optimize labor costs—they will drive them higher.

## The Biggest Risk Is “Unplanned Costs”

Attention tends to focus on the cost of AI technology itself, but the larger risk lies in the workforce transformation required to support it. Unplanned increases in compensation, rehiring, shifts in workforce composition, and performance-based payouts can erode the expected financial returns.

Gartner’s conclusion is a contrast in approaches. Organizations that treat AI as a pure cost-cutting tool expose themselves to the risk of rising labor costs, while organizations that treat AI as a “workforce cost optimization challenge” can unlock more sustainable value. For CHROs, this shift elevates labor cost optimization from a functional responsibility to an enterprise leadership mandate. As concrete next steps, Gartner points to assessing AI’s impact on work and labor cost structures, measuring new cost drivers such as AI talent premiums, rehiring trends, and performance payouts, and positioning HR as a core partner in the company’s overall AI strategy.
