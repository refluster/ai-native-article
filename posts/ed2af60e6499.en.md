## Executive Summary

Global AI-linked stocks sold off on September 14 after the leaders of the industry's biggest companies warned of the risks of breakneck development. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called on AI companies to slow the pace at which they advance model capabilities, in a lengthy essay posted to X, and both Elon Musk and Sam Altman said they agreed. Behind the safety debate sits a second, market-structural worry: the debt and circular financing propping up the industry's data-center buildout.

## The warning and the industry's response

- Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, in a lengthy essay shared on X on Saturday, called on AI companies to slow the rate at which they advance model capabilities.
- Amodei wrote that in six to 12 months, AI agents "could be capable of taking over the entire internet potentially causing hundreds of billions of dollars in damage."
- Both Elon Musk, who runs xAI, and Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, said they agree with Amodei.
- Altman also said OpenAI would not proceed with an IPO this year, citing safety concerns.
- Anthropic released a threat intelligence report on Thursday detailing how several actors had used its Claude AI models for activities ranging from weapons development and cyber operations to surveillance and fraud.
- Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon resigned, stating that the "people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade."
- Altman said in a separate interview that risks of human extinction posed by AI were "unacceptable."
## Markets react around the world

- On Monday, Nasdaq e-mini futures fell 1.9%, with chip stocks — which have led the AI rally — down the most.
- Nvidia fell 3%, Advanced Micro Devices slid 5.7%, and Elon Musk's SpaceX dropped 2.6%. Hyperscalers such as Meta and Amazon dropped over 1.4% each.
- European tech stocks tanked 2.5%, weighed down by chip-equipment giant ASML's 5.8% fall. Chipmaker Infineon slid 8.4%, while AI equipment maker Siemens Energy lost 7.4%.
- Across Asia, OpenAI investor SoftBank tumbled as much as 13.2%, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company slipped 1.2%, and South Korea's SK Hynix slid 6.3%.
## The financing question: who pays for the infrastructure

- The reporting notes that AI-related trades have powered much of the gains in global equities since OpenAI released ChatGPT in 2022, but that more recently, cyberattacks by rogue AI agents and public discontent with data-center construction have raised opposition to the industry's rapid buildout.
- Ipek Ozkardeskaya, senior analyst at Swissquote, said: "If the AI race slows materially, the key question becomes: who pays for all that infrastructure? The leases, debt and power commitments remain even if expected compute demand and revenue growth slow. And that could bring credit risk increasingly into the AI story."
- The reporting ties growing scrutiny of AI-related sectors to companies increasingly relying on debt and circular financing to fund their AI ambitions while raising spending forecasts, at a time when global bond yields — a proxy for borrowing costs — have climbed to multi-year highs.
- Charu Chanana, chief investment strategist at Saxo Bank in Singapore, said: "In the short term, these warnings could still weigh on AI and chip stocks. Their valuations assume both strong demand and a relentless pace of technological progress. When expectations are this high, even a possible delay can trigger profit-taking."
## Skeptics, dissenting views, and a geopolitical edge

- Some investors dismissed the warnings from Anthropic and OpenAI. Michael Burry, whose bets against the U.S. housing market before the 2008 financial crisis were chronicled in "The Big Short," said in a post on X that the warnings were "hype and puffery" and "cover for real uncontrollable slowing growth."
- Morgan Stanley's Brian Nowak forecast that AI spending will surpass $1.2 trillion by 2027, arguing that record capital-spending commitments suggest AI development is unlikely to slow.
- Deutsche Bank wrote in a note: "The key question is whether this is the first sign that the extraordinary AI investment cycle might eventually moderate. For now, that seems unlikely. The competitive race between companies and countries remains intense, and it's difficult to imagine firms voluntarily stepping back while rivals continue to push ahead."
- Despite the caution, Amodei's Anthropic is pushing ahead with a public debut expected next month; sources told Reuters the company is in talks to bring Nvidia on as an anchor investor.
- In the U.S., several lawmakers raised concerns about AI's rapid progress and called for new rules, while President Donald Trump on Sunday likened AI critics to "very negative forces" raising scenarios that will not happen, and said he wants the U.S. to remain the industry's leader.
- The U.S. and Chinese governments are expected to hold AI safety talks as part of bilateral discussions this month, according to two people briefed on the plans. China's state-backed Global Times, meanwhile, blasted Amodei's essay in an editorial as a "Cold War playbook" intended to curb the country's technological development.
