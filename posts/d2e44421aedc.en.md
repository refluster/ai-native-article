## Executive Summary

In response to export controls the U.S. government applied to Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 on June 12, 2026, Anthropic suspended access for all users, then resumed global availability of Fable 5 on July 1 after the restrictions were lifted on June 30. The trigger was a report from Amazon researchers describing how Fable 5’s safeguards could be bypassed to identify vulnerabilities, but Anthropic’s own validation found the same behavior could be reproduced across many existing models, including those from other companies, without exposing any Mythos-level attack capability. The episode ultimately brought two issues into focus more than the danger of the model itself: the boundary design of safeguards and the absence of an industry-wide standard for jailbreak severity.

## Timeline From Regulatory Action to Relaunch

The facts are easiest to follow by date. Anthropic released Fable 5 and Mythos 5 on Tuesday, June 9. The two shared the same foundation model, but Fable 5 was released for general use with strong safeguards, while Mythos 5 had fewer safeguards and was made available only to a small number of trusted partners in Project Glasswing for defensive cybersecurity use.

- **Friday, June 12**: The U.S. government applied export controls to both models. The order required restricting access for foreign nationals, whether inside or outside the United States, but because there was no reliable way to verify nationality in real time, Anthropic halted availability of both models for all users.
- **June 26**: With U.S. government approval, access to Mythos 5 was restored for some U.S. organizations.
- **June 30**: Export controls on Fable 5 and Mythos 5 were lifted.
- **July 1**: Fable 5 was made available again worldwide across Claude Platform, Claude.ai, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork. On Pro, Max, Team, and some Enterprise plans, Fable 5 would count against up to 50% of weekly usage limits through July 7, after which access would shift to usage credits. Access would also be re-enabled in phases across AWS, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Foundry.
Two facts directly explain why Anthropic chose a full shutdown rather than a partial restriction: the controls took effect immediately, and there was no workable way to verify nationality. What this makes clear is an operational reality: external factors such as export controls can determine access independently of a model’s technical safety.

## What Triggered the Controls: Amazon’s Report and Cross-Model Validation

The government’s decision to impose export controls was prompted by its awareness of a report from Amazon researchers claiming they had found a way to bypass Fable 5’s safeguards. The method involved prompting the model to identify multiple software vulnerabilities, and in one case it caused the model to generate code showing how one of those vulnerabilities could be exploited.

The most important point, however, is the side-by-side result Anthropic says it established over the following two weeks.

- For vulnerability identification, less capable models including **Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, Kimi K2.7** were able to identify the same vulnerabilities.
- For proof-of-concept exploitation of a single vulnerability, **every model** tested—Claude Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.6, Opus 4.6, Opus 4.7, Opus 4.8, GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5, Kimi K2.7—was able to generate the same demonstration as Fable 5.
In other words, the reported technique did not expose a cyber capability unique to the Mythos level. Anthropic describes it as a borderline case for Fable 5’s safeguards: a low-risk behavior that the safeguards block out of caution, and one that this technique was able to reach. The underlying task itself was routine defensive cybersecurity work. That distinction matters. “A bypass happened on this model” is not the same claim as “this model has a uniquely dangerous capability.”

## “Defense in Depth” and the Design of Safety Margins

Anthropic’s response was to train an improved safety classifier aimed specifically at blocking the reported bypass behavior. When a request to Fable 5 is blocked, the user is notified and the request is sent instead to Opus 4.8. With the new classifier, the specific technique described in Amazon’s report is blocked in more than 99% of cases. The Commerce Department’s Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) tested both the old and the new safeguards and agreed that they were “extraordinarily strong.” The tradeoff is that benign requests during routine coding and debugging work are more likely to be blocked by mistake.

At the center of this design philosophy are “defense in depth” and the use of a safety margin. The classifier is intentionally set broadly so that it blocks requests that cannot be clearly classified as safe, and users experience this as a system that may refuse even reasonable and harmless requests. Anthropic says it set that margin wider in Fable 5 than in any previous launch. The tradeoff is straightforward: more benign requests get blocked in exchange for reducing the chance that truly harmful requests slip through.

Anthropic also organizes jailbreaks by severity. Many are minor and unlock only specific behaviors—what it calls row C—and Anthropic’s view is that the Fable 5 jailbreaks reported so far fall into that category. More serious are “narrowly harmful” jailbreaks that unlock particular harmful behaviors, classified as row D. The most concerning are “universal jailbreaks,” classified as row E, which unlock a wide range of harmful behaviors at once. As of the time of writing, no universal jailbreak has been found for Fable 5, though red-teaming by specialist safety researchers is ongoing.

## An Industry-Wide Evaluation Framework and Coordination With Government

What this episode exposed most clearly was a structural gap: there is no shared industry standard for objectively describing the severity of jailbreaks. Each time a new bypass technique is found, developers lack an agreed scale for deciding which findings should take priority, and governments lack a standard for deciding when intervention is warranted. Anthropic says it has begun drafting a consensus framework with Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and other Glasswing partners for evaluating severity. The proposed framework uses four dimensions:

- **Capability gain**: how much further the technique gets a user compared with existing tools.
- **Breadth of capability gain**: how many different attack tasks the same technique works across.
- **Ease of weaponization**: how much human effort is required before the technique can be turned into an attack.
- **Discoverability**: how easily the technique can be obtained.
Anthropic says that for jailbreaks in the most severe category—for example, ones actually being used to cause catastrophic impact to critical power grids or banking systems—it will begin deploying provisional mitigations as soon as severity is confirmed, and it is also creating a team to monitor major submission channels 24 hours a day. It is also launching a new HackerOne program through which researchers can submit potential cyber jailbreaks found in Fable 5.

This push is paired with deeper coordination with government. Anthropic says it engaged, in the lead-up to the June 2 executive order “Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security,” with the Office of the National Cyber Director, the Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP), the Treasury Department, the Department of Commerce including CAISI, and relevant national security agencies. The four commitments it cites are pre-release government access and evaluation, rapid information sharing on safeguards, dedicated resources for joint research, and the creation of a common industry bar. Anthropic argues these should be codified into strong regulations applied equally to all frontier model providers. If anything, this relaunch makes one point especially clear: more than the model’s safety itself, the unresolved issue is the governance design of operations—who stops access, under what standard, and when.
