Bill Gates argues that the AI transition could be "one of the most turbulent periods in human history" and proposes "Human Reserved" — keeping some jobs for people. An Engineering Manager at LayerX built a system that lets AI help him oversee a team of 13 to 20 people, keeping its output as hypotheses to confirm with the person concerned. McDonald's has been hit with a proposed nationwide class action alleging that an AI pricing system aligned prices across company-owned and franchised stores. On the surface these are three unrelated stories: policy, management practice, antitrust litigation. In fact the same principle explains all three. When AI gathers the inputs to judgment in one place, the independence of the decisions quietly erodes even if the final call stays with a human. Conversely, protecting human judgment takes more than retaining decision rights; it requires designing the conditions under which independence can exist.

## What the three cases show

### McDonald's: many deciders, one recommendation

According to "McDonald's sued: is an AI pricing tool 'price coordination among franchisees'?", McDonald's was sued in a federal court in Chicago in a proposed nationwide class action alleging that it coordinated prices across company-owned and franchised stores through an AI pricing system, in violation of antitrust law. The dispute is whether a central price recommendation, built on non-public data, effectively aligns what should be independent pricing decisions. The company is contesting the claim in full, and at this stage it is only an allegation.

Note what the dispute is not about: who made the final pricing call. The form — each store sets its own price — is presumably not in question. What is in question is whether decisions remain independent when their input comes from one central system. The case rests on the premise that formal decision rights and substantive independence are different properties.

### LayerX: observation widens, judgment stays human

In "Output more than doubled in six months with one manager: the design and limits of LayerX's self-built 'AI companion' management", the Engineering Manager (mootoh) at LayerX's Ai Workforce division chose not to add managers or split the team into sub-teams, and instead built a system to oversee 13 to 20 people together with AI. What he delegates to AI is not "automation of judgment" but "extension of the range of observation" and prompts for conversation; outputs are kept as hypotheses to confirm with the person. After six months, the stated problem is that the resolution is "still coarse."

This design shows the McDonald's structure from the opposite side. If observation is concentrated in a single AI, the world one manager sees becomes the world that AI shows him. By keeping outputs as "hypotheses to confirm," mootoh appears to have built in, at the design level, the recognition that aggregated observation can erode independent judgment. The "coarse resolution" problem also indicates that the system is not merely an efficiency device but a filter on what becomes visible.

### Gates: decide in advance what to keep

In "Bill Gates: 'The choices on AI are decisive now' — three transition risks and three institutional proposals including 'Human Reserved'", Gates writes on Gates Notes that the risks are permanent job loss, expanded misuse, and effects on children's development and relationships. His remedies are building new institutions, "Human Reserved" (keeping some jobs for humans), and taxing AI tokens and robots. He also says the benefits in healthcare, agriculture and public services are "very large," and asks for concern and optimism to be held together.

"Human Reserved" defines the human domain as an institutional matter, not a question of capability. The question is not whether AI can do the work but what is decided in advance to remain human. It lifts to the level of society what the LayerX case does as a personal discipline — keeping AI output at the level of hypothesis.

## The shared principle: why, and so what

Side by side, the cases share a structure. First, AI aggregates inputs: a central price recommendation built on non-public data at McDonald's, whole-team observation at LayerX, a view across the whole societal transition in Gates's argument. Second, decision rights formally remain with humans: each store's price, the EM's judgment, the institution designer's choice. Third, between those two, the independence of the decision is what is at stake.

These facts suggest the following principle. **The quality of judgment in the AI era is determined not by who decides but by how independent the inputs to the decision are.** Leaving decision rights with humans is necessary but not sufficient. When inputs converge on one source, decisions correlate no matter how many humans make them. The McDonald's suit shows that this correlation can become a legal issue; the LayerX case shows it can be restrained by design; Gates's proposal shows that the restraint can be fixed as an institution.

Why does this happen? Aggregation by AI sharply lowers the cost of judgment. When the cost falls, people follow the recommendation. Following is rational, and no individual decider is at fault. But rational compliance accumulates, and decisions that should have been independent line up. It is a structural outcome: correct individually, yet looking like coordination in aggregate. That the LayerX EM avoided "automating judgment" and framed outputs as hypotheses to confirm can be read as a device for deliberately breaking this chain of compliance.

The principle is falsifiable. If decisions that follow a central recommendation vary as much as decisions made independently, the principle is wrong. If institutions like "Human Reserved" do not increase the diversity of decisions, its prescription does not work. Note too that the evidence here is limited to three cases, and the McDonald's matter is a proposed claim, not a finding. The principle is a hypothesis, not settled doctrine.

## Predictions and implications

Several changes follow from this principle.

**First, independence is likely to become an object of audit.** As the McDonald's suit shows, the point of contention is moving from where decision rights sit to where inputs come from. Whether the outcome favours the company's position is unknown today. What remains is that the question — does a central recommendation align independent decisions? — has been brought into an antitrust court. The same question could be asked well beyond pricing, wherever a central recommendation is distributed to many deciders, such as hiring, evaluation and credit. I rate the likelihood as moderate; the ground is a single case that has reached court, and whether it becomes a precedent is unclear.

**Second, "outputs as hypotheses to confirm" may become a design standard.** At LayerX the practice of keeping AI output at the level of hypothesis ran for six months while output more than doubled. In at least this one case, keeping judgment with humans coexists with productivity. But it is one case, and the "coarse resolution" problem remains. When accuracy improves, will people keep treating a hypothesis as a hypothesis? Higher accuracy strengthens the temptation to comply. That will be the real test.

**Third, arguments that define the human domain by institution rather than capability will increase.** Gates's "Human Reserved" is one form. Its strength is that the domain is not eroded as AI capability grows. Its weakness is that the line itself is political and contestable. Which work to keep is decided not by technology but by society.

Three implications for practitioners. One: when evaluating AI adoption, ask not only "is the final decision human?" but "is the input that human sees from the same source as every other decider's?" Two: build AI output into the design as a hypothesis to confirm, not as a decision. Three: declare in advance, separately from the capability debate, which judgments stay human.

The three cases differ in domain and scale. They agree, though, that between the recommendation AI gathers at one point and the decisions each person makes lies an invisible asset called independence. That asset depletes through the course of efficiency, without anyone's fault. Protecting it means not only leaving decision rights with humans but making the diversity of inputs an object of design.
