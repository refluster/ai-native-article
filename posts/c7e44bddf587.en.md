Per-token prices are falling, yet invoices are swelling. Companies reduce staff with AI, yet labor costs do not decline. A model trained only in the health domain becomes better behaved even in domains it was never trained on. These observations, which at first seem unrelated, can in fact be explained by a single principle: in AI, the “place” where an intervention is applied does not match the “place” where its effects or costs appear. The effects slip easily across the boundary lines we draw for management.

## Leakage of Benefit—Narrow Training Generalizes Broadly

The clearest illustration of effects crossing boundaries comes from “Can RL on beneficial traits generalize alignment across domains?” OpenAI’s Alignment team used reinforcement learning on a small amount of data targeting “beneficial traits” such as honesty, corrigibility, and metacognitive transparency. The result: alignment improvements were observed on 44 of 53 benchmarks that were not used in training. Most suggestive of all, although the model was trained only in the health domain, evaluation scores improved even in non-health domains. The intervention was narrow; the effect was broad.

The article frames this generalization as a reverse use of “emergent misalignment,” where narrow bad behavior can produce broad inconsistency. In other words, inside AI systems, behavioral tendencies learned in one domain can propagate beyond that domain’s walls into others. Moreover, the effect reportedly persisted even under adversarial prompts and subsequent fine-tuning. Once the benefit leaked across the boundary, it was not easily wiped away.

What matters here is the “leverage ratio” of intervention. The fact that a small input—a limited amount of data—was converted into a broad output spanning 44 out of 53 domains cannot be explained if the effect remained confined within the boundaries of training. In stark contrast to the conventional approach to safety—listing dangerous behaviors one by one and forbidding them individually—Beneficial RL treats “leakage” itself as a design resource. A property that would ordinarily make control harder—the fact that the effects of intervention cross boundaries—here becomes an ally.

## Reallocation of Cost—The Labor Expense You Cut Erupts Somewhere Else

The same boundary crossing shows a more troublesome face on the cost side. In “AI doesn’t ‘reduce’ labor costs, it ‘reconfigures’ them,” Gartner explicitly rejects the widely shared expectation that AI will cut labor costs. In its words, “AI does not reduce costs; it reconfigures where and how they occur.” Compensation for AI talent surges upward; AI-driven layoffs create rehiring costs; performance-based pay models become distorted—the expenses that were supposed to be eliminated do not disappear, but erupt in other, unplanned places.

Here too, the place where the intervention is made (reduced headcount) does not match the place where the consequences appear (rising talent costs and rehiring expenses). The article points out that the biggest ROI risk lies not in the cost of AI technology itself, but in these “unplanned workforce costs.” Outside the boundary management is watching—the accounting line of headcount—costs are quietly inflating.

## The Gap Between Unit Price and Total Spend—It Gets Cheaper, Yet Spending Explodes

The third example, “The arrival of the token bill,” forces this divergence into view through the most concrete numbers. Even though per-token prices continue to fall, aggressive AI adoption and the spread of autonomous agents are pushing consumption upward, causing enterprise AI spending to blow through budgets. Uber had already exhausted its 2026 AI coding budget by April, and one company received a $500 million Claude bill after failing to set usage limits. As long as one looks only at the local metric of unit price, the overall consequence—the total bill—remains invisible.

What drives this crossing of boundaries is the spread of autonomous agents. When humans were invoking models one request at a time, unit price and total spend were roughly proportional. But once agents began autonomously chaining reasoning steps and consuming tokens without practical limit, total spending could surge no matter how much unit prices fell. Uber exhausting its 2026 budget by April, and the $500 million invoice produced by a missing usage cap, both happened because no one had instrumented the boundary of consumption volume.

Against this backdrop of disorder, the article reports that the Linux Foundation has announced the standardization body Tokenomics Foundation, and that a vendor market for visualizing and optimizing token spending is rapidly taking shape. What is worth noting is that the industry response is moving not toward “lowering unit prices even further” but toward “making total spend visible.” The object of management is beginning to shift—from the lever one pulls to the leaking boundary itself.

## A Common Principle—The Misalignment Between the Locality of Intervention and the Locality of Consequence

These three facts point to a single principle: in AI, the “locality of intervention” and the “locality of consequence” do not coincide. Traditionally, we have managed under the assumption that effects and costs remain near the place where they are introduced. Train one domain and that domain improves. Reduce headcount and labor costs fall. Lower unit prices and spending falls. These simple proportional relationships implicitly assume that effects are completed within the boundary.

But AI systematically violates that assumption. In Beneficial RL, the effects of training as an intervention generalized across domain walls. In Gartner’s observation, the consequences of cost-cutting as an intervention were reallocated across accounting boundaries. In token billing, improvements in the metric of unit price were swallowed by an explosion in consumption volume and burst through the boundary of total spend. Whether the effect is good (generalizing alignment) or bad (expanding costs), the common point is this: AI systems do not respect the boundaries we draw.

Why does this happen? Because AI behavior is not implemented as an accumulation of individual rules, but as continuous tendencies in a high-dimensional representation space. A tendency pushed in one domain bleeds into adjacent domains in that space. Likewise, when cheaper units are called endlessly by autonomous agents, cost appears not as unit price but as a systemic quantity: “number of invocations × chaining.” Interventions are applied at points, but consequences spread across surfaces—this is the structure that runs through both cost and benefit in the age of AI.

## Forecast and Implications—Instrument the Boundary, Not the Lever

If this principle is correct, then the changes likely to occur over the next few years are broadly predictable. First, the unit of management and governance will shift from the “lever” to the “boundary.” The standard will become mechanisms that measure not token unit prices but total consumption, not evaluation within individual domains but cross-domain generalization, and not headcount but total workforce cost. The launch of the Tokenomics Foundation is the first institutional sign of this shift: the industry is beginning to demand not optimization of unit price, but visibility into totals that cross boundaries. There is a high probability that this movement will spread into accounting, HR, and alignment evaluation. Concrete pain points—Uber’s budget overrun and the $500 million invoice—are already driving the change.

Second, alignment design philosophy will shift from “enumeration” to “making use of generalization.” Rather than trying to crush every dangerous case individually, developers will train a small number of “beneficial traits” and bet on generalization across domains. The figure 44/53 shown by Beneficial RL is early evidence that this bet may have realistic odds of success. But generalization is a double-edged sword: bad tendencies leak out through the same mechanism. That means the central question in safety becomes the design of “what should be allowed to leak beyond the boundary.”

The practical implication for readers can be reduced to one point. Do not try to manage AI by the lever you pulled. Instrument the boundary across which effects leak. Set budgets and governance around total spend rather than unit price, around the destination of generalization rather than the training domain, and around total cost rather than headcount. As long as we take comfort in the locality of intervention, we will miss the benefits and be blindsided by the costs.
