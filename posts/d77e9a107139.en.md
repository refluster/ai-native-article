## Executive Summary

Meta has reassigned about 6,500 engineers and product managers into its three-month-old “Applied AI” unit, with no real option to refuse, to generate puzzles and coding problems for training AI models. Inside the company, backlash has erupted: an employee-only livestream was hijacked by insults, and some of the very employees involved have taken to calling themselves “draftees.” Even after acquiring Scale AI for 14.3 billion dollars, Meta still cannot avoid depending on human-generated training data—and this episode vividly reveals where the real bottleneck in current AI development lies.

## What happened—The “Applied AI” unit that assembled 6,500 people in 3 months

According to Wired’s reporting (summarized by TechCrunch on June 12, 2026), Meta’s “Applied AI” team is on the verge of revolt. The trigger was an internal presentation livestreamed only to employees that was hijacked by a person shouting abuse.

- That person demanded that attendees tell a senior AI executive at Meta, “you are a piece of sh*t.” Wired reported that one of the presenters covered their face with both hands.
- Wired says the incident reflects simmering anger inside the unit, which was formed just 3 months ago and consists of about 6,500 engineers and product managers.
- Many employees were informed of the transfer through a single “surprise email.” One employee, who described themself as a “draftee,” later called the process “quite random” on Reddit.
According to an internal announcement reviewed by Business Insider, the reason employees were mobilized was that Meta’s AI models still lacked knowledge that surpassed humans on technical tasks such as coding. The announcement said: “Agents need to be trained on real examples to understand how people actually complete everyday tasks using computers.”

What this makes clear is that even with state-of-the-art generative AI, “human work procedures themselves”—the kind worth training on—remain a scarce resource. What Meta is trying to produce internally is not a new model architecture, but the training data itself.

## Why employees instead of outsourcing?—The “data wall” that remains even after the Scale AI acquisition

In leaked audio from an internal meeting, CEO Zuckerberg explained why Meta “drafted” its own employees rather than relying on outside contractors.

- Zuckerberg said that Alexandr Wang—who sold the data-labeling startup Scale AI to Meta for 14.3 billion dollars and then became chief AI officer, leading Meta Superintelligence Labs—knows the world of data labeling inside and out.
- He then stated bluntly that the average Meta employee has “significantly higher” intelligence than third-party contractors, and concluded that employees were therefore better suited to the task.
The sheer size of the 14.3 billion dollar acquisition and the decision, even after that, to put highly skilled internal talent to work generating training data may look contradictory, but in fact they are consistent. Meta’s bet is that the “cheap, large-scale labeling” represented by Scale AI is not enough to build models that outperform humans on advanced tasks like coding. The focus has shifted away from the “quantity” of data and toward its “quality”—that is, turning the work of highly skilled labor into training data. What matters here is that the choice to use in-house engineers rather than contractors is being framed not as a cost issue, but as a capability issue.

## The distortions created by the design of the “draft”

The problem was not the logic behind the mobilization, but how it was implemented. Employees’ descriptions of their working conditions reveal deep distortions in the organization’s design.

- Employees say they were folded into the unit under what was effectively a “join or leave” arrangement, with no meaningful alternative. Their assigned work was to generate puzzles and coding problems for training AI models.
- One employee told Wired, “It’s literally the gulag.” Another said that “most human beings would find this work soul-crushing.”
- At first, the unit was structured so that as many as 50 employees reported to a single manager.
- The discontent is not confined to Applied AI. Company-wide, more than 1,600 employees have reportedly signed a protest against a program that monitors clicks and keystrokes to collect AI training data.
Management is aware of the mood. Meta Chief Product Officer Chris Cox was forced to address this “brutal” environment in a call with employees. Zuckerberg himself, in a Friday internal memo, acknowledged that the changes had “caused distress” and said the company intended to correct its mistakes, while adding: “Meta’s north star is to be the best place in the world for the most talented people to have impact.”

The Applied AI team is led by Maher Saba, a 12-year Meta veteran who served as a vice president in Reality Labs—the division that burned through 83 billion dollars on the metaverse before shifting its center of gravity to AI. The new organization sits under Meta CTO Andrew Bosworth.

## What we can infer from this

This should not be dismissed as a labor dispute at a single company. It has three broader implications.

First, Meta’s actions confirm that the limiting factor in AI development has shifted from “compute” to “training data.” Even after a 14.3 billion dollar acquisition, building models for advanced tasks still requires human work procedures—and Meta is trying to source those procedures in-house from its own highest-skilled talent.

Second, this internalization comes with costs that are hard to see on the balance sheet. A management ratio of 50 to 1, compulsory reassignment with no real choice, and a protest petition signed by more than 1,600 employees against a surveillance program all point to the same reality: Meta may have obtained data cheaply, but it is paying in another currency—organizational trust. The very fact that Zuckerberg had to issue a memo acknowledging “distress” and “mistakes” is evidence that the bill has already arrived.

Third, this also serves as primary source material for observers. Every organization pushing AI will eventually have to confront the question: who creates training data, and under what conditions? Meta’s case is now demonstrating, in real time, what happens when that question is deferred—inside an organization led by a veteran of the same division that burned through 83 billion dollars on the metaverse.
