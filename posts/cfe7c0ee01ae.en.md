When the cost of execution approaches zero, what becomes scarce is the quality of the question that determines what to build. Not the speed of writing code, nor model performance.

There are three facts that seem unrelated at first glance. Programmers are using Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex in “vibe coding,” shifting the center of development from handwritten code to interactive generation through dialogue. Elon Musk has repeatedly argued at SpaceX and Tesla for an order of operations that begins with “question every requirement” and “delete” before optimization. And Satya Nadella has argued, “build an ecosystem, not a frontier model.” In reality, all three are explained by the same principle: as the ability to build gets cheaper, value escapes upstream to the question of what to build and why.

## When Generation Gets Cheap, What Becomes Scarce Is “Intent”

The L2 explainer on “vibe coding” reports a shift in the center of gravity of development. Programmers are using tools such as Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex, moving the core of their work away from writing code by hand and toward “expressing intent in natural language, then iterating through generation and revision.” The core of the WSJ observation was that the very assumptions of the software development lifecycle (SDLC) are being rewired.

What has become cheaper here is “implementation.” So what remains expensive? The ability to judge whether generated code is correct, and to articulate in words what should be generated in the first place—in other words, clarity of intent. The era when typing speed was a competitive advantage is ending; vague instructions now simply produce large volumes of vague code. The closer generation gets to free, the more the bottleneck shifts from “building quickly” to “asking correctly.” Not how much you move your hands, but the words that decide what gets moved are becoming the new measure of developer ability.

## Why “Delete” Comes Before Optimization

The L2 explainer on Elon Musk’s “algorithm” points out that the value lies less in the five-step procedure itself than in the order. Most people begin with optimization, acceleration, and automation. Musk instead puts “question every requirement” and “delete” first. The essence of it is condensed in his own words: “The most common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not exist.”

This warning becomes sharper precisely in a context where implementation has become cheap. In an era when automation was expensive, even if you built something unnecessary, work started slowly enough that there was time to realize midway, “Do we really need this?” But when generation becomes instantaneous and inexpensive, “things that should not exist” can be mass-produced in an instant—and because they work, the mistake is harder to see. The cheaper and faster it becomes to build, the more valuable the judgment “do not build” becomes. Musk placed optimization and automation later in the sequence because they are steps that presuppose something whose existence has already been affirmed. In the age of generative AI, his order is the discipline most likely to be betrayed—and at the same time, the one that works best.

## What You Should Own Is Not the “Model” but the “Learning Loop”

What Nadella’s L2 explainer conveys is the argument of the article published on June 14, 2026, “A frontier without an ecosystem is not stable.” Companies should build both human capital—human knowledge, judgment, and pattern recognition—and token capital—the AI capability the company itself owns—and make the “learning loop” that compounds the two into a new form of IP. Nadella argues that a future in which value concentrates in a small number of models is not politically or economically sustainable, and that the priority should be not “frontier models” but a “frontier ecosystem.”

Here again, we see a contrast between the layer that is being commoditized—the frontier model, or implementation and generation capability—and the layer that remains scarce: the learning loop that meshes human capital and token capital. Abstracted one level further, Nadella’s claim becomes this: “Generation capability itself will be replicated and become cheap. Therefore, what you should own is not generation capability, but the loop that connects it to judgment and compounds it over time.” Models can be bought. But the circuitry for deciding what to learn in your own context, what to discard, and how to improve cannot be bought. Structurally, this is continuous with Musk’s “delete first” and with vibe coding’s “intent is scarce.”

## The Shared Principle: The Lower Execution Costs Fall, the Further Scarcity Moves Upstream

These three facts are playing out at different scales: the development floor, managerial discipline, and industrial structure. But the underlying structure being observed is the same. First, the cost of “execution”—generation, implementation, automation—falls sharply. Second, execution itself ceases to be a differentiator. Third, scarcity and competitive advantage migrate to the layer just upstream of execution: judgments about what to build, what not to build, and which loop to own.

Taken together, these facts suggest a single law: **the lower execution costs fall, the more value escapes upstream into judgment**. In vibe coding, value shifts from typing to intent. In Musk’s ordering, from optimization to “questioning and deletion.” In Nadella’s view, from models to learning loops. The scales differ, but the movement is isomorphic in each case: the scarce resource becomes the layer in front of the layer that has become cheap—the layer that formulates the question.

What matters is that this shift is not merely a comforting story about “the work left for humans.” When execution becomes cheap and fast, the consequences of a bad question also become cheap and fast—and massively instantiated. So upstream judgment is not only where value flees; it is also where the greatest risk concentrates. Being able to optimize, at unprecedented speed, something that should not exist—this is the shared danger at the heart of all three cases. In an era when execution was the rate-limiting step, errors could still be stopped midway. In a world where execution is nearly free, the only remaining gate is the question asked before work begins.

## Forecast and Implications: Move to the Side That Measures the Quality of Questions

If this principle is correct, the following changes are highly likely over the next 2–3 years. First, the evaluation axis for organizations and products will shift from “how fast can you build?” to “what can you decide not to build?” Volume of generation will no longer be proof of achievement; deleted requirements and features never started will become indicators of capability. Second, the scarcity of individual skill will concentrate in the “taste” required to judge and reject outputs, and in the ability to translate ambiguous situations into testable questions. Third, a company’s moat will be measured not by possession of the latest model, but by whether it has what Nadella describes as a learning loop—a mechanism in which the company’s judgment and AI capability mesh and improve at compound rates.

There is, however, a condition attached to this forecast. If declining execution costs level off and the quality of generation hits a ceiling, execution capability could once again return as a differentiator. But at least as long as generative AI continues to improve, the side to bet on is the upstream one. This is a falsifiable claim, and it stands in direct opposition to the counter-hypothesis that “generation volume itself is competitiveness.”

The practical implication for readers is simple. The next time something feels like it can be “built quickly,” pause once before you start and follow Musk’s order: ask, “Should this really exist?”—and if possible, delete it. Then reassess your own value, or your team’s value, not by how much was built, but by the sharpness of the questions posed and the quality of the options rejected. In a world where implementation has become cheap for everyone, what is scarce is not the hand that builds quickly, but the question that determines what should be built.
