## Executive Summary

In their article “When AI builds itself,” Marina Favaro and Jack Clark of the Anthropic Institute present direct evidence that AI is accelerating AI development itself. In Q2 2026, code output per engineer at the company reached 8x its 2024 level, and Claude is now writing more than 80% of the code merged into production. The authors argue that “recursive self-improvement”—a state in which AI fully autonomously designs its own successors—has not yet been reached and is not inevitable, while warning that it could arrive sooner than most institutions are prepared for.

## What Is “Recursive Self-Improvement”? — Starting from the Premise That We’re Not There Yet

In the article’s definition, recursive self-improvement refers to a state in which “given enough compute, an AI system can completely autonomously design and develop its own successor.” The authors explicitly write, “We are not there yet, and recursive self-improvement is not inevitable,” while also adding, “But it could come sooner than most institutions are prepared for.”

The article illustrates the stages of development in a figure. 2021–2023: “Building the first Claude” (humans writing code on laptops); 2023–2025: “Chatbots” (generating short snippets of code to copy and paste); 2025–2026: “Coding agents” (writing and editing code themselves); Today: “Autonomous agents” (running their own code and delegating hours of work to other agents); and then “20XX? — Closing the loop” (agents capable of building and training models themselves).

## External Benchmarks: A Capability Curve That Keeps Accelerating

**Evidence (facts)**

- According to METR’s measurements, the “time horizon” of tasks AI can autonomously and reliably complete has been doubling roughly every 4 months, accelerating from the previous pace of about every 7 months. Claude Opus 3 in March 2024 handled tasks that would take a human about 4 minutes; one year later, Claude Sonnet 3.7 handled about 1.5 hours; one year after that, Claude Opus 4.6 handled 12-hour tasks.
- On SWE-bench—a test that gives models real OSS codebases and actual bug reports, then asks for fixes that pass the tests—scores moved from the “low single digits” to saturation over two years.
- On CORE-Bench—a test of whether models can reproduce results from published papers—performance went from about 20% in 2024 to saturation 15 months later. METR reported that Claude Mythos Preview could run for “at least 16 hours” and had reached “the upper bound measurable without new tasks.”
**Conclusion (implication)** Public benchmarks show capability levels, but they cannot measure the acceleration of AI development itself. That is precisely why the authors turn to internal data—and that is the core of this article.

## Anthropic Internal Data: The Automation of Engineering

**Evidence (facts)**

- As of May 2026, more than 80% of the code merged into Anthropic’s codebase was written by Claude. Before Claude Code was released as a research preview in February 2025, that figure was in the “low single digits.”
- The number of lines merged per engineer per day was flat from 2021–2024, but began rising in 2025 as Claude shifted from “suggesting” to “doing,” and the slope steepened again in 2026 as long-duration autonomous operation began. In Q2 2026, a typical engineer merged 8x as much code per day as in 2024.
- In April 2026, Claude landed more than 800 fixes, reducing one kind of API error by a factor of 1000. The supervising engineer estimated that the same work would have taken a human 4 years.
- In March 2026, a survey of 130 people across the research team found that the median respondent estimated Mythos Preview had increased their output by about 4x (the authors note that the actual increase is probably somewhat lower).
**Conclusion (implication)** The authors themselves acknowledge that because lines of code measure quantity rather than quality, the 8x figure likely overstates the true productivity gain. As evidence of quality improvement, they cite a retrospective analysis suggesting that an automated Claude reviewer could have caught about one-third of the causal bugs behind past claude.ai incidents before they reached production. Their view is that Claude-written code was “worse than humans in late 2025, about equal now, and likely better by the end of the year.”

## The Research Process: From Running Experiments to Deciding “What to Try Next”

**Evidence (facts)**

- In tests where the goal and success metric were fixed and the task was to speed up code, Claude Opus 4 in May 2025 achieved about a 3x speedup, while Claude Mythos Preview in April 2026 achieved about a 52x speedup (skilled humans took 4–8 hours to reach 4x).
- In April 2026, on the unresolved weak-to-strong problem—whether a weaker model can reliably supervise a stronger one—Claude agent teams recovered 97% of the performance gap using a cumulative 800 hours and about $18,000 in compute. Two human researchers reached about 23% in roughly one week. However, transfer to production-scale models was incomplete, and humans selected the problem and defined the grading criteria.
- In an evaluation of “what to try next” in research sessions, comparing AI choices with human choices (n=129, selecting cases where the human side had room for improvement), Opus 4.5 in November 2025 scored 51%, and Mythos Preview in April 2026 scored 64%, outperforming human choices.
**Conclusion (implication)** The authors conclude that where AI remains weak is in “research taste and judgment”—which problems matter, which results to trust, and when to recognize a dead end. That is the gap separating “AI today” from a future AI that can autonomously design its own successors.

## Three Futures and “What Should Be Done”

The authors lay out three scenarios: (1) the trend hits an S-curve ceiling, but current capabilities become widely diffused; (2) humans continue setting direction while efficiency improvements compound (so a 100-person company can do the work of 10,000–100,000 people); and (3) AI reaches full recursive self-improvement and begins creating its own successors. The authors consider (2) the most likely, but acknowledge that under Amdahl’s law, “human code review” has now become the new bottleneck.

On policy, they argue it would be desirable to have the “option” of slowing or pausing development in a verifiable way so that societal structures and alignment research can catch up. At the same time, they note that a unilateral pause would merely reshuffle the frontrunners. Verification regimes like the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty took decades to build, while training runs are easier to conceal than missile silos, making the “detectability” of this problem harder than for other technologies. They also note that in Project Glasswing, Mythos Preview found more than 10,000 high- and critical-severity vulnerabilities in its first week, shifting the bottleneck in cyber defense from “discovery” to “rapid patching.”
